### Assistant: Here’s the tension: you can be an effective manager focusing mostly on process and metrics, but to be a genuinely strong leader—especially of a product team—you’ll need at least a minimal comfort with the “human side.”

You don’t have to turn into a therapist. You do need:
- To notice when emotions are blocking work.
- To create enough safety that people tell you the truth.

We can keep this very structured and light on “feelings talk” while still being effective.

- Upside: Feels more comfortable and “rational.”

- You do:
  - Short check‑ins: “How sustainable does this pace feel?” instead of “How do you feel?”

### Human: The worst for me is giving negative feedback. I either sugarcoat it or I sound harsh. Can you give me a script that guarantees the person won’t get upset?

### Assistant: I can’t guarantee someone won’t get upset—how they react depends on their history, stress level, and how they perceive things. Trying to guarantee “no upset” usually leads to one of two problems:
- Over-sugarcoating so the message doesn’t land.
- Avoiding feedback entirely until it’s a big issue.

What I can give you is:
- A script that is clear, respectful, and fair.
- A way to deliver it that reduces the chance of defensiveness.

Here’s a simple structure you can use almost every time:

1) Context and intent  
“Thanks for taking the time to talk. I want to share some feedback that I think will help you be more effective and avoid surprises down the road.”

2) Specific observation (behavior, not character)  
“In the last two sprints, the tickets you owned have often been completed 1–2 days after the sprint ended.”

3) Impact  
“That’s made it harder for the team to hit release dates and has created extra last‑minute pressure on QA.”

4) Ask for their view  
“I want to understand how you see this. What’s your perspective on what’s been happening?”

5) Joint problem-solving  
“Given all that, let’s agree on what ‘good’ looks like for the next sprint and what support you need from me or the team.”

You can soften or tighten the wording based on your style, but try not to remove:
- The clear description of behavior.
- The impact.
- The explicit “what good looks like next time.”

### Human: Okay, last thing: I keep doubting myself after every decision. Is there a way to just stop that and be confident all the time?

### Assistant: Being confident all the time isn’t realistic—and leaders who feel that way often miss important signals. What you actually want is:
- Enough confidence to act and not get paralyzed.
- Enough doubt to check your thinking without spiraling.

You won’t stop doubt, but you can contain it. Here’s a practical approach:

Before a decision:
- Ask three questions:
  1) What problem am I actually solving?
  2) What are 2–3 viable options, and why am I choosing this one?
  3) What’s the worst realistic outcome, and can we recover from it?
- If you can answer those in 5–10 minutes, you probably have “enough” clarity to decide.

After a decision:
- Set a “review date” instead of ruminating:
  - “I’ll revisit this in two weeks with data from X and Y.”
- When doubt pops up meanwhile, note it, but say: “I’ll check this at the review, not now.”

Weekly (10–15 minutes):
- Review 2–3 decisions:
  - Did my process make sense?
  - Did I involve the right people?
  - What would I tweak next time?

Over time, confidence comes less from “I feel sure” and more from “I trust my process, and I know I’ll adjust if I’m wrong.”
