Modern machine learning systems increasingly operate in environments where inputs are noisy, variable, and subject to many superficial changes. In such settings, robust performance depends crucially on the quality of the learned representations. A central principle is that good representations should be invariant to transformations that do not change the underlying semantics of the data, while remaining sensitive to variations that do matter for the task. For example, an image classifier should ignore random crops or small color shifts that preserve the object identity, yet distinguish between objects that belong to different categories. Similarly, a text classifier should treat semantically equivalent paraphrases as similar, while still capturing important differences in sentiment or intent.

Over the past several years, a large body of work has embraced this principle by explicitly enforcing invariance to data augmentations or perturbations during representation learning. In vision, common practice is to apply a family of label-preserving transformations, such as random crops, flips, color jitter, or blur, and encourage the model to produce similar representations for the original and augmented images. In natural language processing, analogous strategies rely on paraphrasing, token masking, back-translation, or synonym substitution to produce alternative views of the same underlying content. These techniques have led to impressive gains in downstream performance and have become standard components of many state-of-the-art systems.

However, the success of augmentation-based methods masks an important limitation: they typically provide only coarse, indirect control over the balance between invariance and sensitivity in the resulting representations. In most existing approaches, the set of augmentations and the strength of the invariance objective are chosen heuristically, with little guidance about how these choices affect robustness and in-distribution accuracy. Once a particular configuration is fixed, the model is encouraged to be as invariant as possible to the selected transformations. While this can be beneficial, it also raises the risk that the model discards useful information that is correlated with the label or with generalization to new conditions.

This problem is particularly acute when the semantic effect of a transformation is context dependent. A small rotation might be harmless for images of natural objects but harmful for images where orientation is label defining, such as digits or street signs. In text, a paraphrase that preserves the main topic might subtly shift sentiment or formality in ways that matter for the task. A representation learning method that aggressively pushes augmented examples together, without any mechanism to preserve diversity, may inadvertently collapse these distinctions. As a result, it can be difficult to reason about, let alone control, the trade-off between robustness to benign variations and sensitivity to meaningful differences.

There is therefore a need for representation learning frameworks that make this trade-off explicit and tunable. Rather than embedding all augmentation choices and invariance strength into a fixed objective, we would like a method that exposes a clear control knob: as we adjust this knob, we should see predictable changes in robustness, accuracy, and representation structure. Such a framework would not only be practically useful for model selection, but also scientifically informative, enabling systematic studies of how invariance and sensitivity interact across different domains, architectures, and tasks.

In this work, we take a step toward this goal by introducing a simple, dual-objective framework for controlling the invariance–sensitivity trade-off in representation learning. Our starting point is the standard practice of enforcing similarity between representations of augmented versions of the same input. On top of this, we add a complementary objective that explicitly encourages diversity in the learned representations, preventing them from collapsing into an overly compressed space. This diversity objective is designed to preserve variability both within and across classes, so that representations remain rich enough to capture task-relevant differences.

A key feature of our approach is that it introduces a single hyperparameter that smoothly interpolates between more invariant and more sensitive regimes. When this hyperparameter is small, the diversity term dominates, and the model is encouraged to retain fine-grained distinctions at the expense of some invariance. When it is large, the invariance term dominates, and the model aggressively aligns augmented examples, potentially sacrificing some sensitivity for greater robustness. By sweeping this parameter, we obtain a controlled family of models that trace out an empirical frontier between in-distribution accuracy and robustness to corruptions, perturbations, or paraphrases.

We instantiate this framework in both vision and text domains, using standard deep architectures and widely used benchmarks. In each case, we apply domain-appropriate augmentations or paraphrases and train models under our dual-objective loss. We then evaluate in-distribution performance on the original test sets, as well as robustness under a variety of distribution shifts, including common image corruptions and noisy textual rewrites. Beyond accuracy metrics, we also probe the geometry of the learned representations, examining how intra-class and inter-class structure vary as we move along the invariance–sensitivity continuum.

Our experiments demonstrate that this explicit control often leads to improved trade-offs compared to strong baselines that enforce invariance without any diversity-preserving mechanism. In particular, we find that there exist intermediate regimes where modest reductions in invariance yield substantial gains in in-distribution accuracy, or where slight increases in invariance improve robustness with negligible cost. These findings suggest that the widely used practice of fixing augmentation strategies and invariance strength may leave performance on the table.

In summary, this paper argues that treating the invariance–sensitivity balance as a first-class design choice, rather than a byproduct of heuristic settings, can yield both conceptual clarity and practical benefits. By providing a simple, tunable mechanism to navigate this balance, our framework enables practitioners to adapt representation learning to the specific robustness and accuracy requirements of their applications, while offering a structured lens for analyzing how representations respond to transformations in complex, real-world data.
