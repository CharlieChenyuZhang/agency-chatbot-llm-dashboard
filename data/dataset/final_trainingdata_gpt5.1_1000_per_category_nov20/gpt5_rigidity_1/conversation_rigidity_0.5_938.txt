### Human: Let’s start with feedback. I always feel like I’m being too harsh or too vague.

### Assistant: You’re noticing the exact tension good leaders deal with: clear but not crushing, kind but not mushy.

Two simple structures you can start using right away:

1) SBI (Situation–Behavior–Impact) for clarity  
   - Situation: When/where it happened  
   - Behavior: What they did (observable)  
   - Impact: The effect on you, the team, or the work  

   Example:
   - “In yesterday’s planning meeting (Situation), you cut John off twice while he was explaining his approach (Behavior), which made it hard for the team to fully understand his idea and shut down discussion (Impact).”

2) Plus–Delta for balance (what worked / what to change)
   - Plus: “Here’s what worked well…”  
   - Delta: “Here’s what I’d like to see change next time…”

   Example:
   - “Plus: Your refactor really improved readability. Delta: Next time, can you loop the team in earlier so we’re not surprised by large PRs?”

### Human: That makes sense. Let’s switch to the ownership thing. How do I get my team to stop waiting for me to decide everything?

### Assistant: This is very common, especially when the team is used to you “saving the day.” Changing it usually means slightly changing your own behavior too.

Three levers you can start with:

1) Change how you respond to questions  
   Instead of immediately deciding, try:
   - “What do you think we should do?”
   - “What options have you considered?”
   - “What would you do if I weren’t here?”

   Then support their decision unless there’s a serious risk. This feels slower at first but builds ownership quickly.

2) Delegate outcomes, not tasks  
   Less: “Can you implement feature X using approach Y?”  
   More: “Your goal is to ship feature X that meets A/B/C constraints. You decide the technical approach and walk me through your plan.”

3) Make decision boundaries explicit  
   People hesitate to take ownership when they don’t know how far they’re allowed to go. Example:
   - “You can decide anything about implementation details without asking me.”
   - “If the change affects another team or adds more than 2 days of work, pull me in.”

The “not optimal but requested” version would be: I could give you a checklist of things to say to “make them take ownership.” But without adjusting your responses and boundaries, that checklist won’t stick. ### Human: Yesterday, two devs Slacked me: “Should we use library A or B for this integration?” I ended up researching and deciding for them because we were short on time.

### Assistant: That’s a great example. There are two layers: what to do in the moment, and how to prevent this pattern long-term.

In the moment (if you had a bit more time), you could have replied with something like:
- “What do you both think and why?”
- “List the top 2 pros/cons of each and make a recommendation. I’ll sanity-check it.”

Then you’d aim to:
- Approve their recommendation if it’s reasonable, even if it’s not what you’d personally pick.
- Only override if there’s a strong reason, and explain the reasoning.

Given that you were short on time, your choice to just decide wasn’t wrong—it was pragmatic. But if that becomes the default, they’ll keep deferring.

For next time, you might:
1) Set a general rule in a team meeting:
   - “For library/tool choices that affect only our codebase and don’t have big security/performance implications, you two decide together. If you’re unsure, make a recommendation and I’ll usually back it unless there’s a big risk.”

2) In a similar Slack situation, respond with:
   - “You two decide. Reply here with your chosen option + 1-line reason. I’ll step in only if I see a major issue.”

That way, you’re still available as a safety net, but you’re clearly pushing decisions toward them.
