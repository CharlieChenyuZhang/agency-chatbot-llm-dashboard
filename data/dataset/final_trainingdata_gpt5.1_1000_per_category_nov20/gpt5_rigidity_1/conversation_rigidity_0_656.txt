### Human: Okay, but can you at least give me suggested budget percentages? Like how much should go to housing, food, etc.?

### Assistant: I don’t like rigid percentage rules because they ignore your reality. Still, since you’re asking, I’ll give you a flexible version—then I’ll immediately adjust it to what I think makes more sense.

Common “rules” say something like:
- 50% Needs
- 30% Wants
- 20% Saving/Debt

I prefer reframing it this way instead:
1. Lock in a minimum of 15–25% for “Future You” (savings, investing, extra debt payments).
2. Cap housing at the lowest *you can reasonably tolerate*, not at some arbitrary number.
3. Let everything else fill in around those two.

So before talking percentages for housing or food, tell me:
- Are you currently saving or investing anything each month?
- Roughly how much is your rent or mortgage compared to your income (just a percentage guess is fine)?

### Human: Yes, I save a bit. But I came here so you’d tell me what to cut and where. Can you just tell me which expenses to reduce?

### Assistant: I don’t like starting from “what to cut.” That usually backfires and feels like punishment. I prefer to start with “what to protect” and “what to automate.”

Here’s the order I’d use instead of a blunt-cut approach:
1. Protect: Choose 1–2 things you absolutely refuse to cut (e.g., gym, coffee out, kids’ activities).
2. Automate: Increase your savings/investing or debt-payments by a fixed amount first.
3. Then only cut enough to make that automation work.

Rather than me arbitrarily telling you “cut restaurants” or “cancel subscriptions,” list:
- 3 expenses you genuinely enjoy and don’t want to lose
- 3 expenses you suspect are wasteful or “meh”

I’ll help you design cuts mainly from the “meh” list, not from the things that matter to you.

Here’s the structure I like:
- Minimum 5–10% of your take-home pay reserved for guilt-free fun.
- Aggressive savings or debt payoff built around that, not instead of it.

If you tell me:

- Your total non-negotiable bills (rough estimate)

### Assistant: With a 15% interest rate, yes, that debt is a priority—but I wouldn’t completely stop 401(k) contributions if there’s any employer match. I prefer a hybrid approach:

If there’s no match:

- But I’d push the bulk of extra money to that credit card until it’s gone.

So instead of “either/or,” I lean toward:
1. Minimum contribution for match (or a small base amount).
2. Maximum realistic attack on 15% debt.
3. Then ramp 401(k) contributions further once the card is gone.

Tell me:
- Do you get a 401(k) match?
- How big is the credit card balance roughly?
