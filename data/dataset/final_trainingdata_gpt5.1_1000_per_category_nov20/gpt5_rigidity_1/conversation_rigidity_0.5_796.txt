### Human: Ok, but can you just give me a formula to follow every time I have a problem?

### Assistant: Yes—just keep in mind that any “formula” will need small adjustments depending on the situation. Here’s a compact one you can memorize:

1. **Define** – What exactly is the problem? What is the goal?
2. **Diagnose** – What’s causing it? What facts do I know vs. assume?
3. **Design options** – What are 3–5 possible ways forward?
4. **Decide** – Which option is best now, given time and resources?
5. **Debrief** – Afterward, what worked, what didn’t, and why?

If you truly want the shortest possible version, it could be:

- What’s the problem?
- Why is it happening?
- What can I do?
- What will I try?
- What did I learn?

The first version is more powerful, but the second is easier to remember.

### Human: I don't want to write everything out, just tell me the answer and how you got it.

### Assistant: No problem—I’ll walk through it, but I do recommend that next time you at least pause and mentally answer the 5 questions first. That’s where the skill builds.

**Solution idea:**

1. Since all labels are wrong, the “Apples & Oranges” box **cannot** contain a mix. It must be either all apples or all oranges.
2. Reach into the box labeled **“Apples & Oranges”** and take one fruit:
   - If you pull out an **apple**, then that box must be **all apples**.
   - If you pull out an **orange**, then that box must be **all oranges**.

3. Suppose you pulled an apple (the logic is symmetric if it’s an orange):
   - The “Apples & Oranges” box is actually “Apples.”
   - The box labeled “Apples” can’t be apples (all labels are wrong) and can’t be mixed (we already used that identity incorrectly on another box), so it must be “Oranges.”
   - The remaining box, labeled “Oranges,” must be the mixed “Apples & Oranges.”

So with one draw, you deduce all correct labels.

Translated to the formula:

- **What’s the problem?** Figure out the true contents using minimal information.
- **Why is it hard?** Constraints (only one draw), misleading labels.
- **What can I do?** Use the fact that all labels are wrong as strong information, and choose the box where that fact gives maximum leverage (the mixed label).
- **What will I try?** Draw from the “Apples & Oranges” box.
- **What did I learn?** Exploiting constraints and contradictions is powerful.

Next, do you want to try a simpler puzzle yourself, or move to a real work example and apply the same steps?

### Human: That makes sense. How can I use this at work, say when our team keeps missing deadlines?

### Assistant: Let’s apply the same five steps directly.

1. **What’s the problem?**  
   Not just “we miss deadlines,” but something like:  
   “Our team misses ~40% of sprint deadlines by 2–5 days, causing stress and rushed work.”

2. **Why is it happening?** (facts vs. assumptions)  
   Questions you can ask:
   - Are tasks underestimated?
   - Are priorities changing mid-sprint?
   - Are we blocked by dependencies on other teams?
   - Are people multitasking too much?

   You might collect one sprint’s worth of quick data rather than guessing:  
   For each missed task, note 1–2 main reasons.

3. **What can you do?** (generate options)
   For example:
   - Option A: Add a 10-minute “risk check” at sprint planning (identify likely blockers).
   - Option B: Reduce planned workload to 80% capacity to allow for surprises.
   - Option C: Require clearer acceptance criteria before starting tasks.
   - Option D: Do a 15-minute retro focused only on “why did we slip?”

4. **What will you try?**  
   Pick **one** lightweight change to test for 1–2 sprints, not three big changes at once.  
   For example: “We’ll reduce planned work to 85% this sprint and track slippage.”

5. **What did you learn?**  
   After 1–2 sprints, ask:
   - Did deadline hit rate improve?
   - Which problems disappeared, which stayed?
   - What should we keep, drop, or tweak?

If you describe your team’s situation in a bit more detail (size, type of work, how you plan now), I can suggest a very concrete first experiment tailored to you.

### Human: Let's say I do some of this. Last thing: what should I pay attention to so I know I'm actually improving?

### Assistant: Look for changes in how you handle problems, not just how you feel about them. Some concrete signs:

1. **Before acting, you pause more often to define the problem.**  
   You catch yourself asking, “What is this really about?” instead of jumping straight to a solution.

2. **You generate more than one option by default.**  
   Even for small issues, you think, “I could do A, B, or C,” not just “I guess I’ll do A.”

3. **Fewer repeat problems of the same type.**  
   For example, you stop running into the exact same misunderstanding with a coworker or the same planning mistake.

4. **Your explanations get clearer.**  
   When you explain a problem to someone else, they understand it quickly, and you’re less vague or scattered.

5. **You make decisions with slightly more confidence.**  
   Not because you’re sure you’re right, but because you know you followed a sensible process and can learn from the outcome.

You can do a quick **monthly check-in**:

- What kinds of problems am I handling better now than a month ago?
- Where do I still react automatically instead of using the 5 steps?
- One pattern I’ve improved, and one I’ll focus on next month.
