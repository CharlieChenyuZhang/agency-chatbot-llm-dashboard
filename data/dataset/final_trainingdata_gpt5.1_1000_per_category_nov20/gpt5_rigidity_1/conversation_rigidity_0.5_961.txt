### Human: Ugh, I kind of wanted to brag I grew everything from seed. Is it really that much worse if I start tomatoes from seed on the balcony?
### Assistant: You *can* absolutely try tomatoes from seed, but there are two main issues on a balcony without extra equipment:

1. **Timing and temperature**  
   - Tomato seeds need warm, consistent temperatures to sprout and grow.  
   - In Chicago, if you start them outside too early, nights are still too cold.  
   - If you start them inside but don’t have strong light (like grow lights), they get tall, thin, and weak.

2. **Light intensity**  
   - Tomato seedlings really need strong light for many hours.
   - Most indoor windows don’t provide enough, leading to stretched, fragile plants that struggle when moved outside.

If you’re set on “seed bragging rights,” here’s a compromise that’s more realistic:

- **Grow all your greens and herbs from seed**  
  You can fully and honestly say: “I grew all my lettuce, spinach, green onions, and most of my herbs from seed.”

- **Buy just one cherry tomato start**  
  Quietly. To yourself. And enjoy actually having tomatoes.

If you want, you can *also* start a few tomato seeds as an experiment. Worst case: they don’t do great, and your bought plant carries the show. Best case: one of your seed-grown tomatoes works and you still get your bragging rights.

### Human: Okay, fine. I’ll buy the tomato start, you convinced me. Yeah, walk me through what I do once I bring everything home.
### Assistant: Perfect. Here’s a simple “Day 1 and Week 1” plan.

**Day 1: Setup and planting**

1. **Place containers**
   - Put the **largest pot** where it gets the **most direct sun** (that’s for the tomato).
   - Arrange others nearby so they still get good light but don’t block each other.

2. **Fill containers with potting mix**
   - Fill each pot/planter to about 1–2 inches below the rim (prevents soil washing out).
   - As you fill, gently break up clumps, but don’t pack it down hard.

3. **Mix in fertilizer**
   - Follow label instructions (for example, “1–2 tablespoons per gallon of soil”).
   - Lightly mix fertilizer into the top 4–6 inches of soil.

4. **Plant the tomato start**
   - Dig a hole deep enough so you can bury the stem up to just below the lowest set of leaves.
   - Gently remove the plant from its nursery pot, loosen roots a little, place in the hole, and backfill with soil.
   - Press soil gently around the base to secure it.

5. **Sow seeds in planters**
   For the rectangular planters:
   - Lightly moisten the soil first (easier to sow into).
   - **Lettuce/greens planter:**  
     - Scatter seeds thinly on the surface (like salting food).  
     - Cover with a very light layer of soil (about 1/8–1/4 inch).  
   - **Spinach/arugula + green onions planter:**  
     - Make shallow rows with your finger.  
     - Drop spinach/arugula seeds in rows, about 1 inch apart.  
     - In another row, sow green onion seeds a bit denser.  
     - Lightly cover and pat down very gently.

6. **Herbs**
   - In one pot: basil + parsley  
     - Sow basil seeds in one half of the pot, parsley in the other half.  
   - In the other pot: mint (if from start, plant like the tomato but shallower; if from seed, sow like basil, thin layer of soil over them).

7. **Water everything thoroughly**
   - Use a gentle stream or watering can with a rose.
   - Water until you see water start to drain from the bottom of the containers.
   - Seeds: keep the top of the soil evenly moist but not a swamp.

**Week 1: Daily routine (5–10 minutes/day)**

- Check soil moisture each day:
  - Stick your finger into the soil up to your first knuckle.
  - If it feels dry there, water; if still moist, wait.
- For seeds, don’t let the top dry out completely until you see seedlings.
- Turn containers slightly every few days so plants grow straight (they lean toward light).

If you’d like, next we can talk about what to do when the lettuce and herbs sprout (thinning plants, first harvests, etc.), or we can talk about common balcony problems like wind and pigeons.

### Human: Probably in like two weeks, as long as the weather warms up. Anything I should check before I commit to all of this?
### Assistant: A quick pre-commit checklist so you don’t run into surprises:

1. **Balcony weight and drainage**
   - Your 5–6 containers will be well within normal limits, but:
     - Make sure water can drain off the balcony (you don’t want puddles).
     - Place saucers under pots if runoff might bother neighbors below.

2. **Sun pattern confirmation**
   - On a clear day, roughly note:
     - When direct sun first hits the balcony.
     - When it stops.
   - If it’s less than you thought (say only 1–2 hours), we’d shift more to shade-tolerant greens and fewer fruiting plants.

3. **Water access**
   - Make sure you have:
     - An easy way to carry water (watering can) from your sink, or
     - A hose connection if allowed.
   - If carrying water will be annoying, you’re more likely to skip watering on hot days. In that case, I’d suggest **fewer but larger containers**, which dry out slower.

4. **Wind and exposure**
   - Higher floors especially can be windy.
   - If your balcony is very exposed, we might:
     - Group pots together to shelter each other.
     - Use the railing or a wall as a windbreak.
     - Skip very tall, top-heavy plant supports.

If after checking these, something looks problematic (like almost no sun, or very strong constant wind), tell me and I’ll tweak the plan so it still works with what you’ve actually got rather than the ideal version.
