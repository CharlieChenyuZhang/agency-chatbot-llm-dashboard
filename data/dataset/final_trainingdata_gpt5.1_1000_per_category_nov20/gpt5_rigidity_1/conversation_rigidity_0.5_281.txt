Email 3 – 3–5 days later
Subject: How this newsletter will help you get from A → B

- Define “Point A” (their current situation).
- Define “Point B” (their desired outcome).

- Invite them to stick around for [upcoming issue topics].

Hi [First name],

When your pipeline is thin, it’s tempting to say yes to any client who can pay.

The problem: misfit clients eat time and attention you could use to find better ones — and they rarely turn into renewals or good referrals.

Here’s a simple way to reduce bad-fit projects without pretending your pipeline is full.

Step 1: Define your “no-go” list

Take 5–10 minutes and write down 3–5 traits of projects you regret taking. For example:
- No clear decision-maker
- Vague problem: “We just need things to run better”
- No business metric tied to success
- Tiny team but huge expectations
- Wants you to be “hands” with no real authority

Turn this into a simple rule:

“If a project has 2 or more of these traits, I don’t take it.”

You don’t have to tell clients this. It’s a private filter to protect your future time.

Step 2: Add 3 clarifying questions to your sales calls

Before you say yes, ask:
1) “What will be different in the business 3–6 months after this project, if it goes well?”
2) “Who actually decides whether this was worth it?”
3) “What would make you look back and say this engagement was a clear win?”

Thin answers or lots of hand-waving are a warning sign. If they can’t describe a clear change, you’re likely being hired as a pair of hands, not a consultant.

Step 3: Have a default “polite no” ready

When you see two or more no-go traits, use a simple line like:

“Based on what you’ve shared, I don’t think I’m the best fit for this project. I typically do my best work when [briefly describe better-fit projects]. I’m happy to recommend other options if that would be helpful.”

This makes it easier to say no when you need to, instead of deciding in the moment.
