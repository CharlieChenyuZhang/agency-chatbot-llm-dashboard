**Quantum entanglement (overview – approximate)**  
Quantum entanglement is a physical phenomenon in which groups of particles are generated or interact in ways such that the quantum state of each particle cannot be described independently of the others, even when the particles are separated by large distances. Measurements on one entangled particle are correlated with measurements on the others in a way that cannot be explained by any local hidden-variable theory.

- **Origin and history** (high confidence)  
  The concept was famously discussed in the 1935 Einstein–Podolsky–Rosen (EPR) paper, which argued that quantum mechanics might be incomplete. In 1964, John Bell derived Bell’s theorem, showing that no local hidden-variable theory can reproduce all the predictions of quantum mechanics. Experiments by Alain Aspect and others in the 1980s and later “loophole-free” Bell tests (2010s) confirmed violations of Bell inequalities, strongly supporting quantum mechanics.

- **Nonlocal correlations (high confidence)**  
  Entanglement leads to correlations that are stronger than any classical theory allows, but it does not permit faster-than-light communication. Changes in one subsystem cannot be used to send controllable signals to another spacelike-separated system.

- **Mathematical description (medium confidence on exact phrasing)**  
  In quantum theory, entangled states are described by a joint wavefunction in a tensor-product Hilbert space that cannot be written as a product of states of the individual subsystems. For example, Bell states (such as \(|\Phi^+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|00\rangle + |11\rangle)\)) are maximally entangled states of two qubits.

- **Applications (high confidence on categories; medium on detail)**  
  Entanglement is a key resource in:
  - **Quantum teleportation**
  - **Superdense coding**
  - **Quantum key distribution (QKD)**
  - **Quantum computing**, especially in algorithms and error correction
  - **Quantum metrology**, improving measurement sensitivity

- **Interpretational issues (medium confidence)**  
  Entanglement plays a central role in debates about the interpretation of quantum mechanics, including many-worlds, Copenhagen, and objective collapse theories. It’s closely related to topics like decoherence and the quantum-classical boundary.

**Entanglement entropy (approximate, Wikipedia-style)**  
Entanglement entropy is a measure of quantum entanglement for a bipartite system. Consider a system described by a pure state \(|\psi\rangle\) on a Hilbert space that can be decomposed into two subsystems, \(A\) and \(B\). The total density matrix is \(\rho = |\psi\rangle\langle\psi|\). To study entanglement between \(A\) and \(B\), one defines the **reduced density matrix** for subsystem \(A\):

\[
\rho_A = \mathrm{Tr}_B(\rho),
\]

where \(\mathrm{Tr}_B\) denotes the partial trace over subsystem \(B\).

The **von Neumann entropy** of \(\rho_A\),

\[
S(\rho_A) = -\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_A \log \rho_A),
\]

is called the **entanglement entropy** of subsystem \(A\) (for a pure state of the whole system). For a bipartite pure state, \(S(\rho_A) = S(\rho_B)\), and this quantity serves as a measure of how entangled the two subsystems are:

- If the state is **separable** (not entangled), then \(\rho_A\) is pure and \(S(\rho_A) = 0\).
- If the state is **maximally entangled**, \(S(\rho_A)\) is maximal (for finite-dimensional systems, \(\log d\), where \(d\) is the dimension of \(A\)).

In many-body physics and quantum field theory, entanglement entropy is used to study:
- **Quantum phase transitions**
- **Area laws** (where entropy scales with the boundary area of a region rather than its volume)
- The connection between **quantum information** and **spacetime geometry**, especially in holography and black hole physics.
