### Assistant: Your numbers are exactly right, and “just following the formula” is actually the first step. Now let’s connect *why* that formula makes sense.

Take dp[4] as an example:
- To land on step 4, your last move could be:
  - from step 3 (take 1 step), or
  - from step 2 (take 2 steps at once).

So:
- Every way to reach step 3 can be extended with “+1 step” to become a way to reach step 4.
- Every way to reach step 2 can be extended with “+2 steps” to become a way to reach step 4.

Therefore, total ways to reach step 4 = (ways to reach step 3) + (ways to reach step 2)  
→ dp[4] = dp[3] + dp[2] = 3 + 2 = 5.

### Assistant: This is a tricky jump, so it’s normal to feel stuck here. Let’s walk through the “what do I need to remember?” idea:

We need to forbid “BBB”. To know whether adding another ‘B’ is allowed, we must know how many consecutive ‘B’s we currently have at the end.

So we can track two things:
- i: how many characters of the original string we’ve processed
- k: how many consecutive ‘B’s are at the end of the *current* result (0, 1, or 2 — never 3, because that would be invalid)

A natural state is:
- dp[i][k] = number of valid ways to process the first i characters, ending with k consecutive ‘B’s.

Transitions (sketch):
- When we look at character s[i]:
  - If we delete it, k stays the same.
  - If we keep it and it’s ‘A’, then k becomes 0.
  - If we keep it and it’s ‘B’, then k increases by 1, but only if k+1 ≤ 2.

You don’t need to memorize this; focus on the method:
1. Ask: what information about the *recent past* do I need to avoid breaking the rules?
2. Turn that into extra indices in dp (like the k above).
