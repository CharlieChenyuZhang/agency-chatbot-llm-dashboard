### Assistant: There are a few low-cost tricks to squeeze a bit more usable light out of a shady space, though they won’t fully replace missing sun:

1. **Reflective surfaces**
   - Put a light-colored or reflective surface behind/next to the tomato:
     - A piece of white foam board or a white-painted board.
     - Even aluminum foil on cardboard (not pretty, but works).
   - Angle it so it bounces light onto the plant during the brightest part of the day.

2. **Moveable pot**
   - If you’re home when there *is* some sun, you can:
     - Shift the pot slightly to catch any rays coming in at different angles.

3. **Keep leaves healthy**
   - Don’t let the plant sit in wet, cold soil constantly.
   - Remove yellowing, diseased leaves so the plant uses its limited energy efficiently.

4. **Don’t overcrowd**
   - Only **one tomato plant per 5-gallon pot**.
   - Don’t try to cram multiple tomatoes—better one reasonably happy plant than several struggling ones.

These can help a bit, but they won’t turn 1–2 hours into 6–8. So I’d frame the tomato as a **fun experiment**, and rely on your greens/herbs for reliable harvest.

### Human: It’s a little brighter in the morning but still not much. Also, I travel sometimes for work, like gone 10–14 days. Is that going to kill everything?

### Assistant: Being away 10–14 days is tough but not impossible. It depends on **season** and **temperature**:

- In **cool, cloudy weather** (which you get a fair bit in Seattle), containers dry more slowly.
- In **warm, sunny stretches**, 10–14 days without water can easily kill container plants.

You have a few options, some low-effort:

1. **Ask a plant-sitter**
   - Neighbor/friend building mate waters once every 3–4 days.
   - You can leave a simple note: “Water each pot until water drips out the bottom.”

2. **DIY low-cost watering help**
   - Use **plastic bottles** with small holes in the cap, buried partway in the soil, filled before you leave.
   - Or “water spikes” that attach to bottles—cheap at garden stores.
   - This slows drying, but probably won’t cover 2 weeks in hot weather.

3. **Seasonal timing**
   - Try to avoid planting right before a long trip.
   - Plant after a trip so the most vulnerable stage (tiny seedlings) is when you’re home.

Given your schedule, I’d suggest:
- Start with greens/herbs and accept that if a mid-summer heatwave hits while you’re gone, you might lose some plants.
- Over time, you’ll get a feel for how fast your specific containers dry out.

If having to arrange watering sounds like too much, we can also talk about **even lower-maintenance options** (like only planting in the cooler, wetter shoulder seasons, or focusing on indoor microgreens).
