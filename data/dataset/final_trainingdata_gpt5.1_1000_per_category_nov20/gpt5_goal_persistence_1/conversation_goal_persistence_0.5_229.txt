That means we can move pretty quickly through basic function notation and into the core calculus idea: **rate of change**, which leads to derivatives.

#### Intuition step: Average rate of change

Take f(x) = x².

- At x = 2, f(2) = 4  
- At x = 3, f(3) = 9

The **average rate of change** from x = 2 to x = 3 is:
\[
\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \frac{9 - 4}{3 - 2} = \frac{5}{1} = 5
\]

That 5 means: “on average, the function’s value increased by 5 for each 1 unit of x” between 2 and 3.

In graph terms, that’s the slope of the secant line through the points (2, 4) and (3, 9).

#### Where calculus comes in

Calculus asks: what is the rate of change **at a single point**, say at x = 2?

We can’t just do “from 2 to 2” because that’s 0/0. So we:
- look at average rate of change from 2 to 2.1, 2 to 2.01, 2 to 2.001, etc.
- watch what those slopes are *approaching*.

That “approaching” idea is the core of a limit and the derivative.

#### Tangent slope as “instant speed”

Imagine you’re driving and your position (in km) after t hours is:
- s(t) = t².

So:
- At t = 2 hours, you’re at s(2) = 4 km.
- At t = 3 hours, you’re at s(3) = 9 km.

Average speed from 2h to 3h:
\[
\frac{9 - 4}{3 - 2} = 5 \text{ km/h}
\]

Average speed from 2h to 2.1h:
- s(2.1) = (2.1)² = 4.41
\[
\frac{4.41 - 4}{2.1 - 2} = \frac{0.41}{0.1} = 4.1 \text{ km/h}
\]

If we kept going, from 2 to 2.01, 2 to 2.001, etc., the average speed numbers get closer to **4**.

So we say:  
“Your **instantaneous speed** at t = 2 is 4 km/h.”

That instantaneous speed **is** the derivative of s(t) at t = 2.

For s(t) = t², calculus tells us the derivative is:
- s′(t) = 2t
- so at t = 2, s′(2) = 4.

You don’t need to fully accept the formula yet; the key idea:

> Derivative at a point = limit of average rate of change over smaller and smaller intervals around that point.

Let’s do that step-by-step once. I’ll keep the algebra light.

We’ll find the derivative of f(x) = x² using the **definition**:

\[
f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}
\]

1. Compute f(x + h) for f(x) = x²:
   - f(x + h) = (x + h)² = x² + 2xh + h²

2. Form the difference:
   - f(x + h) − f(x) = (x² + 2xh + h²) − x² = 2xh + h²

3. Divide by h:
   - \(\frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h} = \frac{2xh + h²}{h} = 2x + h\)

4. Now take the limit as h → 0:
   - 2x + h → 2x

So:
\[
f'(x) = 2x
\]

Nothing magical—just expanding (x + h)² and simplifying.

### Assistant: That’s a very common preference, and it’s reasonable. You’ve seen enough of the “definition with limits” to know it’s not pure magic; we can now lean more on rules.

Here’s a small **cheat sheet** of basic derivative rules:

1. Constant rule:  
   - If f(x) = C (a constant), then f′(x) = 0

2. Power rule:  
   - If f(x) = xⁿ, then f′(x) = n·xⁿ⁻¹  
     (works for n = 1, 2, 3, … and beyond)

3. Constant multiple rule:  
   - If f(x) = k·g(x), then f′(x) = k·g′(x)

4. Sum rule:  
   - If f(x) = g(x) + h(x), then f′(x) = g′(x) + h′(x)

With just those, you can handle a lot.
