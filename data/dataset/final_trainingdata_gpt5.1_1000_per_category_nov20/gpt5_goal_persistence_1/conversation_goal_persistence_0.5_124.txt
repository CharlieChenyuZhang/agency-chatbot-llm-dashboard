### Assistant: That’s a valid instinct—understanding the “why” can help. The trap is spending so long on theory that you never do the practice, but we can sketch a lean version.

Here’s a simple, practical **mini-theory of creativity**:

1. **Input:** You need varied, rich experiences and information to remix (movies, music, conversations, places, reading).
2. **Connection:** Creativity is often connecting unrelated things in a new way.
3. **Play:** Low-pressure experimentation. Treat ideas like toys, not tests.
4. **Constraint:** Small limits (time, rules, themes) actually boost creativity by giving your brain something to push against.
5. **Iteration:** First attempts are usually rough; creativity grows as you tweak and repeat.
6. **Reflection:** Noticing what you liked, what surprised you, and what felt alive.

So every good exercise should:
- give you some *input* or reference,
- ask you to *connect* it with your life or something else,
- be *playful* enough that perfectionism chills out,
- include some *constraints*,
- and invite a tiny bit of *reflection* after.

**Mini-Chapter: Handling Boredom and Resistance**

- Boredom and resistance are *signals*, not verdicts. They often mean:
  - the task feels too big or vague,
  - there’s no immediate payoff,
  - or you’re afraid the result won’t be “good enough.”

To work with them:
1. **Shrink the task**  
   Make it so small it’s hard to refuse: “3 minutes,” “one paragraph,” “one silly idea.”

2. **Add a twist**  
   Turn a dull activity into a game:
   - timer challenge (do X in 3 minutes),
   - constraint (only use 10 words, only draw with your non-dominant hand),
   - roleplay (do this as if you’re a director / comedian / reporter).

3. **Reward the attempt, not the result**  
   Decide that “I showed up for 3–5 minutes” is a win. Everything beyond is bonus.

4. **Stop while it’s still a little fun**  
   End before you’re totally drained. That makes it easier to return next time.
