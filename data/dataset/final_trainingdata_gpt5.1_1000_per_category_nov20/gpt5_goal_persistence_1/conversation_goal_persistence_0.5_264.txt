[Subheader] When Merchant Storage Actually Pencils Out

Hi [First Name],

For the first issue, I want to start with a question I hear constantly:

“Can we make merchant storage bankable, or is this just equity gambling?”

Below is a practical framework I use when thinking about merchant exposure for grid-scale batteries.

—

1) The 3 merchant storage archetypes I actually see

In practice, most “merchant” storage deals fall into one of three buckets:

1) “Quasi-merchant” with partial offtake  
   - A share of capacity is locked in with a contract (often short to medium term).  
   - The rest rides the market for arbitrage and/or ancillary services.  
   - Lenders underwrite primarily to the contracted portion, with conservative upside from merchant.

2) “Merchant tail” after contracted period  
   - First X years under a tolling agreement or RA contract.  
   - After that, merchant exposure kicks in.  
   - Banks size debt mostly on the contracted period and haircut the tail heavily.

3) “Pure merchant” (usually equity-only or heavily structured)  
   - No long-term offtake.  
   - Revenue from arbitrage, ancillary services, and grid products.  
   - Still rare for plain-vanilla project finance; typically needs strong sponsor, sophisticated risk appetite, and conservative leverage.

If someone tells you “it’s a merchant storage deal,” one of these three is almost always hiding underneath.

—

2) What lenders actually worry about (beyond the pitch deck)

From a debt perspective, three things tend to dominate the discussion:

- Revenue visibility  
  - How much of the stack is fixed or contracted vs. modeled?  
  - How correlated is that revenue with market conditions they’ve seen before?

- Market depth  
  - Are you in a market where ancillary services or arbitrage opportunities are deep and persistent?  
  - Or are you assuming that today’s spreads/volatility last forever?

- System role  
  - Is this asset “nice to have” (opportunistic arbitrage), or is it effectively grid infrastructure (congestion relief, local reliability)?  
  - The closer you are to critical infra, the easier it is to justify long-term value.

It’s not that lenders “hate” merchant—it’s that they don’t like underwriting to thin, unproven revenue stacks in shallow markets with rosy assumptions.

—

3) Four revenue stacks that are likelier to get financed

These are patterns I see more often in deals that move forward:

1) Capacity + ancillary + limited arbitrage  
   - Capacity payment anchors the model.  
   - Ancillary and arbitrage are upside, not the core of the debt case.  
   - Works best in markets with tested capacity constructs.

2) Short- to medium-term tolling + merchant tail  
   - Tolling contract underpins debt sizing.  
   - Merchant tail is valued, but heavily haircut.  
   - Sponsor may rely on refi or upside equity value later.

3) “Storage as transmission” / grid service contracts  
   - Asset is sited to relieve congestion or provide reliability.  
   - Compensation structured more like infra than trading P&L.  
   - Often involves utilities or system operators; complexity is higher, but value is clearer.

4) Portfolio effects  
   - Single-asset merchant storage is hard.  
   - Portfolio of assets across nodes/markets with different profiles can smooth risk.  
   - Lenders may be more comfortable when diversification is real, not theoretical.

If your model leans heavily on arbitrage in a shallow market with no clear infra role, expect pushback.

—

4) Questions to sanity-check your own storage model

When I look at a model or deck for a merchant-heavy storage project, I ask:

- Which part of this revenue stack would I defend in an IC memo if prices halve?  
- What’s the simplest story I can tell about why this asset is still needed in 10+ years?  
- Who is my marginal buyer of this asset if the original plan fails?  
- Does this look more like a trading book or an infrastructure asset?

If you can’t answer those clearly, you probably need to simplify the story or adjust assumptions.
