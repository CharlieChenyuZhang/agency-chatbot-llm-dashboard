### Assistant: Let’s do it. I’ll give you a simple structure, then we can practice together.

A solid, general-purpose problem-solving framework:

1. **Understand the problem**
   - Restate it in your own words.
   - Identify: What is given? What is asked?
   - Clarify constraints: time, resources, rules.

2. **Break it down**
   - Split the big problem into smaller subproblems.
   - Ask: “What’s the very first tiny step I can take?”

3. **Generate options**
   - Brainstorm multiple approaches (even imperfect ones).
   - Ask: “If this *did* work, what would it look like?”
   - Don’t judge ideas yet; just list.

4. **Choose a strategy**
   - Pick one approach to try first (based on effort vs impact).
   - Decide how you’ll know if it’s working.

5. **Execute and adjust**
   - Try your plan in small steps.
   - Observe results and adapt quickly.

6. **Reflect and generalize**
   - What worked, what didn’t, and why?
   - How can you reuse this process next time?

### Human: Chunking and time boxing sound useful. How do I actually do chunking in practice?
### Assistant: Let’s walk through chunking using a concrete example.

Imagine you have: “Write a 5-page essay on climate change” (you can substitute your real topic in your head).

A chunked version might look like:

1. Open the assignment instructions and read them once.
2. Write a one-sentence summary of what the teacher is asking.
3. Open a blank document and give it a filename.
4. Write a provisional title (it can be bad).
5. Brainstorm 5–10 bullet points of ideas (no judging).
6. Group related bullet points into 2–3 main sections.
7. Turn each section into a heading.
8. Under each heading, write 3 bullet points you want to say.
9. Turn the bullets of the first section into rough sentences.
10. Repeat for other sections.
11. Write a quick intro (2–3 sentences that just set the stage).
12. Write a quick conclusion (2–3 sentences that summarize).
13. Read once and fix only obvious typos.
14. Save and step away for 10 minutes.
15. Come back and do one more pass for clarity.

Each of these is small enough that it doesn’t feel like “write an entire essay”; it’s more like clicking through checkboxes.

### Assistant: That makes sense; pushing too hard when you’re mentally tired usually backfires. Let’s pivot.

Here’s a low-effort **daily problem-solving habit** you can do in ~10–15 minutes:

**“One Small Problem a Day” practice**

1. **Pick a tiny real-life problem** (nothing huge), for example:
   - “I waste time scrolling before homework.”
   - “My desk gets messy by the end of the day.”
   - “I’m always a bit late in the morning.”

2. **Spend 2 minutes defining it**:
   - What exactly happens?
   - When does it happen?
   - Why is it a problem for me?

3. **Spend 5 minutes generating options**:
   - Write at least 3 possible solutions, even if they seem silly.
   - Example for scrolling:  
     - Put phone in another room during work.  
     - Use an app blocker for 30 minutes.  
     - Only allow scrolling after finishing one task.

4. **Pick 1 option to test today**:
   - Make it very small and specific.
   - Example: “For the next 20 minutes while I study, my phone stays in my backpack.”

5. **End of day: 2-minute reflection**:
   - Did I try the solution?
   - What happened?
   - What would I change tomorrow?

This trains:
- Defining problems clearly,
- Generating options,
- Taking small experimental actions,
- Reflecting and adjusting.
