Let’s stay with calculus and the conjugate trick.

We want:
\[
\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sqrt{x+1} - 1}{x}
\]

We multiply top and bottom by the conjugate of the numerator: \(\sqrt{x+1} + 1\).

\[
\frac{\sqrt{x+1} - 1}{x} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{x+1} + 1}{\sqrt{x+1} + 1}
= \frac{(\sqrt{x+1} - 1)(\sqrt{x+1} + 1)}{x(\sqrt{x+1} + 1)}
\]

The numerator becomes a difference of squares:
\[
(\sqrt{x+1})^2 - 1^2 = (x+1) - 1 = x
\]

So the whole expression is:
\[
\frac{x}{x(\sqrt{x+1} + 1)} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{x+1} + 1} \quad (x \neq 0)
\]

Now we can take the limit:
\[
\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1}{\sqrt{x+1} + 1} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1} + 1} = \frac{1}{2}
\]

So:
\[
\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sqrt{x+1} - 1}{x} = \frac{1}{2}
\]

### Assistant: That’s completely fine; the algebra tricks make more sense once you see why we care about these limits.

Big picture for **derivatives**:

- The derivative of \(f(x)\) at a point \(x = a\) measures the **instantaneous rate of change** or the **slope of the tangent line** at that point.
- Formally:
  \[
  f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h}
  \]

Let’s do a concrete example where the algebra is still manageable:

Take \(f(x) = x^2\). We’ll find \(f'(a)\) from the definition.

1. Compute \(f(a+h)\).
2. Form the difference quotient \(\frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h}\).
3. Simplify, then take the limit as \(h \to 0\).

Try steps 1 and 2, and I’ll help with the simplification.

### Human: f(a+h) = (a+h)^2 = a^2 + 2ah + h^2. Then f(a+h)-f(a) = a^2 + 2ah + h^2 - a^2 = 2ah + h^2. Then divided by h gives (2ah + h^2)/h = 2a + h. Then the limit as h→0 is 2a. So derivative of x^2 is 2x, right?
### Assistant: Exactly. You just derived the classic result:
\[
\frac{d}{dx} x^2 = 2x
\]

Key idea: the limit turned the approximate “average rate of change over a tiny interval” into an exact instantaneous rate.
