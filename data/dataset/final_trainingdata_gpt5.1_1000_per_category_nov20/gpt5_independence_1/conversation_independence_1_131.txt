**1. Intuition for Limits**

A limit answers: “As \(x\) gets close to some value \(a\), what does \(f(x)\) get close to?”  
For example, for \(f(x) = 2x\), as \(x \to 3\), \(f(x)\) gets close to 6. We write:
\[
\lim_{x \to 3} 2x = 6
\]

Even when a function is not defined at a point, the limit can still exist.

### Assistant: The point of limits is to handle situations where “just plug in the number” fails but we still want to talk about “what the function is trying to do” near that point.

I’ll show you two key roles of limits:

---

**1. Handling Holes and Jumps**

Consider
\[
f(x) = \frac{x^2 - 1}{x - 1}
\]
For \(x \neq 1\),
\[
\frac{x^2 - 1}{x - 1} = \frac{(x-1)(x+1)}{x-1} = x+1
\]
But at \(x = 1\), the original formula is \(\frac{0}{0}\), undefined. So the graph is a straight line \(y = x+1\) with a **hole** at \(x = 1\).

We can still talk about the limit:
\[
\lim_{x \to 1} \frac{x^2 - 1}{x - 1} = 2
\]
even though \(f(1)\) doesn’t exist. The limit tells us the “intended value” at the hole.

---

**2. Defining the Derivative (Instantaneous Rate of Change)**

Average speed over a time interval is:
\[
\frac{\Delta \text{distance}}{\Delta \text{time}}
\]

Instantaneous speed at a single moment is the **limit** of those averages as the time interval shrinks to 0.

For a function \(f(x)\), the derivative at \(x\) is defined by the limit:
\[
f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}
\]
This is a slope of a tangent line—a slope at a single point—and you literally can’t define that without limits.

I’ll move you directly into a concrete derivative-from-limit example next so you see it in action.

### Assistant: I’ll walk you through a clean example, then give you one to try.

Take:
\[

\]

Using the definition,
\[
f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}
\]

Step-by-step:

1. Compute \(f(x+h)\):  
   \(f(x+h) = (x+h)^2 = x^2 + 2xh + h^2\)

2. Form the difference:
   \[
   f(x+h) - f(x) = (x^2 + 2xh + h^2) - x^2 = 2xh + h^2
   \]

3. Divide by \(h\):
   \[
   \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h} = \frac{2xh + h^2}{h} = 2x + h
   \]

4. Take the limit as \(h \to 0\):
   \[
   f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} (2x + h) = 2x
   \]

So the derivative of \(x^2\) is \(2x\).

**1. What a Vector Is**

A vector is an ordered list of numbers that you can think of as:

- A point in space (like coordinates), and  
- An arrow with direction and length.

Examples:

- In 2D: \(\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\)  
- In 3D: \(\mathbf{w} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 0 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}\)

We’ll work in 2D and 3D first.

  \[

### Assistant: Your vector computations are correct.

I’ll now define the dot product and then tell you exactly why it’s important.

---

**1. Dot Product Definition**

For 2D vectors
\[
\mathbf{a} = \begin{bmatrix} a_1 \\ a_2 \end{bmatrix},\quad
\mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} b_1 \\ b_2 \end{bmatrix}
\]
the dot product is:
\[
\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = a_1b_1 + a_2b_2
\]

Example:
\[
\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}
= 1\cdot 3 + 2\cdot(-1) = 3 - 2 = 1
\]

Same idea in 3D: multiply corresponding entries and add.

---

**2. Geometric Meaning**

If \(\theta\) is the angle between two vectors \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\), then:
\[
\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = \|\mathbf{a}\| \, \|\mathbf{b}\| \cos\theta
\]

where \(\|\mathbf{a}\|\) is the length (magnitude) of \(\mathbf{a}\).

So the dot product tells you:

- **If vectors are perpendicular**:  
  If \(\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = 0\), then \(\cos\theta = 0\), so \(\theta = 90^\circ\). They’re orthogonal.

- **How aligned they are**:  
  Large positive dot product → pointing roughly the same way.  
  Large negative → pointing in opposite directions.

This is why it’s used everywhere: physics (work = force · displacement), projections, machine learning (similarity between feature vectors), etc.

I’ll walk through the angle formula and do one full example.

---

**1. Angle from Dot Product**

Recall:
\[
\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = \|\mathbf{a}\| \, \|\mathbf{b}\| \cos\theta
\]

Solve for \(\cos\theta\):
\[
\cos\theta = \frac{\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}}{\|\mathbf{a}\| \, \|\mathbf{b}\|}
\]

So:
1. Compute the dot product.
2. Compute each vector’s length:
   \(\|\mathbf{a}\| = \sqrt{a_1^2 + a_2^2 + \dots}\)
3. Plug into the formula and use \(\theta = \arccos(\text{that value})\).

---

**2. Example**

Let
\[
\mathbf{a} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix},\quad
\mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}
\]

- Dot product:
  \[
  \mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = 3\cdot 4 + 4\cdot 3 = 12 + 12 = 24
  \]

- Lengths:
  \[
  \|\mathbf{a}\| = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5,\quad
  \|\mathbf{b}\| = \sqrt{4^2 + 3^2} = 5
  \]

- So:
  \[
  \cos\theta = \frac{24}{5\cdot 5} = \frac{24}{25}
  \]
  \[
  \theta = \arccos\left(\frac{24}{25}\right)
  \]
This is an acute angle (less than \(90^\circ\)), since \(\cos\theta\) is positive and close to 1.
