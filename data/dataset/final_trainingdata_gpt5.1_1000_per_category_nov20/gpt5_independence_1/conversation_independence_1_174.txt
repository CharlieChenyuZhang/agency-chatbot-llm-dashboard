### Human: I’m not very creative. How do I actually make good posts for these businesses?

### Assistant: I’ll give you a simple content system you can reuse for almost any local business, so you don’t rely on “creativity.”

For each client, you work with 4 recurring content types:

1) Showcase the product/service  
- Photos of dishes (restaurants), haircuts (barbers), workouts (gyms), etc.  
- Format:  
  - Before/after  
  - Close-up of the product  
  - Short video of the process (making a dish, doing a haircut, training session).  
- Caption formula:  
  - What it is  
  - Who it’s for  
  - What benefit it gives  
  - Soft call to action

Example (restaurant):  
Photo: Best-selling dish.  
Caption:  
“Today’s favorite: our spicy chicken bowl 🔥  
Perfect if you want something filling but not too heavy. Cooked fresh every day.  
Visit us at [Street] in [Area] or call [number] to order ahead.”

2) Showcase people and story  
- Photos/videos of staff smiling, working, or interacting with customers.  
- Caption formula:  
  - Introduce the person  
  - Share one small detail about them  
  - Connect back to the business

Example (salon):  
Photo: Stylist with tools.  
Caption:  
“This is Ana. She’s been a stylist for 7 years and loves doing bold color transformations.  
If you’re thinking about changing your look before the weekend, ask for Ana when you book.”

3) Social proof and results  
- Before/after, testimonials, screenshots of reviews.  
- Caption formula:  
  - Client’s situation before  
  - What they got  
  - Subtle call to action

Example (gym):  
Photo: Member before/after (or blurred if needed).  
Caption:  
“Sam wanted more energy for work and to feel confident again. After 3 months of 3x/week sessions, he’s stronger, leaner, and more energetic.  
If you want similar results, send us a DM with the word ‘START’ and we’ll help you plan your first week.”

4) Helpful tips and FAQs  
- Simple text posts or photos with overlaid text.  
- Answer questions customers already have: pricing, booking, parking, preparation, opening hours, etc.  
- Caption formula:  
  - Question  
  - Short, clear answer  
  - Invite further questions

Example (barber):  
Post: “How often should you get a haircut?”  
Caption:  
“Short styles: every 3–4 weeks.  
Medium: every 5–6 weeks.  
Long: every 6–8 weeks.  
If you’re not sure what works for your face shape, we’ll help you pick when you visit.”

Your weekly posting formula per client (3 posts/week):  
- 1x product/service showcase  
- 1x people or story post  
- 1x social proof OR helpful tip

You’ll store “content prompts” per client in a simple doc, like:  
- “Top 5 dishes to feature”  
- “Photos of staff to introduce”  
- “Most common questions customers ask”

This way, you’re executing a system, not relying on inspiration.

“In the first 1–2 months, the main signs we’re on the right track are:

This gives them confidence without you lying or overpromising.

### Human: When should I quit my job for this business?

### Assistant: You’ll treat quitting your job as a milestone decision based on numbers, not feelings. Use these rules:

1) Minimum financial threshold  
You only consider quitting when your business:  
- Consistently makes at least 70%–100% of your current monthly take-home pay for 3–6 consecutive months.  
- Has at least 5–7 active clients paying monthly, with more leads in your pipeline.

2) Basic safety buffer  
Before quitting, you have:  
- 3–6 months of living expenses saved.  
- Enough clients that even if 1–2 leave, you can still cover core bills.

3) Clear growth path  
You’ve already:  
- Raised prices for new clients at least once.  
- Systematized your work (standard onboarding, content process, reporting templates).  
- Identified at least one niche (e.g., “restaurants in [city]”) where you’re getting traction.

A concrete example:  
- You charge $500/month on average per client.  
- You have 7 clients = $3,500/month revenue.  
- Your personal expenses are ~$2,000/month.  
- You have $6,000 saved.  
In that scenario, quitting becomes a rational option, especially if demand is growing and you’re near full capacity with your current time.

Until those conditions are met, you keep the job, treat the business as a serious “second shift,” and use the job to fund your runway.
