  - Exposure to alarming or misleading content (e.g., health scares, conspiracy theories, sensational news) can increase stress and confusion.  
  - “Doomscrolling” during crises correlates with higher anxiety.

**4. Nuanced Findings from Research (1–2 minutes)**  
- Correlation ≠ causation:  
  - Most studies show links, not proof that social media directly causes mental illness.  
- How it’s used matters more than how much:  
  - Active, meaningful interaction (chatting with friends, sharing) tends to be less harmful than passive scrolling and comparing.  
- Individual differences:  
  - People with prior mental health vulnerabilities may be more negatively affected.  
  - Personality traits (e.g., neuroticism, low self-esteem) may increase risk.

**5. Strategies for Healthier Social Media Use (2–3 minutes)**  
- **Set boundaries**  
  - Time limits (e.g., 30–60 minutes per day for recreational use).

- **Shift how you engage**

  - Seek professional help if social media use worsens mood or functioning.  
  - Report and block harassing accounts; use platform safety tools.

**6. Conclusion (1 minute)**  
- Social media isn’t purely good or bad; its impact depends on design, individual vulnerability, and usage patterns.  
- With awareness and healthy habits, users can reduce risks and amplify benefits.

**Slide 1 – Title: “The Impact of Social Media on Mental Health”**

“Hello, my name is [Name], and today I’m going to talk about something that affects almost all of us every day: social media, and how it impacts our mental health.

We usually hear strong opinions—either that social media is destroying our generation, or that it’s the best thing that ever happened. In this presentation, I’ll look at both sides: the ways social media can help our mental health, the ways it can harm it, and some practical ideas for using it in a healthier way.”

---

**Slide 2 – What Is Social Media & Why It Matters**

“When I say ‘social media,’ I’m talking about platforms like Instagram, TikTok, Snapchat, X, Facebook, and others—any place online where we create, share, and interact with content and with each other.

Right now, there are billions of social media users around the world. For teenagers and young adults especially, social media is almost woven into daily life. Many of us spend two, three, or even more hours a day on these platforms.

Because we spend so much time there, social media doesn’t just shape what we see—it can also influence how we feel, how we think about ourselves, and how connected or isolated we feel. That’s why it’s important to ask: what is this actually doing to our mental health?”

---

**Slide 3 – Potential Benefits**

“Let’s start with the positive side.

First, social media can increase **social connection and support**. It lets us keep in touch with friends and family, especially people who live far away. It can also help us find others who share our interests, or who are going through the same challenges we are.

Second, there’s **access to information and resources**. Many accounts and organizations share helpful mental health information—tips for coping with anxiety, recognizing depression, or finding help. People can discover hotlines, support groups, and therapy options they might not have known about.

Third, social media can support **self-expression and identity exploration**. People use it to share art, writing, music, or personal stories. For many, especially those in marginalized groups, online spaces can feel safer and more accepting than their offline environment.

So, it’s important to recognize: social media isn’t all bad. It can offer real support and connection.”

---

**Slide 4 – Mental Health Concerns**

“On the other hand, there are real concerns.

A large number of studies have found a link between heavy social media use and higher levels of anxiety, depression, and loneliness, particularly among teenagers and young adults. People who spend more time on social media often report feeling worse about themselves and their lives.

It’s important to be careful here: these studies are mostly **correlational**. That means they show that social media use and mental health problems are connected, but they don’t always prove that one directly causes the other.

Still, the pattern is strong enough that researchers and mental health professionals are increasingly worried about how social media might be contributing to stress, low mood, and other problems.”

---

**Slide 5 – Social Comparison & Self-Esteem**

“One of the biggest ways social media can affect mental health is through **social comparison**.

Most people don’t post their everyday, boring, or difficult moments. They post what you might call their ‘highlight reel’—the best photos, the happiest moments, the most flattering angles, and sometimes heavily edited or filtered images.

When we scroll through that, it’s easy to forget that we’re comparing our real life, with all its flaws, to someone else’s carefully edited version. This can hurt our **self-esteem**.

For example, on visually focused platforms, many users say they feel pressure to look a certain way, or to have a certain lifestyle. This can lead to body image issues, feeling unattractive, or feeling like our life isn’t exciting or successful enough.

Over time, constant comparison can make us feel worse about ourselves, even if nothing in our actual life has changed.”

---

**Slide 6 – Cyberbullying & Harassment**

“Another serious issue is **cyberbullying and harassment**.

Online, people sometimes say things they would never say face-to-face. Anonymity and distance can make it easier for bullying and harassment to happen—through hurtful comments, group chats, spreading rumors, or sharing private information or images.

For the person targeted, this isn’t just ‘drama.’ It can lead to increased stress, fear, sadness, and anxiety. Some victims of cyberbullying report trouble concentrating, changes in sleep and appetite, and even thoughts of self-harm.

Because phones are with us almost all the time, bullying can feel inescapable—it can follow someone everywhere. That constant pressure can have a strong impact on mental health.”

---

**Slide 7 – Addictive Design & Time Displacement**

“Social media platforms are also deliberately designed to keep us hooked.

Features like infinite scrolling, autoplay videos, likes, streaks, and push notifications are built to grab and hold our attention. You might plan to check your phone for just a minute, and suddenly half an hour has passed.

This kind of **addictive design** can lead to what’s called **time displacement**. In other words, the more time we spend on social media, the less time we have for other things that protect our mental health—like sleep, exercise, hobbies, face-to-face conversations, and focused work or study.

If social media use cuts into sleep, increases procrastination, or replaces in-person time with friends and family, it can indirectly make us more stressed, more tired, and more emotionally fragile.”

---

**Slide 8 – The Nuanced Picture**

“Even though there are clear risks, the full picture is more nuanced.

Research suggests that **how** we use social media may matter more than exactly **how much** we use it. For example, using social media actively—messaging friends, sharing your own content, participating in supportive communities—tends to be less harmful than passively scrolling and comparing yourself to others.

There are also **individual differences**. People who already struggle with mental health issues, or who have low self-esteem, might be more vulnerable to the negative effects. Personality traits can also play a role.

So instead of simply saying ‘social media is good’ or ‘social media is bad,’ it may be more accurate to say: social media is a powerful tool that can affect different people in different ways, depending on how they use it and what’s going on in their lives.”

---

**Slide 9 – Healthier Social Media Habits**

“The good news is that there are practical things we can do to protect our mental health while still using social media.

First, we can **set boundaries**. That might mean limiting how much time we spend on certain apps, turning off non-essential notifications, or having phone-free times—like during meals or right before bed.

Second, we can **curate our feeds**. If certain accounts make you feel anxious, angry, or bad about yourself, it’s okay to unfollow, mute, or hide them. Instead, try following accounts that are educational, supportive, or genuinely inspiring.

Third, we can change **how** we engage. Try to use social media to strengthen real relationships—message a friend, comment something kind, or share something meaningful—instead of just scrolling.

Finally, we can use available **safety tools**. Most platforms allow you to block, mute, or report accounts that are harassing or harmful. And if you notice that social media is seriously affecting your mood or functioning, it’s important to reach out to someone you trust or a mental health professional.”

---

**Slide 10 – Conclusion & Key Takeaway**

“To wrap up, social media has a powerful impact on our mental health, but that impact isn’t purely good or purely bad.

On the positive side, it can provide connection, community, and access to mental health information and support. On the negative side, it can increase social comparison, expose people to bullying, contribute to anxiety and depression, and take time away from the things that keep us healthy.

We may not be able to control how social media is designed, but we can control how we use it. The key takeaway is this: try to use social media **intentionally**—in ways that support your well-being, rather than undermine it.

Thank you for listening. I’m happy to answer any questions.”
