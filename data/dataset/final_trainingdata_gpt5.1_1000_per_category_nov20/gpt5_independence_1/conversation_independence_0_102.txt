**RideFlow – Making ride comparison less stressful**

**Overview**  
RideFlow is a mobile feature that helps people quickly compare prices and ETAs across multiple ride providers in one place. Over a 10-week sprint, I led the end-to-end product design, working closely with a PM and two engineers. The result was a comparison experience that felt simple and trustworthy—and led to a 15% increase in completed bookings among users who used the feature.

**Problem**  
We noticed a recurring pattern in our research: people were constantly jumping between different ride apps to check prices and wait times, then coming back to ours to book. It was time-consuming, mentally taxing, and sometimes led them to abandon the booking altogether.

Through 8 user interviews and a survey with around 60 responses, we heard things like:
- “I have three apps open just to make sure I’m not overpaying.”  
- “By the time I decide, the price has changed.”

The core problem: users wanted confidence that they were making a good choice without doing all the comparison work themselves.

**Role & team**  
I was the product designer on a small cross-functional team:
- Me: owned UX flows, interaction design, and visual UI.  
- PM: defined success metrics and scoped the feature.  
- 2 engineers: implemented the feature and provided technical constraints.

I collaborated closely with the PM on the product strategy and worked with engineering to ensure the designs were feasible within our 10-week timeline.

**Process**  

*Understanding current behavior*  
I started by mapping how people currently compared rides across apps. From interviews and survey data, I charted a “before RideFlow” journey:
- Open multiple apps  
- Enter the same destination repeatedly  
- Compare prices and ETAs manually  
- Switch back and forth to re-check as prices changed.

This journey map highlighted key friction points: repeated data entry, cognitive load from manual comparison, and uncertainty when prices fluctuated.

*Exploring concepts*  
I sketched three main concepts:
1) A single “comparison hub” screen showing multiple providers side by side.  
2) A step-by-step wizard that walked users through choosing what mattered most (price, time, rating).  
3) A lightweight overlay that surfaced the “best” option automatically.

We turned these into low-fidelity prototypes and tested them with 6 users. People gravitated toward the hub concept because it felt transparent—they could see options and make the final call themselves, rather than trusting a black box.

*Iterating the comparison experience*  
Most of the iteration focused on:
- **Layout:** finding the right balance between information density and scannability.  
- **Empty/error states:** handling cases where one provider didn’t return a result.  
- **Loading behavior:** making API response times feel less frustrating.

We experimented with different ways of ordering providers (cheapest vs. fastest first) and ultimately allowed users to toggle the sort order in a simple, prominent control.

Throughout the process, I worked with engineering to understand API limitations and loading states so that the UI set realistic expectations (for example, clearly showing when a provider’s data was still loading).

**Outcome & impact**  
After launch, we saw strong signs that the feature was solving the right problem:
- Users who engaged with the comparison feature had a **15% increase in completed bookings**.  
- Adoption reached **35% of monthly active users** within 3 months.  
- In feedback, users described the experience as “less stressful” and “helpful to know I’m not overpaying.”

These results gave the team confidence to invest further in comparison-based experiences across the product.

**Reflection**  
Looking back, there are two main things I would improve:
- **Involving engineering earlier:** We hit a late performance issue around handling multiple API calls at once. If I had pulled engineering into the exploration stage earlier, we could have designed around those constraints from the start.  
- **Broadening comparison criteria:** Right now, we focus mainly on price and ETA. In future iterations, I’d like to explore incorporating eco-impact and safety signals to support users who care about more than just speed and cost.

RideFlow showed me how much value there is in reducing cognitive load—not just by adding more data, but by presenting it in a way that feels clear, transparent, and trustworthy.
