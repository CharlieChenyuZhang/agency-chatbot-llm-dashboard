**1 Introduction**

Pre-trained language models have become the de facto foundation for natural language processing (NLP), enabling strong performance on a wide range of tasks via fine-tuning on relatively small labeled datasets. Despite this progress, fine-tuning in genuinely low-resource settings remains challenging: performance often degrades sharply when labeled data is scarce, and models can be brittle under distribution shifts or minor input perturbations. These limitations are especially problematic in domains where annotation is expensive or sensitive, such as healthcare, legal text, and low-resource languages.

A central difficulty in low-resource fine-tuning is overfitting. When only a handful of labeled examples are available, standard supervised objectives provide weak constraints on the model’s behavior, allowing it to memorize idiosyncrasies of the training set rather than learn robust patterns. Common regularization techniques such as weight decay, dropout, and early stopping partially mitigate this issue, but they do not directly encourage the model to behave consistently under natural variations of the input that should not change its label.

In contrast, consistency-based regularization methods aim to explicitly enforce stability of model predictions under input perturbations. Originally popularized in semi-supervised learning, these approaches encourage a model to produce similar outputs for augmented views of the same input, thereby leveraging unlabeled data or augmentations as an additional source of training signal. While consistency regularization has shown promise in computer vision and semi-supervised classification, its application to low-resource fine-tuning of pre-trained language models remains relatively underexplored and fragmented.

In this work, we investigate a simple question: *Can a lightweight consistency-based regularization strategy substantially improve the robustness and data efficiency of medium-scale language models in low-resource text classification?* To answer this, we propose a straightforward method that builds directly on standard supervised fine-tuning. For each labeled example, we generate one or more stochastically augmented variants of the input using inexpensive text transformations. We then train the model with a combined objective that includes (1) the usual supervised loss on the original labeled examples and (2) a consistency loss that penalizes discrepancies between the model’s predictive distributions on the original and augmented inputs. This design requires no changes to the underlying model architecture and introduces only a small computational overhead.

We conduct an empirical study across multiple low-resource benchmarks and data regimes. Concretely, we fine-tune a medium-scale pre-trained language model on [N] text classification datasets using small labeled subsets spanning from [X] to [Y] examples per class. We compare our method to standard fine-tuning baselines and commonly used regularization techniques. Our results show that the proposed consistency-based regularization consistently improves accuracy by 2–5 points over strong baselines in the smallest data regimes, while also yielding more stable performance under synthetic text perturbations that mimic common variations such as synonym replacements, spelling noise, and paraphrasing.

To better understand the source of these gains, we perform a set of ablation studies. We examine the role of augmentation strength, the weighting of the consistency loss, and the interaction with standard regularizers such as weight decay and dropout. We find that both the augmentations and the consistency term are necessary to obtain robust improvements, and that our method provides complementary benefits to conventional regularization. Additionally, we analyze the calibration and confidence of the resulting models, observing that consistency-regularized models tend to be less overconfident on perturbed examples.

Our contributions are threefold:
1. **A simple, general method for consistency-based regularization in low-resource fine-tuning.** We introduce an easily implemented training objective that augments standard supervised fine-tuning with a consistency loss between original and augmented inputs, without modifying the model architecture.
2. **An empirical evaluation across multiple low-resource benchmarks and robustness settings.** We demonstrate that the proposed method yields consistent gains in accuracy and robustness for medium-scale language models across diverse datasets and data regimes.
3. **An ablation-driven analysis of design choices.** We systematically study the impact of augmentation strategies, consistency weighting, and interactions with standard regularization techniques, providing practical guidance for applying consistency regularization in low-resource NLP scenarios.

Taken together, our findings indicate that simple consistency-based regularization is a practical and effective tool for improving the reliability of language models when labeled data is limited. We release our code and experimental configurations to facilitate further research and adoption of these techniques.

**4 Method**

We propose a simple consistency-based regularization approach for fine-tuning pre-trained language models in low-resource text classification. Our method augments the standard supervised objective with a consistency loss that encourages the model to produce similar predictions for original labeled inputs and their stochastically augmented variants.

#### 4.1 Problem Setup

Let \(\mathcal{D} = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^N\) denote a labeled dataset, where \(x_i\) is a text input (e.g., a sentence or document) and \(y_i \in \{1, \dots, C\}\) is a class label among \(C\) possible classes. We assume \(N\) is small (e.g., tens to a few hundreds of labeled examples per class), corresponding to a low-resource setting.

We consider a pre-trained language model \(f_\theta\) parameterized by \(\theta\), which maps an input text \(x\) to a probability distribution over classes:
\[
p_\theta(y \mid x) = f_\theta(x).
\]
In standard supervised fine-tuning, \(\theta\) is optimized to minimize the average cross-entropy loss on \(\mathcal{D}\):
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{sup}}(\theta) = - \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \log p_\theta(y_i \mid x_i).
\]

#### 4.2 Consistency-Based Regularization

To improve robustness and data efficiency, we introduce a consistency loss that penalizes discrepancies between the model’s predictions on original inputs and their augmented counterparts.

Let \(\text{Aug}(\cdot)\) denote a stochastic text augmentation function. Given an input \(x_i\), we generate an augmented example \(\tilde{x}_i = \text{Aug}(x_i)\). We then compute the model’s predictive distributions:
\[
p_i = p_\theta(\cdot \mid x_i), \quad \tilde{p}_i = p_\theta(\cdot \mid \tilde{x}_i).
\]

We define the consistency loss as the average divergence between \(p_i\) and \(\tilde{p}_i\) across the training set:
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{cons}}(\theta) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N D\big(p_\theta(\cdot \mid x_i) \,\|\, p_\theta(\cdot \mid \tilde{x}_i)\big),
\]
where \(D(\cdot\|\cdot)\) is a divergence measure. In our implementation, we use the symmetric Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence:
\[
D_{\text{sym}}(p \,\|\, q) = \frac{1}{2} \big( \text{KL}(p \,\|\, q) + \text{KL}(q \,\|\, p) \big),
\]
with
\[
\text{KL}(p \,\|\, q) = \sum_{c=1}^C p_c \log \frac{p_c}{q_c}.
\]

Thus, the consistency loss becomes:
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{cons}}(\theta) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N D_{\text{sym}}\big(p_\theta(\cdot \mid x_i),\, p_\theta(\cdot \mid \tilde{x}_i)\big).
\]

#### 4.3 Combined Training Objective

Our final training objective combines the supervised loss and the consistency loss:
\[
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathcal{L}_{\text{sup}}(\theta) + \lambda \, \mathcal{L}_{\text{cons}}(\theta),
\]
where \(\lambda \ge 0\) controls the strength of consistency regularization.

In practice, we compute the losses at the minibatch level. For a minibatch \(\mathcal{B} = \{(x_j, y_j)\}_{j=1}^B\), we form augmented inputs \(\tilde{x}_j = \text{Aug}(x_j)\) and compute:
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{sup}}^{(\mathcal{B})} = - \frac{1}{B} \sum_{j=1}^B \log p_\theta(y_j \mid x_j),
\]
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{cons}}^{(\mathcal{B})} = \frac{1}{B} \sum_{j=1}^B D_{\text{sym}}\big(p_\theta(\cdot \mid x_j),\, p_\theta(\cdot \mid \tilde{x}_j)\big),
\]
\[
\mathcal{L}^{(\mathcal{B})} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{sup}}^{(\mathcal{B})} + \lambda \, \mathcal{L}_{\text{cons}}^{(\mathcal{B})}.
\]

We then perform stochastic gradient descent (or AdamW) steps to minimize \(\mathcal{L}^{(\mathcal{B})}\).

#### 4.4 Text Augmentation Strategy

We use simple, label-preserving text augmentations that are inexpensive to apply during training. Concretely, we use a combination of:

1. **Synonym replacement**  
   - Randomly select a small fraction (e.g., 10–15%) of non-stopword tokens.  
   - Replace each selected token with a synonym sampled from a lexical resource (e.g., WordNet) or a precomputed embedding-based neighbor.

2. **Random deletion**  
   - Independently delete each token with a small probability (e.g., 5–10%), avoiding deletion of all tokens.

3. **Random swap**  
   - Randomly select pairs of tokens and swap their positions with low probability.

We compose these transformations with low intensities to maintain semantic equivalence and label invariance. In practice, for each input \(x\), we sample one augmentation strategy at random and apply it with fixed hyperparameters tuned on a validation set.

For stronger experiments (optional), you can include:
- **Back-translation** (e.g., English → German → English using a public MT model).  
- **Paraphrasing** via a pre-trained paraphrase model.

However, to keep the method practical and fast, our main experiments rely only on the simple, local augmentations above.

We instantiate \(f_\theta\) as a medium-scale Transformer-based language model:

- Base model: a pre-trained encoder-only model (e.g., `bert-base-uncased`) or encoder-decoder model suitable for classification.
- Classification head: a linear layer on top of the [CLS] token (or pooled representation), mapping to \(C\) classes.

**Implementation details:**

- **Initialization:**  
  - Load pre-trained weights from Hugging Face (e.g., `bert-base-uncased`).  
  - Initialize a randomly-initialized classification head.

- **Optimizer and schedule:**  
  - Use AdamW optimizer with learning rate \(1\text{e-}5\) to \(3\text{e-}5\), weight decay 0.01.  
  - Linear learning rate warmup over the first 10% of training steps, followed by linear decay.

- **Batching and epochs:**  
  - Batch size: 16 or 32 (depending on GPU memory).  
  - Train for 10–20 epochs in the low-resource regime, with early stopping on validation performance.

- **Consistency weight \(\lambda\):**  
  - Tune \(\lambda\) in \(\{0.1, 0.5, 1.0\}\).

- **Stability tricks:**  
  - Apply label smoothing (e.g., 0.1) to the supervised loss if overfitting is severe.  
  - Gradients are clipped to a maximum norm (e.g., 1.0).  
  - Dropout rate: 0.1–0.3 in the classification head.

**Algorithm 1** below summarizes the training procedure.

**Algorithm 1: Consistency-Regularized Fine-Tuning**

1. Initialize model parameters \(\theta\) from a pre-trained checkpoint; initialize classification head.  
2. Repeat for each training epoch:  
   a. For each minibatch \(\mathcal{B} = \{(x_j, y_j)\}_{j=1}^B\):  
   &emsp;i. Sample augmented texts \(\tilde{x}_j = \text{Aug}(x_j)\) for all \(j\).  
   &emsp;ii. Compute \(p_\theta(\cdot \mid x_j)\) and \(p_\theta(\cdot \mid \tilde{x}_j)\).  
   &emsp;iii. Compute \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{sup}}^{(\mathcal{B})}\) and \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{cons}}^{(\mathcal{B})}\).  
   &emsp;iv. Form \(\mathcal{L}^{(\mathcal{B})} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{sup}}^{(\mathcal{B})} + \lambda \mathcal{L}_{\text{cons}}^{(\mathcal{B})}\).  
   &emsp;v. Update \(\theta\) using the gradient \(\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}^{(\mathcal{B})}\).  
3. Select the best checkpoint based on validation performance.  
4. Evaluate on the test set without augmentations (standard inference).

This method can be implemented by minimally modifying an existing supervised fine-tuning loop: the only required additions are (1) generating augmented texts on-the-fly and (2) computing the additional consistency loss term.
