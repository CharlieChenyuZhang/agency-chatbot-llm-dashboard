**1. Introduction (Draft)**

Large language models (LLMs) have rapidly become the backbone of modern natural language processing, achieving strong performance across a wide range of tasks including text classification, question answering, and natural language inference. As model sizes have grown into the billions of parameters, however, fully fine-tuning all weights for each downstream task has become increasingly impractical. The computational and memory requirements of full fine-tuning pose substantial barriers for both academic and industrial practitioners, and the resulting models are often brittle when deployed under distribution shifts that differ from the training data.

To address these challenges, a growing body of work on parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) aims to adapt large pretrained models by introducing a small number of additional parameters while keeping the original weights frozen. Techniques such as adapters, prefix tuning, and low-rank adaptation (LoRA) have demonstrated that strong downstream performance can often be achieved by modifying only a tiny fraction of the model’s parameters. This line of research has made fine-tuning more accessible and has enabled sharing of compact task-specific parameter sets rather than entire models.

Despite these advances, existing PEFT approaches typically adapt all layers of the underlying model in a uniform manner. In practice, not all layers contribute equally to task adaptation: empirical analyses suggest that certain intermediate or upper layers are particularly important for encoding task-relevant features, while others can often remain unchanged without harming performance. Moreover, most PEFT methods have been developed and evaluated primarily in in-domain settings, with relatively limited attention to robustness under distribution shifts. As a result, there is a gap between the efficiency of PEFT methods and the robustness and flexibility required in real-world deployment scenarios.

In this work, we propose **Layer-Selective Adapters (LSA)**, a parameter-efficient fine-tuning framework that explicitly learns *which* layers of a large language model should be adapted for a given downstream task, while simultaneously encouraging robustness to input perturbations and distribution shifts. LSA augments a frozen pretrained transformer with lightweight adapters inserted into each layer, together with a learnable gating mechanism that assigns a continuous importance score to every adapter. These gates modulate the contribution of each adapter to the model’s hidden representations and are regularized to induce sparsity, leading the model to rely on a small subset of adapted layers. To improve robustness, LSA incorporates a simple consistency-based regularization term that encourages stable predictions under small input perturbations.

We conduct extensive experiments on a diverse set of NLP benchmarks, including sentiment analysis, natural language inference, and topic classification. Across these tasks, LSA consistently matches or improves upon the performance of full fine-tuning while training at most a small fraction of the parameters, and it outperforms strong PEFT baselines such as adapters and LoRA in both in-domain and out-of-domain evaluation settings. Our analysis of the learned gating patterns reveals that LSA tends to select a compact set of mid-to-upper transformer layers for adaptation, providing insights into where task-specific information is integrated within large language models.

Our contributions are threefold:
1. We introduce **Layer-Selective Adapters (LSA)**, a parameter-efficient fine-tuning framework that learns to adapt only a sparse subset of transformer layers via a simple, learnable gating mechanism.
2. We propose a lightweight robustness regularization strategy that promotes prediction consistency under input perturbations, improving performance under distribution shift with minimal overhead.
3. We provide a comprehensive empirical study across multiple NLP tasks, demonstrating that LSA achieves strong accuracy with substantially fewer trainable parameters than full fine-tuning and yields more robust performance than existing PEFT baselines, along with an analysis of the emergent layer-selection behavior.

Together, these results suggest that selectively adapting a small number of layers, rather than uniformly modifying the entire model, is a promising direction for building efficient and robust fine-tuning strategies for large language models.

**4. Method (Draft)**

#### 4.1 Problem Setup

We consider a large pretrained language model \( f_{\theta_0} \) with parameters \( \theta_0 \), such as a transformer-based decoder or encoder–decoder model. Given a downstream supervised task with input–label pairs \( (x, y) \sim \mathcal{D} \), our goal is to adapt \( f_{\theta_0} \) to minimize the expected task loss
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{task}}(\phi) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}} \big[ \ell(f_{\theta_0, \phi}(x), y) \big],
\]
where \( \phi \) denotes a small set of task-specific parameters, \( f_{\theta_0,\phi} \) is the adapted model, and \( \ell \) is typically the cross-entropy loss.

In full fine-tuning, one optimizes all parameters \( \theta_0 \) for each task, which is computationally expensive and memory-intensive for large models. In contrast, parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) keeps \( \theta_0 \) frozen and introduces a small number of additional parameters \( \phi \) that modulate the model’s behavior.

We aim to design a PEFT method that:
1. Uses significantly fewer trainable parameters than full fine-tuning.
2. Learns to adapt only a subset of layers, rather than all layers uniformly.
3. Improves robustness to input perturbations and distribution shift.

#### 4.2 Layer-Selective Adapters

We adopt a standard transformer architecture with \( L \) layers. Let \( h_\ell \in \mathbb{R}^d \) denote the hidden representation at layer \( \ell \in \{1, \dots, L\} \) for a given token (or aggregate representation such as [CLS]). In a typical adapter-based PEFT method, each layer is augmented with a small bottleneck module \( A_\ell \) that transforms \( h_\ell \) and adds the result back to the hidden state:
\[
\tilde{h}_\ell \;=\; h_\ell + A_\ell(h_\ell),
\]
where \( A_\ell \) is parameterized by a down-projection, nonlinearity, and up-projection:
\[
A_\ell(h_\ell) \;=\; W^{\text{up}}_\ell \, \sigma\!\big( W^{\text{down}}_\ell h_\ell \big),
\]
with \( W^{\text{down}}_\ell \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times d} \), \( W^{\text{up}}_\ell \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r} \), bottleneck dimension \( r \ll d \), and nonlinearity \( \sigma \) (e.g., ReLU or GELU). The base model parameters \( \theta_0 \) remain frozen, and only adapter parameters are trained.

**Layer-Selective Gating.**  
Instead of activating all adapters uniformly, we introduce a learnable *gate* \( g_\ell \in [0, 1] \) for each layer \( \ell \). The adapted representation becomes
\[
\tilde{h}_\ell \;=\; h_\ell + g_\ell \cdot A_\ell(h_\ell).
\]
Collecting all gates into a vector \( g = (g_1, \dots, g_L) \in [0, 1]^L \), we parametrize \( g \) via unconstrained logits \( \alpha \in \mathbb{R}^L \) and a sigmoid function:
\[
g_\ell \;=\; \sigma(\alpha_\ell) \;=\; \frac{1}{1 + e^{-\alpha_\ell}}.
\]
The gates \( g \) are shared across tokens and inputs for a given task and are learned jointly with the adapter parameters.

**Sparsity Regularization.**  
To encourage adaptation of only a sparse subset of layers, we impose an \(\ell_1\) penalty on the gates:
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{sparse}}(g) \;=\; \| g \|_1 \;=\; \sum_{\ell=1}^L |g_\ell|.
\]
Minimizing \( \mathcal{L}_{\text{sparse}} \) drives many gates \( g_\ell \) toward zero, effectively disabling the corresponding adapters and leaving the underlying layer essentially frozen.

The trainable parameters for a given task are therefore:
\[
\phi \;=\; \{ \{ W^{\text{down}}_\ell, W^{\text{up}}_\ell \}_{\ell=1}^L, \, \alpha \},
\]
while \( \theta_0 \) remains fixed.

#### 4.3 Robustness Regularization via Consistency

To improve robustness under distribution shifts and small input perturbations, we adopt a consistency-based regularization scheme. For each training example \( (x, y) \), we construct a perturbed input \( \tilde{x} \) using simple text augmentations, such as:
- Random token masking or dropout.
- Synonym replacement from a predefined dictionary or embedding neighbors.
- Small perturbations to word order (e.g., swapping non-critical adjacent tokens).

Let \( p_{\phi}(y \mid x) \) denote the model’s predictive distribution for input \( x \). We encourage consistency between predictions on \( x \) and its perturbed version \( \tilde{x} \) by minimizing the symmetrized Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence:
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{robust}}(x, \tilde{x}) \;=\; \mathrm{KL}\big( p_{\phi}(y \mid x) \,\big\|\, p_{\phi}(y \mid \tilde{x}) \big)
\,+\, \mathrm{KL}\big( p_{\phi}(y \mid \tilde{x}) \,\big\|\, p_{\phi}(y \mid x) \big).
\]
In practice, we approximate \( p_{\phi}(y \mid x) \) and \( p_{\phi}(y \mid \tilde{x}) \) using the model’s softmax outputs over the label space for both the original and perturbed inputs.

#### 4.4 Overall Training Objective

Combining the supervised task loss, sparsity regularization, and robustness term, our overall objective for a batch \( \mathcal{B} = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^N \) is:
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}(\phi) \;=\; \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \Big[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{task}}(x_i, y_i) \,+\, \lambda_{\text{robust}} \, \mathcal{L}_{\text{robust}}(x_i, \tilde{x}_i) \Big]
\,+\, \lambda_{\text{sparse}} \, \mathcal{L}_{\text{sparse}}(g),
\]
where
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{task}}(x_i, y_i) \;=\; - \log p_{\phi}(y_i \mid x_i),
\]
\( \tilde{x}_i \) is the perturbed version of \( x_i \), and \( \lambda_{\text{robust}}, \lambda_{\text{sparse}} \geq 0 \) are hyperparameters controlling the trade-off between predictive accuracy, robustness, and sparsity.

During training, we:
1. Sample a mini-batch \( \{(x_i, y_i)\} \).
2. Generate perturbations \( \tilde{x}_i \) for each \( x_i \).
3. Compute predictions \( p_{\phi}(y \mid x_i) \) and \( p_{\phi}(y \mid \tilde{x}_i) \).
4. Evaluate \( \mathcal{L}_{\text{task}} \), \( \mathcal{L}_{\text{robust}} \), and \( \mathcal{L}_{\text{sparse}} \).
5. Update \( \phi \) via backpropagation and an optimizer such as AdamW.

#### 4.5 Implementation Details

We now describe concrete implementation choices that make LSA straightforward to integrate with existing transformer models.

**Adapter Placement.**  
We insert adapters in each transformer layer after the feed-forward sub-layer (and optionally after the attention sub-layer). For a standard transformer block receiving input \( h_\ell \), with attention module \( \text{Attn} \) and feed-forward network \( \text{FFN} \), we use:
\[
u_\ell = \text{Attn}(h_\ell) + h_\ell, \quad
v_\ell = \text{FFN}(u_\ell) + u_\ell,
\]
\[
\tilde{h}_\ell = v_\ell + g_\ell \cdot A_\ell(v_\ell).
\]
This requires only minor modifications to existing implementations of transformer blocks.

**Parameter Budget.**  
We typically set the adapter bottleneck dimension \( r \) such that the total number of adapter parameters constitutes at most a few percent of the base model parameters. The gates \( \alpha \in \mathbb{R}^L \) add negligible overhead.

**Training Strategy.**  
We initialize adapters and gates to have minimal impact at the start of training. Concretely:
- Adapter weights are initialized near zero so that \( A_\ell(h_\ell) \approx 0 \) initially.
- Gate logits \( \alpha_\ell \) are initialized such that \( g_\ell = \sigma(\alpha_\ell) \approx g_0 \) for a small constant (e.g., 0.2–0.5), allowing the model to discover whether to increase or decrease each gate.

We train with a standard optimizer (e.g., AdamW) using task-specific learning rates and batch sizes. We typically apply weight decay only to adapter weights, not to gate logits.

**Inference.**  
At inference time, we use the learned gates \( g \) as-is. Optionally, we can *hard-prune* adapters whose gates fall below a small threshold \( \tau \), setting their contributions to zero and reducing computation:
\[
g_\ell^{\text{pruned}} \;=\;
\begin{cases}
0, & \text{if } g_\ell < \tau,\\
g_\ell, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\]
This provides an additional reduction in inference cost without retraining.

**3. Related Work (Draft)**

*Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning.*  
As large language models have grown in size, parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) has emerged as a practical alternative to full model adaptation. Early approaches introduced lightweight modules, or *adapters*, into each transformer layer, enabling task-specific adaptation with a small fraction of the original parameters. Subsequent work proposed alternative mechanisms such as prefix tuning and prompt tuning, which modify the model’s behavior by prepending or learning continuous prompts at the input or intermediate layers. Low-rank adaptation (LoRA) further reduced the parameter footprint by learning low-rank updates to existing weight matrices, achieving performance comparable to full fine-tuning on many tasks.

These methods have shown that strong downstream performance is attainable without updating all model parameters. However, they generally adapt all layers in a uniform, task-agnostic manner: adapters or low-rank updates are inserted at every layer, and the model is not explicitly encouraged to select which parts of the network to modify. In contrast, our Layer-Selective Adapters framework learns a sparse pattern of layer-wise adaptation, allowing the model to focus its limited capacity on the most relevant layers for each task.

*Layer-Wise Analysis and Selective Fine-Tuning.*  
A complementary line of work has examined the roles of different layers in pretrained transformers, finding that various linguistic and task-specific features tend to emerge at different depths. Building on these observations, some methods have proposed freezing subsets of layers, fine-tuning only higher layers, or gradually unfreezing layers during training. While these strategies can reduce computation, they typically rely on fixed heuristics or manual choices about which layers to adapt. Our approach instead learns layer selection automatically through continuous gates and sparsity regularization, enabling data-driven discovery of task-specific adaptation patterns.

*Robustness in Fine-Tuning.*  
Fine-tuned models are often brittle when evaluated under distribution shift, adversarial perturbations, or noisy inputs. Robustness-enhancing methods include data augmentation, adversarial training, and consistency-based regularization. In the context of LLMs, recent work has explored robustness under prompt variations, domain shifts, and corrupted inputs, but robustness-aware PEFT methods remain relatively underexplored. Our method incorporates a simple consistency-based robustness term into the PEFT framework, encouraging stable predictions across perturbed inputs while maintaining parameter efficiency. This combination of layer-selective adaptation and robustness regularization leads to improved performance under both in-domain and shifted evaluation conditions.

Overall, LSA integrates ideas from parameter-efficient adaptation, layer-wise analysis, and robustness into a unified framework that selectively adapts a small number of layers while promoting robust behavior.
