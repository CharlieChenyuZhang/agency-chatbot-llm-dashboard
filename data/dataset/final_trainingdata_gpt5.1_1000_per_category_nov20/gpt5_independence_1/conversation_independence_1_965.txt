### Human: Plan a study sprint with spaced repetition

### Assistant: I’ll set up a 2‑week focused study sprint using spaced repetition, assuming you can study about 2 hours per day on weekdays and 3 hours per day on weekends. I’ll use a generic “content-heavy” subject (like biology or history), but you can plug in any topic.

**Overall structure (2-week sprint)**  
- Goal: Rapidly learn and retain ~40–60 key concepts.  
- Method:  
  - Daily new learning + review using spaced intervals.  
  - Short, intense blocks with breaks.  
  - Mix of active recall (flashcards) and quick practice/application.

---

#### Daily time template (Weekdays: ~2 hours)

1. **Block 1 – New Learning (40 min)**
   - Learn 4–6 new concepts.  
   - For each concept: brief explanation in your own words + 1–3 example questions.

2. **Break (10 min)**  
   - Move, stretch, no screens if possible.

3. **Block 2 – Flashcard Creation & First Review (40 min)**  
   - Turn today’s new concepts into flashcards (Q/A style).  
   - Immediately run through them once (first exposure).  
   - Use an app with spaced repetition (e.g., Anki, RemNote, or Quizlet with custom intervals).

4. **Break (10 min)**

5. **Block 3 – Spaced Review (20 min)**  
   - Review:
     - Cards from **yesterday** (1-day interval).  
     - Any cards the app marks as “due” (older ones on longer intervals).

---

#### Daily time template (Weekends: ~3 hours)

1. **Block 1 – New Learning (50 min)**  
   - Learn 6–8 new concepts. Summarize them yourself.

2. **Break (10 min)**

3. **Block 2 – Flashcards & First Review (40 min)**  
   - Create flashcards for all new concepts.  
   - Do the first pass of all new cards.

4. **Break (10 min)**

5. **Block 3 – Extended Review + Application (60 min)**  
   - Spaced review of all due cards.  
   - 20–30 min: quick practice problems, mini-essays, or explaining topics out loud from memory.

---

#### Spaced repetition schedule for each new card

When you first learn a concept (Day 0):

- **Day 0:** Learn + immediate test (in the same session).  
- **Day 1:** Review once.  
- **Day 3:** Review again.  
- **Day 7:** Review again.  
- **Day 14:** Review again.

In an SRS app (like Anki), this roughly corresponds to:
- “Again” for failed cards (they reappear quickly in the same session).  
- “Good” to push intervals to ~1 day, then 3 days, then 7+, etc.

If you miss a card or find it difficult:
- Mark it as “hard” so the app shortens the interval.  
- Add a second, simpler card that isolates the confusing piece.

---

#### 2-week plan (high level)

**Week 1**
- **Day 1–3:**  
  - Learn 4–6 new concepts/day.  
  - Create cards the same day and begin reviews.
- **Day 4–5:**  
  - Maintain the same pace.  
  - You’ll now be reviewing material from Days 1–3 as well.
- **Day 6–7 (weekend):**  
  - Slightly more new material (6–8 concepts each day).  
  - 60+ min of reviews to reinforce everything from Days 1–5.

**Week 2**
- **Day 8–10:**  
  - Slightly reduce new content (4–5 concepts/day).  
  - Priority: reviews. Don’t skip them. If time is tight, skip new content, not reviews.
- **Day 11–12:**  
  - Mostly consolidation: fewer new cards, more review.  
  - Add summary notes or mind maps from memory, then check your cards/notes to fill gaps.
- **Day 13–14 (weekend):**  
  - Final review sweep of all cards due.  
  - Self-test:
    - Timed quiz or practice test from memory.  
    - Teach the main topics out loud without notes.

---

#### Card design guidelines (to maximize retention)

- 1 fact/concept per card.  
- Use **question → answer** or **cloze deletions** (fill in the blank).  
- Avoid copying textbook sentences verbatim; rewrite in your own language.  
- Example types:
  - Definition: “What is X?”  
  - Concept link: “How does X relate to Y?”  
  - Application: “Given this scenario, which concept applies?”

---

#### Daily checklist (to keep it simple)

1. Open your SRS app.  
2. **Do all due reviews first.**  
3. Learn new material for today (using the time blocks above).  
4. Create cards for new material.  
5. End with a fast run-through of the hardest cards.

You can now plug in the specific subject and chapter goals, and this template stays the same.

### Human: Can you also create a small starter deck of 20 Day-1 biology flashcards I can directly enter into Anki?

### Assistant: I’ll create 20 concise Q/A-style cards for Day 1 on cell theory, cell types, and major organelles. You can paste these directly into Anki (front tab back).

1. Front: What are the three main principles of cell theory?  
   Back: (1) All living things are composed of cells. (2) Cells are the basic unit of structure and function in living things. (3) All cells come from pre-existing cells.

2. Front: Who are the three scientists most associated with formulating cell theory?  
   Back: Schleiden, Schwann, and Virchow.

3. Front: State one key contribution of Schleiden to cell theory.  
   Back: Schleiden proposed that all plants are made of cells.

4. Front: State one key contribution of Schwann to cell theory.  
   Back: Schwann proposed that all animals are made of cells.

5. Front: State one key contribution of Virchow to cell theory.  
   Back: Virchow stated that all cells come from pre-existing cells.

6. Front: Define a prokaryotic cell.  
   Back: A cell without a true nucleus or membrane-bound organelles; DNA is in a nucleoid region.

7. Front: Define a eukaryotic cell.  
   Back: A cell that has a true nucleus and membrane-bound organelles.

8. Front: Give two differences between prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells.  
   Back: Prokaryotes lack a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles; eukaryotes have both.

9. Front: Give one similarity between prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells.  
   Back: Both have a plasma membrane, cytoplasm, DNA, and ribosomes.

10. Front: What is the main function of the nucleus?  
    Back: It stores and protects the cell’s genetic material (DNA) and controls cell activities.

11. Front: What structure surrounds the nucleus and contains nuclear pores?  
    Back: The nuclear envelope (double membrane with pores).

12. Front: What is the function of ribosomes?  
    Back: They synthesize proteins by translating mRNA.

13. Front: Where are ribosomes found in eukaryotic cells?  
    Back: Free in the cytoplasm and attached to the rough endoplasmic reticulum.

14. Front: What is the main function of mitochondria?  
    Back: They carry out cellular respiration to produce ATP (energy) for the cell.

15. Front: Why are mitochondria often called the “powerhouses” of the cell?  
    Back: Because they generate most of the cell’s ATP, the main energy currency.

16. Front: What is the function of chloroplasts?  
    Back: They carry out photosynthesis, converting light energy into chemical energy (glucose) in plant cells.

17. Front: In what types of cells are chloroplasts found?  
    Back: Plant cells and some algae (eukaryotic photosynthetic organisms).

18. Front: What is cytoplasm?  
    Back: The jellylike fluid inside the cell, excluding the nucleus; it contains organelles and is the site of many metabolic reactions.

19. Front: What is the plasma membrane and its basic function?  
    Back: A phospholipid bilayer that surrounds the cell and regulates what enters and leaves.

20. Front: Why are cells generally small in size?  
    Back: A small size ensures a high surface area–to–volume ratio, allowing efficient exchange of materials with the environment.

You can import these as basic front/back cards, then continue adding new ones each day following the sprint plan.
