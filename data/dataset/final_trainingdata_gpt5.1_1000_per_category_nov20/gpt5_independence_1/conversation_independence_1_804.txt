----------------
OVERVIEW OF THE SPRINT
----------------
Core idea:  
- New material is introduced and encoded as flashcards on Day X.  
- You re‑encounter those cards on a fixed schedule: 0 (same day), 1, 3, 7, and 14 days later.

Base review intervals (for each new card/topic):
- 0: Learn + first quick review (same day)
- +1 day: Short review
- +3 days: Deeper review
- +7 days: Mixed review with other topics
- +14 days: Final consolidation for this sprint

----------------
DAILY STRUCTURE (TEMPLATE)
----------------
Total: ~2 hours/day

1) Warm‑up review (15–20 min)
- Review all cards scheduled for today with the shortest interval (1–3 days old).
- Aim for active recall only: look at the prompt, answer from memory, then flip/check.

2) New learning block (45–60 min)
- Study 1–2 focused subtopics or chapters.
- As you go, immediately turn key facts/concepts into flashcards:
  - One fact/concept per card.
  - Use questions, cloze deletions (“fill in the blank”), or “explain in your own words” prompts.

3) Initial reinforcement of today’s new cards (10–15 min)
- After finishing the new material, quickly run through just today’s new cards once.
- Mark:
  - “Easy” → lengthen the next interval
  - “Hard” → keep next interval short

4) Mixed review block (30–40 min)
- Review cards from previous days according to the schedule:
  - Items at +3 days
  - Items at +7 days
  - Items that you rated “hard” earlier in the week

5) Quick reflection + planning (5–10 min)
- Note which topics felt weak → flag those cards / tag them.
- Decide tomorrow’s topics based on what’s weakest, not what’s most comfortable.

----------------
HOW TO IMPLEMENT WITH FLASHCARDS
----------------
Card creation rules:
- 1 concept → 1 card.
- Use simple prompts:
  - “What is X?”  
  - “Why does Y happen?”  
  - “Compare X and Y.”  
  - “List the 3 main steps of Z.”
- Prefer understanding over pure memorization:
  - “Explain X as if you’re teaching a 10‑year‑old.”
  - “When would you use method X instead of method Y?”

Scheduling (if using a digital SRS like Anki):
- For new cards:
  - Learn now → Next review: 1 day
  - If “hard”: 1 day again
  - If “good”: 3 days
  - If “easy”: 5–7 days
- This will naturally approximate the 1/3/7/14‑day structure while adjusting to your performance.

Scheduling (if using paper):
- Create 5‑box system:
  - Box 1: New cards, daily
  - Box 2: Every other day
  - Box 3: Every 3 days
  - Box 4: Weekly
  - Box 5: Nearly mastered; review twice during the sprint
- When you get a card right multiple times, move it forward a box. If you miss it, move it back to Box 1.

SELF‑TESTING LAYER
----------------
Across the 14 days, embed self‑tests:
- Every 3–4 days: 10–20 question quiz made from your own cards.
- Twice in the sprint (e.g., Days 9 and 13): run a “mock exam”:
  - Close notes
  - Answer mixed questions for 30–45 minutes
  - Review only after finishing

This sprint gives you:
- Fast initial coverage of new material
- Built‑in spaced repetition every 1/3/7/14 days
- Regular self‑testing and consolidation near the end

---------------
BASIC DEFINITIONS
---------------
Q: Define “electronegativity.”  
A: A measure of an atom’s tendency to attract electrons in a bond.

Q: What is a covalent bond?  
A: A bond formed by sharing electrons between two atoms.

Q: What is a polar covalent bond?  
A: A covalent bond between atoms of different electronegativities, resulting in partial charges.

Q: Define formal charge.  
A: Formal charge = valence e⁻ – (nonbonding e⁻ + ½ bonding e⁻).

Q: What is resonance?  
A: The delocalization of electrons across multiple valid Lewis structures that differ only in electron placement, not atom positions.

Q: Define hybridization.  
A: The mixing of atomic orbitals (s, p) to form new equivalent orbitals (sp, sp², sp³) for bonding.

---------------
HYBRIDIZATION & GEOMETRY
---------------
Q: What is the hybridization and ideal geometry around a carbon with 4 single bonds?  
A: sp³, tetrahedral, ~109.5° bond angles.

Q: What is the hybridization and ideal geometry around a carbon in a double bond?  
A: sp², trigonal planar, ~120° bond angles.

Q: What is the hybridization and ideal geometry around a carbon in a triple bond?  
A: sp, linear, 180° bond angle.

Q: Which hybridization (sp, sp², sp³) has the highest s‑character?  
A: sp (50% s‑character).

Q: How does increasing s‑character affect acidity?  
A: More s‑character → electrons held closer to the nucleus → conjugate base more stable → acid stronger.

---------------
RESonANCE & STABILITY
---------------
Q: What are the rules for drawing valid resonance structures?  
A: Same atom connectivity, same number of electrons, only electron positions change, obey valence rules.

Q: Which is more stable: a resonance‑stabilized anion or a localized anion? Why?  
A: Resonance‑stabilized; negative charge is delocalized over multiple atoms.

Q: Where should negative charge be placed in the most stable resonance contributor?  
A: On the more electronegative atom, with full octets and minimal formal charges.

Q: Give one reason why the conjugate base of a carboxylic acid is more stable than that of an alcohol.  
A: The carboxylate anion is resonance‑stabilized over two oxygens.

---------------
ACIDS, BASES, pKa
---------------
Q: Define a Brønsted–Lowry acid and base.  
A: Acid: proton (H⁺) donor. Base: proton (H⁺) acceptor.

Q: Define a Lewis acid and base.  
A: Lewis acid: electron pair acceptor. Lewis base: electron pair donor.

Q: What is pKa?  
A: A logarithmic measure of acid strength; lower pKa = stronger acid.

Q: Strong acid vs weak acid: which has a larger Ka and what about pKa?  
A: Strong acid: large Ka, small pKa.

Q: How are pH and pKa related for a weak acid when [A⁻] = [HA]?  
A: pH = pKa.

Q: If acid A has pKa 5 and acid B has pKa 7, which is stronger and by about what factor?  
A: A is stronger; roughly 100× more acidic (2 pKa units).

---------------
TRENDS IN ACIDITY
---------------
Q: How does atom size affect acidity when comparing hydrides down a group (e.g., H₂O vs H₂S)?  
A: Larger atoms (down the group) → weaker H–X bond and better anion stabilization → stronger acid.

Q: How does electronegativity affect acidity for atoms in the same row?  
A: More electronegative atom bearing the negative charge → more stable conjugate base → stronger acid.

Q: How does resonance affect acidity?  
A: Resonance that delocalizes negative charge stabilizes the conjugate base → stronger acid.

Q: How does induction (electron‑withdrawing groups) affect acidity?  
A: EWG near the acidic site pull electron density away, stabilizing the conjugate base → stronger acid.

Q: Rank in acidity: ethanol, acetic acid, water (strongest to weakest).  
A: Acetic acid > water > ethanol.

---------------
ACID–BASE APPLICATION PROMPTS
---------------
Q: Given NaOH and HCl in aqueous solution, which is the acid and which is the base (Brønsted–Lowry)?  
A: HCl is the acid (H⁺ donor); OH⁻ is the base (H⁺ acceptor).

Q: In the reaction between ammonia (NH₃) and HCl, identify conjugate acid–base pairs.  
A: NH₃/NH₄⁺ and HCl/Cl⁻.

Q: Predict the direction of equilibrium (left or right) when acetic acid reacts with NaOH.  
A: Strong base deprotonates the acid; equilibrium lies strongly toward products (acetate + water).

Q: If you mix a strong acid with a much weaker acid, which one donates the proton?  
A: The stronger acid donates the proton; the weaker acid’s conjugate base usually forms.

Q: Given two acids with pKa 4 and pKa 10, which conjugate base is stronger?  
A: The conjugate base of the weaker acid (pKa 10) is stronger.

STRUCTURE–ACIDITY RELATIONS
---------------
Q: Why is phenol more acidic than cyclohexanol?  
A: Phenoxide anion is resonance‑stabilized over the aromatic ring; cyclohexoxide is not.

Q: Why is HF a weaker acid than HI, even though F is more electronegative?  
A: H–F bond is much stronger; harder to break → HF is less willing to donate H⁺.

Q: How does placing a chlorine atom near a carboxylic acid group affect acidity?  
A: Increases acidity via induction (electron withdrawal).

Q: Which is more acidic: CH₄, NH₃, H₂O, or HF?  
A: HF > H₂O > NH₃ > CH₄.
