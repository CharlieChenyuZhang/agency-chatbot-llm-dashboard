## WEEK 1 – Leadership identity & execution habits (Engineering focus)

**Goal:** Show up as a clear, stable decision-maker for your team.

### 1. Define your leadership identity for this team

Today, write this down:

- 3 adjectives (example for an engineering manager):
  - Calm under pressure
  - Decisive with ambiguity
  - Fair and transparent

- 3 “never” rules (example):
  - I never blame individuals publicly for failures.
  - I never cancel 1:1s unless there’s a true emergency.
  - I never let scope creep slide without explicit tradeoff discussion.

- 3 “always” rules (example):
  - I always explain priority changes with the business context.
  - I always respond to blockers within 24 hours.
  - I always give engineers credit publicly for their work.

Use mine if they fit you, or adjust them. Then, in your next team meeting, weave 2–3 of them into how you talk, without labeling them as “rules.”  

Example line you can use this week:
> “You’ll always get context from me on priority changes, even if the decision is unpopular. And if you’re blocked, I commit to responding within a day so you’re not stuck.”

### 2. Set decision rules for technical & product decisions

Use this immediately for your team:

- Reversible / low-risk (small refactor, logging tweak):
  - Let the owning engineer decide; you only need a quick heads-up in standup or Slack.
- Important but reversible (changing a minor service API, adjusting estimates, minor tech choice):
  - Engineer proposes in a short comment or 1–2 paragraph doc.
  - You give a clear yes/no or adjustment within 24 hours.
- High-impact and hard to reverse (architecture changes, key dependencies, major process changes):
  - Require a short RFC (1–2 pages max).
  - Set a decision deadline (e.g., 3 days).
  - Consult exactly 1–2 senior people, then decide.

Communicate this once to the team this week:
> “For small, reversible changes, I want you to decide and just inform me. For bigger, harder-to-undo decisions, we’ll use a lightweight RFC with a clear deadline so you’re never waiting weeks for a yes/no.”

### 3. End-of-day reflection (engineer-focused)

For the next 5 workdays, answer these three questions in writing (5–10 minutes):

1. Where today did I give clarity vs. add confusion?  
2. Where did I help someone move faster vs. become a bottleneck?  
3. What 1 thing will I do differently tomorrow as this team’s leader?

This starts shifting you from “individual contributor mindset” to “system/leader mindset.”

## WEEK 3 – Coaching instead of just solving

**Goal:** Help your engineers think better and grow, instead of you being the single problem-solver.

### 1. Use the “3 Questions” whenever someone brings you a problem

For the entire week, whenever an engineer says “What should I do about X?”, respond with:

1. “What have you tried so far?”
2. “What options do you see from here?”
3. “Which option do you think is best, and why?”

Then either:
- Approve: “That sounds good; go with that.”
- Refine: “That’s close—add X constraint and it’s solid.”
- Redirect: “Given Y context, I’d lean toward another option; here’s why.”

This trains them to think and propose, rather than escalate every decision.

### 2. Give real-time feedback using SBI

Do this twice this week: once for positive behavior, once for something to improve.

**Positive example:**
> Situation: “In yesterday’s standup…”  
> Behavior: “…you clearly called out the risk on the integration work and proposed a fallback plan…”  
> Impact: “…that helped the team adjust early and probably saved us from a late surprise. Nice job.”

**Corrective example:**
> Situation: “In the design review on Tuesday…”  
> Behavior: “…you pushed back pretty hard on the new approach without asking clarifying questions first…”  
> Impact: “…a couple of people shut down and we lost some good input. Next time, start by asking 1–2 questions to fully understand before challenging.”

Stick to this structure: Situation → Behavior → Impact → Clear next time suggestion.

### 3. Deliberate delegation

This week, pick ONE thing you usually do and hand it off to a capable engineer as a growth opportunity. For example:
- Running the sprint demo
- Driving a small cross-team integration
- Owning an internal tool improvement

Use this handoff script:

> “I’d like you to own X. Success looks like Y by [date].  
> Constraints: A, B, C.  
> I’d like a quick update [weekly / twice a week].  
> You have room to make decisions within these boundaries. If you hit something you’re unsure about, bring me 2 options you’re considering, not just the problem.”

## 1. Clarify what “ownership” means in your team

Engineers often “lack ownership” because the term is fuzzy. You’re going to define it concretely.

Share this in your next team meeting or Slack message, adapted to your voice:

> “When I say I want more ‘ownership,’ here’s exactly what I mean on this team:  
>   
> - You don’t wait for detailed step-by-step instructions; you propose a plan and ask for feedback.  
> - When you see a risk or problem, you flag it early with options, not just the issue.  
> - You follow tasks from start to finish, including cleanup, documentation, and communication.  
> - If something is unclear, you ask for clarification instead of waiting in silence.  
>   
> My job is to give you clear goals and constraints. Your job is to figure out a good path inside those constraints and keep me informed.”

This removes ambiguity.

## 2. Change how you assign work

You will stop giving “task lists” and start giving “problems with constraints.”

### Before (low ownership pattern)
- “Implement endpoint A, then B, then add validation C. Use library D.”

### After (ownership pattern)
- “Goal: Users can do X using endpoint A.  
  Constraints: Must handle load of Y req/sec, must log failures with Z fields, done by [date].  
  I’d like you to propose an approach by [short deadline], including tradeoffs.”

Use this template for the next 3 pieces of work you assign:

1. **Goal:** What success looks like from a user/business perspective.
2. **Constraints:** Deadlines, performance requirements, must/never conditions.
3. **Autonomy:** “You decide the implementation details. I’ll review your proposal and help where needed.”
4. **Check-in:** “Let’s sync for 10–15 minutes on [date] to review your plan.”

You apply this consistently; don’t revert to micro-tasks unless someone is brand new or truly stuck.

## 3. Install a simple initiative ritual in standups

For the next month, change standup slightly:

After the usual “yesterday/today/blocked” updates, add:

> “What’s one thing you’re proactively improving this week? It can be a bug, a small refactor, a test improvement, or a process tweak.”

You’re training the team that initiative is an expected part of the job, not “extra credit.”

## 4. Use the “two options” rule when people come to you stuck

Any time someone comes to you with, “What should I do?” or “I’m not sure how to proceed,” respond with:

> “Bring me at least two options you’ve thought of, plus which one you’d pick and why. Then I’ll help you refine or pick.”

Do not solve it for them before they do this thinking.

If they say, “I don’t know what options there are,” you can nudge once:
- “Okay, think in these terms: simpler vs. more robust, short-term vs. long-term, manual vs. automated. Come back with at least two options along those lines.”

This shifts their habit from “escalate problems” to “escalate proposals.”

## 5. Targeted 1:1 conversations with low-ownership individuals

For the 1–2 people who are the most passive, you’ll have a structured 1:1 focused on ownership.

Use this outline:

1. **Describe the pattern, not the person**
   > “I’ve noticed on projects like [example 1] and [example 2], you wait for quite detailed instructions from me instead of proposing a plan first.”

2. **Describe the impact**
   > “That slows you down and limits how much responsibility I can give you. It also affects how quickly the team can move.”

3. **Describe what you want instead (concretely)**
   > “On future tasks, I want you to come to me with:  
   > - Your understanding of the goal  
   > - Your first-pass plan  
   > - Any questions or risks you see  
   > before I give you more detail.”

4. **Set a clear experiment**
   > “On your next task [specify it], I expect you to send me a short message or doc with your plan before asking me what to do next. We’ll review it together. I care more that you try than that the plan is perfect.”

5. **Commit to support**
   > “You’re not on your own—your job is to propose, my job is to help you refine. But I need to see you take that first step.”

Afterward, watch their very next task and catch any progress, even if small, with positive feedback:
> “You did exactly what I asked by proposing that initial plan. That’s what ownership looks like. Let’s keep pushing in that direction.”

## 6. Adjust your own behavior that may be killing ownership

You will deliberately stop doing two common anti-ownership behaviors:

1. **Answering too fast**
   - When someone asks, “Should I do A or B?”  
     Instead of answering immediately, say:
     > “What’s your instinct and why?”  
     Then guide from there.

2. **Rewriting their work without explanation**
   - If you need to change their design or code, don’t silently rewrite.
   - Instead:
     - Highlight: “Here are the parts I changed, and why.”
     - Ask: “Given that, how would you approach it next time?”

You’re signaling: “I expect you to think, not just implement my ideas.”
