However, this robustness comes at a significant computational cost. A major challenge is therefore to preserve the robustness benefits of adversarial training while reducing its computational overhead. [Draft Method Section]

3 Method

3.1 Problem Setup

Let f_θ : ℝ^d → ℝ^K denote a K‑class classifier with parameters θ, mapping input x ∈ ℝ^d to logits f_θ(x). Given a training dataset {(x_i, y_i)}_{i=1}^N with labels y_i ∈ {1, …, K}, standard empirical risk minimization (ERM) minimizes the expected loss

L_ERM(θ) = E_{(x,y)}[ℓ(f_θ(x), y)],

where ℓ is a classification loss such as cross‑entropy.

Adversarial training augments this objective by replacing each clean input x with a worst‑case perturbation x + δ within a small norm ball ‖δ‖ ≤ ϵ. This yields the robust optimization problem

L_adv(θ) = E_{(x,y)} [ max_{‖δ‖ ≤ ϵ} ℓ(f_θ(x + δ), y) ].

In practice, the inner maximization is approximated with iterative attacks such as projected gradient descent (PGD), which significantly increases the computational cost.

3.2 Local Linearity as a Surrogate for Robustness

Our goal is to approximate the effect of adversarial training without solving a costly inner maximization problem. We build on the intuition that if f_θ behaves approximately linearly in a small neighborhood around each training point x, then its predictions will be less sensitive to small, worst‑case perturbations.

Consider the first‑order Taylor expansion of f_θ around x:

f_θ(x + δ) ≈ f_θ(x) + J_θ(x) δ,

where J_θ(x) ∈ ℝ^{K×d} is the Jacobian of f_θ at x. If f_θ is exactly linear in the neighborhood of x, this approximation is exact. Deviations from linearity can therefore be measured by the discrepancy between the true prediction f_θ(x + δ) and its linear approximation f_θ(x) + J_θ(x) δ.

We define a local nonlinearity measure at (x, δ) as

r_θ(x, δ) = ‖ f_θ(x + δ) − [ f_θ(x) + J_θ(x) δ ] ‖^2,

where ‖·‖ denotes an appropriate norm (e.g., ℓ_2 on logits). Large values of r_θ(x, δ) indicate strong curvature or nonlinearity in the model’s behavior around x.

3.3 Local Linearity Regularization

To encourage local linearity, we penalize r_θ(x, δ) for small random perturbations δ sampled around each training point. Specifically, for each (x, y) we sample δ from a distribution D (e.g., a uniform or Gaussian distribution constrained to ‖δ‖ ≤ ϵ_reg), and define the regularization term

R(θ) = E_{(x,y)} E_{δ∼D} [ r_θ(x, δ) ].

Our overall training objective combines the standard classification loss on clean inputs with the local linearity regularizer:

L(θ) = E_{(x,y)} [ ℓ(f_θ(x), y) ] + λ E_{(x,y)} E_{δ∼D} [ r_θ(x, δ) ],

where λ ≥ 0 controls the strength of the regularization.

In practice, we approximate the expectations with minibatches and a single sample δ per input:

L̂(θ) = 1/B ∑_{i=1}^B ℓ(f_θ(x_i), y_i) + λ 1/B ∑_{i=1}^B r_θ(x_i, δ_i),

where δ_i ∼ D for each sample in the minibatch.

3.4 Training Algorithm

Computing r_θ(x, δ) requires both f_θ(x) and f_θ(x + δ), as well as the Jacobian J_θ(x) applied to δ. Instead of explicitly forming J_θ(x), which is costly for high‑dimensional inputs, we compute the Jacobian–vector product J_θ(x) δ using automatic differentiation.

A single training step proceeds as follows:

1) Sample a minibatch {(x_i, y_i)}_{i=1}^B.  
2) For each i, sample a perturbation δ_i ∼ D with ‖δ_i‖ ≤ ϵ_reg.  
3) Compute logits at clean and perturbed inputs: f_θ(x_i) and f_θ(x_i + δ_i).  
4) Compute the Jacobian–vector product J_θ(x_i) δ_i via automatic differentiation.  
5) Evaluate r_θ(x_i, δ_i) = ‖ f_θ(x_i + δ_i) − [ f_θ(x_i) + J_θ(x_i) δ_i ] ‖^2.  
6) Form the total loss L̂(θ) and update θ using standard gradient‑based optimization.

This procedure requires only a single pass over the data and one Jacobian–vector product per input, avoiding the multiple forward‑backward passes needed to generate strong adversarial examples with PGD.

3.5 Computational Complexity

Compared to PGD adversarial training, which typically performs k ≫ 1 gradient steps of the loss with respect to the input for each example, our method uses only a single random perturbation and one Jacobian–vector product per input. In common deep learning frameworks, a Jacobian–vector product has roughly the same cost as a single backward pass.

As a result, the total cost of our method per training step is comparable to that of standard training plus one additional backward‑like computation, while PGD adversarial training scales linearly with the number of attack steps k. In our CIFAR‑10 experiments, this leads to an empirical speedup of approximately 1.7× per epoch compared to PGD adversarial training, while still providing competitive or superior robustness.
