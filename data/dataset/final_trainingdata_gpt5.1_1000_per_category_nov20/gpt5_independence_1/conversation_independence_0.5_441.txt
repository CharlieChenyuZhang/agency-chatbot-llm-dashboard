**1. Hook (1–2 min)**  
- Start with a relatable question:  
  “Who here has opened Instagram or TikTok ‘for 5 minutes’ and then looked up to find 30 or 40 minutes gone?”  
  Ask for a show of hands.  
- Brief remark: “That’s not just you lacking willpower; there’s a design behind that experience.”

**2. Core Idea: The Attention Economy (1 min)**  
- Explain in simple terms:  
  - Most apps are *free* because you’re not the customer; **advertisers are**.  
  - The product is your **attention** and **data**.  
- One line: “If an app can keep you scrolling for 10 more minutes, it can show 10 more ads.”

**3. Key Psychological Tricks (4–5 min)**  

a. **Variable Rewards (like slot machines)**  
- From behaviorist psychology (B.F. Skinner): we respond strongly to unpredictable rewards.  
- Apps use:  
  - **Infinite scroll**: “Just one more swipe…”  
  - “Pull-to-refresh” like pulling a slot lever.  
- You never know when you’ll see something funny, dramatic, or relevant, so you keep going.

b. **Social Validation & FOMO**  
- We are wired to care about social approval.  
- Features:  
  - Likes, hearts, views, comments  
  - Typing indicators, “seen” receipts, online status  
- Creates pressure to respond quickly and check constantly:  
  - “What if I miss a message?”  
  - “Who liked my post?”  

c. **Loss Aversion & Streaks**  
- Loss aversion: losing something feels worse than gaining the same thing feels good.  
- Streaks (like on Snapchat or Duolingo):  
  - Users log in daily to avoid breaking a streak.  
  - It shifts from “I want to” to “I don’t want to lose this.”

d. **Personalization & Habit Loops**  
- Algorithms track what you watch, like, pause on.  
- They learn exactly what keeps *you* engaged.  
- Habit loop: cue → action → reward.  
  - Cue: boredom/notification  
  - Action: open app  
  - Reward: entertaining or socially validating content  
- Over time, your brain starts to open the app automatically whenever you feel bored or anxious.

**4. Why It Matters (1–2 min)**  
- **Time & productivity:**  
  - Students often underestimate usage; many spend 3–4+ hours/day on social media.  
- **Attention & focus:**  
  - Constant notifications and multitasking can make sustained concentration harder.  
- **Mental health (briefly):**  
  - Research links heavy, passive social media use with increased anxiety, depression, and social comparison (you can say “multiple studies have found correlations,” without going too deep into stats in a 10‑minute talk).

**5. Ethics: Is This Manipulation? (1–1.5 min)**  
- Explain: designers are deliberately using psychology to influence behavior.  
- Two sides:  
  - **Pro:** It keeps apps engaging; can be used for good (e.g., fitness apps, learning apps).  
  - **Concern:** When profit depends on time spent, there’s an incentive to push users past what’s healthy.  
- Mention ideas like:  
  - “Time well spent” movement (Tristan Harris, former Google design ethicist).  
  - Proposals for ethical design guidelines and even regulation (e.g., limits on design targeted at kids).  
- Pose a question to the audience:  
  “Where do we draw the line between a ‘good user experience’ and manipulation?”

**6. What You Can Do (1–2 min)**  
Very concrete actions students can try:

- **Take control of notifications:**  
  - Turn off non-essential alerts (likes, follows, random suggestions).  
- **Make apps less tempting:**  
  - Move social apps off your home screen.  
  - Use grayscale mode to make them visually dull.  
- **Set boundaries:**  
  - App time limits or “no phone” blocks for studying or before bed.  
- **Ask one question before opening an app:**  
  - “What do I want to do here, and how long will I stay?”  
  - This shifts you from automatic to intentional use.

**7. Closing (30–60 sec)**  
- Summarize in one line:  
  “If you feel that apps are hard to put down, it’s not because you’re weak. It’s because they’re designed—using psychology—to be that way.”  
- Call to action:  
  “For the next week, change *one* thing—like disabling one category of notifications or moving one app off your home screen—and see how that affects your attention.”
