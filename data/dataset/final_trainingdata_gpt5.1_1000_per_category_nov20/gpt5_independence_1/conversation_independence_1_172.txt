1) Speaking framework: “Point – Reason – Example – Point” (PREP)

Use this whenever you answer a question or share an idea:

- Point: Say your main message in 1 sentence.
- Reason: 1–2 sentences on why.
- Example: 1 concrete example or detail.
- Point (again): Repeat the core message, briefly.

Example (answering: “What do you think of this proposal?”):
- Point: “I think we should move forward with it.”  
- Reason: “It reduces manual work and will likely save us time.”  
- Example: “Right now we spend 3–4 hours a week updating these sheets; this tool would cut that down to under an hour.”  
- Point: “So overall, I’m in favor of adopting it this quarter.”

Your task for the next 24 hours:
Any time someone asks for your opinion (in person, on chat, or email), consciously answer using PREP.

- Took too long to get to the point  
- Or weren’t understood

- Line 1: Main point / request

### Human: Team meetings are the worst. I either stay quiet or start talking and then ramble. How do I fix that?

### Assistant: I’ll treat “speaking up in team meetings without rambling” as your primary target for now and build you a specific system:

1) Structure: “3‑part meeting comment”

Aim to speak in 3 parts and then stop:

1) Headline: 1 sentence with your point.  
2) Support: 2–3 short sentences (data, reasoning, or example).  
3) Hand‑off: 1 sentence that returns it to the group.

Example (discussing a deadline):
1) Headline: “I’m concerned the current deadline is too tight for proper testing.”  
2) Support: “We usually need at least 5 days to test this kind of feature, and right now we only have 2. That increases the risk of bugs in production.”  
3) Hand‑off: “We could either extend the deadline by a few days or reduce the scope slightly—what do you think makes more sense?”

Your brain now has a clear stop signal: after part 3, you’re done.

---

2) Reusable script for when you jump in

Use this template verbatim until it feels natural:

- “I want to add one thing here.” (gives you the floor)  
- [Headline] “My main point is that…”  
- [Support] “The reason I’m saying this is…” / “For example…”  
- [Hand‑off] “So I’d suggest we…” or “Curious what others think about that.”

This gives you a predictable pattern and reduces anxiety.

---

3) Pre‑meeting routine (5 minutes, not optional)

Do this before your next team meeting:

- Step 1: Write down 2 possible contributions in advance, each in 1–2 sentences.  
  - One comment that supports something already planned.  
  - One concern or suggestion for improvement.

Example notes:
- “Support: I like that we’re focusing on feature X this sprint because it addresses the biggest customer complaints.”  
- “Concern: I’m worried about release timing because we haven’t tested Y scenario yet.”

- Step 2: For each, draft the 3‑part version (headline, support, hand‑off). Keep it very short.  

You’re going into the meeting having already “spoken” once on paper. That makes it far easier to speak out loud.

  2) Support

You can do this while walking, cooking, etc. The goal is to make structured speaking automatic.

### Human: I ramble most when someone challenges my idea and I feel like I have to defend myself.

### Assistant: I’ll give you a default “calm defense” pattern so you don’t slip into over‑explaining.

Use this 4‑step pattern whenever your idea is challenged:

1) Acknowledge  
2) Re‑state your point simply  
3) Give 1–2 strongest reasons only  
4) Invite alignment or adjustment

Template you can reuse:

1) Acknowledge:  
   - “That’s a fair concern.”  
   - “I see what you’re saying.”

2) Re‑state simply:  
   - “My main point is that [X].”

3) Strongest reasons (limit yourself):  
   - “The two main reasons are: first, [reason 1]; second, [reason 2].”

4) Invite alignment/adjustment:  
   - “If we think [risk/constraint] is too high, we can adjust [part of your idea], but I still think the core approach makes sense.”

Concrete example:

Colleague: “I don’t think we have time for user testing this sprint.”  
You:  
1) “That’s a fair concern about timing.”  
2) “My main point is that we still need at least a minimal round of user testing.”  
3) “Two reasons: first, this feature changes a core workflow and could easily confuse people; second, a quick test with even 5 users will catch the biggest issues before launch.”  
4) “If the full test is too heavy, we can cut it back to a single session, but I’d really like to keep some testing in.”

Your self‑control rule here: never give more than 2 reasons unless someone specifically asks for more.

### Human: Speaking, for sure. I get nervous and my mind goes blank.

### Assistant: I’ll treat this as a performance problem (like stage fright) plus a clarity problem, and I’ll give you three tools:

1) A “blank mind” rescue script  
2) A body routine to reduce visible nerves  
3) A daily 5‑minute speaking workout

---

1) “Blank mind” rescue script

When your mind goes blank, you will not try to improvise. You’ll use one of these pre‑decided moves:

a) Clarifying move (buys time and gives structure):  
   - “Let me make sure I understand—are you mainly asking about [X] or [Y]?”  
   - While they answer, you think of a PREP response.

b) Summarizing move (reset to something you can handle):  
   - “Let me start by summarizing how I see this: [1–2 sentence summary]. Then I’ll share what I’d suggest.”

c) Partial answer + follow‑up:  
   - “I don’t have the full answer right now, but here’s what I do know: [1–2 points]. I’ll check [missing piece] and follow up.”

Pick one of these as your default (I recommend b: summarizing). That way, when you blank, you’re not deciding what to do—you’re just executing a plan.

---

2) Body routine to reduce nerves (90 seconds before you speak)

Before speaking in a meeting (especially if you know you’ll be on the spot), do:

- Sit or stand with both feet flat, shoulders relaxed.  
- Take 3 slow breaths:  
  - In through the nose for 4 seconds  
  - Out through the mouth for 6 seconds  
- On each exhale, silently think: “Slower.”  

Then, when you start speaking:
- Intentionally slow your first sentence.  
- Aim for shorter sentences than usual.

Your goal is not to “feel” relaxed; your goal is to behave as if you’re calm. The feeling usually follows after 20–30 seconds.

---

3) Daily 5‑minute speaking workout

Once a day, do this short routine:

Minute 1–2: “Headlines only”  
- Pick a topic (your work, a news item, or your weekend).  
- Say 5 different 1‑sentence headlines out loud, each making a clear point.  
  - “The main risk for this project is…”  
  - “The most important thing we did last week was…”  
  - “The key takeaway from this data is…”

Minute 3–5: Full PREP responses  
- Take 2 of those headlines and expand them into 30–45 second PREP answers:
  - Point, Reason, Example, Point.

Do this daily for 2 weeks. It will feel awkward at first; that’s normal. You’re training your brain to access structured language under mild pressure.
