#### Limits: what happens *as* you approach a value

Idea: A limit asks, “What value does \(f(x)\) get close to as \(x\) gets close to some number?”

Example function:
\[
f(x) = 2x + 1
\]

Consider \(x\) getting close to 3.

- At \(x = 2.9\), \(f(2.9) = 2(2.9) + 1 = 6.8\)
- At \(x = 2.99\), \(f(2.99) \approx 6.98\)
- At \(x = 3.01\), \(f(3.01) \approx 7.02\)

As \(x \to 3\), \(f(x) \to 7\).  
We write:
\[
\lim_{x \to 3} (2x + 1) = 7
\]

For nice functions like lines and parabolas, the limit as \(x \to a\) is just the value at \(a\).

---

#### A slightly trickier example

\[
f(x) = \frac{x^2 - 1}{x - 1}
\]

If you plug in \(x = 1\):

- Numerator: \(1^2 - 1 = 0\)
- Denominator: \(1 - 1 = 0\) → undefined

But simplify:
\[
\frac{x^2 - 1}{x - 1}
= \frac{(x-1)(x+1)}{x-1}
= x + 1 \quad (\text{for } x \ne 1)
\]

So when \(x\) is near 1, \(f(x)\) behaves like \(x + 1\). Then:
\[
\lim_{x \to 1} \frac{x^2 - 1}{x - 1} = \lim_{x \to 1} (x + 1) = 2
\]

Even though the function isn’t defined at 1, the limit still exists.

---

#### Short practice (with answers)

Compute the limits by just plugging in and simplifying:

1. \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 2} (3x - 5)\)  
2. \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to -1} (x^2 + 2x)\)  
3. \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 4} \frac{x^2 - 16}{x - 4}\)

**Answers:**

1. \(3(2) - 5 = 1\)  
2. \((-1)^2 + 2(-1) = 1 - 2 = -1\)  
3. Factor:
   \[
   \frac{x^2 - 16}{x - 4}
   = \frac{(x-4)(x+4)}{x-4}
   = x + 4 \quad (x \ne 4)
   \]
   Then the limit is \(4 + 4 = 8\).

#### Core picture: “Zooming in”

Think of a graph of \(y = f(x)\). Pick a point \(x = a\). Now imagine:

- You zoom in around \(x = a\).
- As you zoom in, the graph near \(x = a\) starts to look more and more like a straight line with some height.

The **limit** \(\lim_{x \to a} f(x)\) is the **height** that the graph is trying to settle on as you zoom in around \(x = a\), even if the actual point at \(x = a\) is missing, wrong, or has a hole.

Three key cases:

1. **Nice case (continuous)**  
   As you zoom in, the graph becomes a straight line and the point at \(x = a\) sits right on that line.  
   Then:
   \[
   \lim_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a)
   \]

2. **Hole in the graph**  
   The graph has a missing point at \(x = a\), but if you ignore that missing dot and just look at nearby points, they still settle on a single height.  
   The limit is that height, even though \(f(a)\) might be undefined or different.

3. **Jump or chaos**  
   If you zoom in and the graph **jumps** or wiggles without settling, the limit does **not** exist.

You can think:  
“Forget the exact value at \(x = a\). Just look at what happens **near** it. Does everything around it try to be some definite number? That number is the limit.”

With this picture in place, moving to **derivatives as slopes of that zoomed-in line** becomes straightforward. I’ll take you there next.

### Human: Okay, that helps. So how does this connect to derivatives?

### Assistant: I’ll go straight from your current picture of limits to the idea of derivatives as **instantaneous slope**.

---

#### Average slope first

For a function \(f(x)\), between two points \(x = a\) and \(x = a + h\):

- Point 1: \((a, f(a))\)
- Point 2: \((a + h, f(a + h))\)

The **average slope** between them is:
\[
\text{Average slope} = \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{(a + h) - a}
= \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h}
\]

This is the slope of the **secant line** connecting the two points.

---

#### Derivative = limit of average slopes

To get the **instantaneous** slope at exactly \(x = a\), shrink \(h\) toward 0:

\[
f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h}
\]

That’s the **derivative** at \(a\).

Same pattern every time:

1. Form \(\dfrac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h}\).
2. Simplify.
3. Take the limit as \(h \to 0\).

---

#### Example: \(f(x) = x^2\)

Compute \(f'(a)\):

1. \(f(a) = a^2\)  
2. \(f(a + h) = (a + h)^2 = a^2 + 2ah + h^2\)  
3. Difference:
   \[
   f(a + h) - f(a) = (a^2 + 2ah + h^2) - a^2 = 2ah + h^2
   \]
4. Divide by \(h\):
   \[
   \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h} = \frac{2ah + h^2}{h} = 2a + h
   \]
5. Take the limit as \(h \to 0\):
   \[
   f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} (2a + h) = 2a
   \]

So the derivative of \(x^2\) at \(x = a\) is \(2a\). In function notation:
\[
\frac{d}{dx} (x^2) = 2x
\]

---

#### Matrices as “machines” for vectors

A **matrix** is a rectangular array of numbers, e.g.:

\[
A =
\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 2 \\
3 & 4
\end{bmatrix}
\]

A 2×2 matrix takes a 2D vector and spits out another 2D vector by multiplication:

\[
A \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 2 \\
3 & 4
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
x \\ y
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
1x + 2y \\
3x + 4y
\end{bmatrix}
\]

So the matrix defines a **linear transformation**: it stretches, rotates, and/or shears vectors.

---

#### Simple examples

Let
\[
A =
\begin{bmatrix}
2 & 0 \\
0 & 2
\end{bmatrix}
\]

Then
\[
A \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
2x \\
2y
\end{bmatrix}
\]

This **doubles** every vector. It’s a uniform scaling.

Another one:
\[
B =
\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 0 \\
0 & -1
\end{bmatrix}
\]

Then
\[
B \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
x \\
- y
\end{bmatrix}
\]

This reflects points across the x-axis.

---
