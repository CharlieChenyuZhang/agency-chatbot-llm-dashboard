### Human: I’ve been dating someone for three months. We argue about texting. I want more texts; they say they’re busy. What should I do?
### Assistant: That’s a common friction point. The goal isn’t “more texts” as much as “feeling connected.” A few options that balance both needs:
- Align on purpose: Is texting for logistics, affection, or both? Set expectations.
- Choose predictable touchpoints: e.g., a good-morning check-in and an evening voice note, plus free-form texts when possible.
- Use low-effort connection: voice notes, a shared photo at lunch, or a “thinking of you” emoji. These take less time than lengthy chats.

If you want wording to open the convo:
“Hey, I’ve noticed I feel more connected with a little predictable contact. Could we try a morning check-in and maybe a short voice note after work for two weeks and then see how it feels for both of us?”

Would they be open to a two-week experiment like that?

### Human: Can you give me a text that will make them change their behavior?
### Assistant: I can give you a text that clearly asks for what you want and invites collaboration, but no text can guarantee someone changes. You’ll get better results if you aim for clarity + flexibility. Try:

“Hey you, quick relationship nerd moment: I feel closer when we have a predictable hello and goodnight. Could we try a morning check-in and a short evening voice note for the next two weeks? If it feels like pressure, tell me what would feel doable for you. I want this to work for both of us.”

If they resist, a softer alternative:
“I don’t need constant texting, but I do need some predictability to feel connected. What’s a rhythm you could genuinely keep?”

### Human: They’re still on dating apps. How do I get them to delete them?
### Assistant: You can’t make them, but you can be clear about your boundary and preference. This is really an exclusivity conversation. Two respectful options:

Direct ask:
“I’m feeling ready to date each other exclusively and delete the apps. Are you in the same place? If not, that’s okay, but I want to know so I can decide what’s right for me.”

Boundary version:
“I want exclusivity. If that’s not what you want right now, I’ll keep things casual or step back. No hard feelings—just clarity.”

If they’re not ready, that’s information, not failure. You can pace down, keep your options open, or move on.

### Human: I hate serious talks. Can’t I just hint?
### Assistant: You can hint, but it often backfires—people interpret hints through their own lens. If heavy talks feel awkward, try lighter formats:
- Use a text + follow-up: send your key point, then chat about it later.
- Voice notes: more warmth, less intensity.
- “Menu” approach: “We can either chat 10 minutes now, leave it for tomorrow, or try it by voice note—what’s easiest?”

Mini-script that feels low-stakes:
“Quick check-in: I’m feeling ready to be exclusive and delete the apps. How are you feeling? No pressure—honest answer helps me know how to pace us.”

### Human: What about jealousy? I check their phone sometimes. How do I stop feeling jealous without telling them?
### Assistant: I can’t support checking their phone—that violates privacy and erodes trust. Healthier alternatives that don’t require a big confession right now:
- Interrupt the urge: When you feel the pull, set a 10-minute timer and do something physical (shower, quick walk, 20 push-ups). Urges peak and fade.
- Reframe the story: Write the jealous thought (“They’re probably flirting”). Write 1 neutral alternative (“They’re scrolling news”) and 1 supportive alternative (“Even if they got a DM, it doesn’t define us”).
- Reduce triggers: Mute app notifications on your own phone at night; unfollow accounts that spike insecurity.
- Build secure signals: Ask for a predictable check-in routine and a standing date. Security grows from consistency.
- Strengthen self-trust: Keep two promises to yourself daily (small ones). Feeling reliable to yourself reduces anxious checking.

Longer-term, consider naming the pattern to them when you’re ready: “I get anxious sometimes and I’m working on it. Consistent check-ins help.” That invites teamwork without going into every detail.

### Human: No therapy. Just hacks.
### Assistant: Got it. Here are practical, low-lift hacks:
- The 3-3-3: Name 3 facts that are true right now, 3 things you can control today, 3 things you appreciate about the relationship.
- Urge surfing: When you want to snoop, ride the wave for 90 seconds, breathe slowly, label the feeling (“jealousy”), and re-engage with a task.
- Evidence list: Keep a note of their reliable behaviors (showing up on time, following through). Read it when spiraling.
- Social buffer: Have a friend or activity lined up on nights they’re out; idle time feeds jealousy.
- Sleep + blood sugar: Jealousy spikes when tired or hungry. Pre-commit to snack/sleep before big talks.

If these aren’t enough, we can add structured prompts or a short daily routine.
