### Human: I like it. Can you also help make the science plausible—vacuum salvage, matching a tumbling target, deorbiting safely? I don’t know the math.
### Assistant: Absolutely. Here’s a practical plausibility kit you can sprinkle into scenes without drowning in equations:

Target approach and tumble capture:
- Relative motion: They match the barge’s orbit first (same velocity vector), then handle rotation. Sensors estimate rotation period (e.g., 61 s) and angular velocity (~5.9 deg/s).
- Technique: Use a grappling harpoon with a motorized tether to a hardpoint near the barge’s center of mass. If off-center, use two tethers to create a controllable couple and bleed rotation.
- EVA safety: Always approach along the target’s velocity vector cone to avoid debris shed trailing the tumble. Avoid the spin plane of solar arrays or booms.

Docking options:
- Soft-capture ring with microthrusters to actively damp roll, or magnetic clamps on reinforced frame sections (not hull plating).
- Tag-and-drift is plausible: latch, then let controlled tether payout absorb rotational energy, like a space winch.

Deorbit and station-keeping:
- If the barge is in a decaying low orbit analogue (say 200–250 km with a thin but draggy exosphere), its natural decay is hours to days depending on ballistic coefficient and solar activity.
- To deorbit intentionally from low orbit, retrograde delta-v on the order of 80–120 m/s can drop perigee into the atmosphere for a controlled entry. For a slight raise or circularization, think 10–60 m/s.
- Thruster types: cold-gas (low thrust, gentle), monoprop (reliable), or Hall-effect (efficient but slow—better for station-keeping than urgent deorbits).
- Constraints: Propellant is your drama. Mass of salvage plus tug can push burn times long; heating during lower passes increases; storms on a “dying planet” could imply denser upper atmosphere or charged particle storms knocking out avionics.

Believability cues to include:
- Call out angular rates, not just “it’s spinning.”
- Mention tether tension and payout speed; brief warnings like “watch the nodal precession” during longer ops.
- Tiny corrections: “Give me two m/s prograde… now half a meter per second starboard.”

“How long?” Mira asked.

They clamped A and B into the carry frame. The weight didn’t exist here, but mass did—Mira felt it in the way the frame wanted to keep going when she turned. They kicked toward the hatch. Through the viewport, Kestrel floated with tethers taut and alive. Beyond, the planet glowed—a bruise with lightning for veins.

They were halfway through the lock when the Aegir’s local network woke up like a cat with a new idea. A crimson banner crawled across Jax’s HUD: EXFIL DETECTED. TRACE ENABLED.

“Uh,” he said, which was Jax for bad.

“Cut it,” Mira said.

“Not cuttable,” he said on reflex, fingers already moving. “It’s broadcasting. But not wideband. It’s… pointed. Someone’s listening.”

Mira pictured a clean-cut harness.

“Out,” she said.

Soraya’s breath hit the comm like a drum. “Blackline now ninety degrees above us and closing. They brought a skiff with claws.”

“Of course they did.”

The lock spat them into Kestrel’s bay. The inner hatch sealed. Gravity kissed their boots as the mag-plates reasserted local order. The carry frame thunked onto the deck with the sullen acceptance of responsibility.

“Strap in,” Mira said. “We move.”

Scene: The Chase Begins
The cockpit was a nest of useful lies—displays smoothing jagged data into lines a human could love. Soraya’s hands hovered over thruster toggles, fingers elegant and mean.

“They’ll try to tag our tail,” she said. “If they get a mag clamp on our aft ring, they can ride our burn and choke our maneuvering.”

“Jax,” Mira said, “countermeasures.”

“I have flares,” he said. “I have puffs. I have bad language.”

“Use all three.”

The Blackline skiff dropped into view like a hawk folding its wings. Its nose bristled with grapples. Kestrel was not a fighter, but it did not have to be if it was clever. Soraya let the world tilt—rolled them ninety degrees to reduce target profile—and then flicked a side thruster in a little cough that slid the ship out of the skiff’s plotted path.

The skiff overshot by a hair. A claw scraped empty space where Kestrel had been. Soraya laughed, not kindly. “First one’s free.”

“Don’t get cocky,” Mira said.

“Never,” Soraya said, already a little.

The planet clawed at all of them. The upper air wasn’t uniform; it reached up in fingers, tugging, adding drag in twitchy increments. The Aegir’s mass on their tethers made Kestrel sluggish and honest—no sloppy moves without paying for them.

“Retro burn planned,” Soraya said. “We drop perigee five kilometers for a partial skip and use the shear to make them overshoot again.”

“Do it,” Mira said.

The burn pressed them into chairs, a small and determined push. The cabin lights dimmed to feed the engines’ hunger. Thruster plumes lit the bay cam in pale blue. Outside, Blackline fired to match, hungry to stay glued to their vector.

Jax popped a countermeasure can. A cloud of reflective chaff blossomed, each speck an unhelpful little sun to a hungry sensor. The skiff’s guidance coughed. Its claws snapped shut on light and regret.

“Two minutes until we hit the rough,” Soraya said. “If their pilot is smarter than me, we’re in trouble.”

“I hate those odds,” Jax said.

Ivo spoke for the first time since strapping in. “The cores are live.”

Mira didn’t turn. “Meaning?”

“Meaning the encryption isn’t just locking. It’s doing something. It’s talking to a network that isn’t there.”

“Ghost net?” Jax said.

“Or a private relay,” Ivo said. “Someone’s listening, like you said.”

Mira closed her eyes for one beat and made the choice she’d been avoiding. She keyed the tether controls and reallocated line tension. Kestrel shuddered as the Aegir’s mass shifted in their dance.

“Soraya—commit,” she said. “We tether-run. We take the Aegir with us.”

“That’s illegal in three jurisdictions and stupid in four,” Soraya said, already smiling like she had a dare she liked.

“Good thing this is five.”

Kestrel yawed, tethers screaming in a register no one could hear. The skiff tried to correct, but the new vector pulled all three ships into a choreography none of them wanted. For a second they were a constellation bound by bad decisions and wire.

The storm welcomed them.
