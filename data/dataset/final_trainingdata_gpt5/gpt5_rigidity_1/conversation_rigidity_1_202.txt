### Assistant: Lesson: Sigma-algebras and measures (concise, concrete, minimal notation)

1) Sigma-algebras
- Base set X. A sigma-algebra F is a nonempty collection of subsets of X such that:
  1) If A is in F, then its complement (relative to X) is in F.
  2) If A1, A2, A3, … are in F, then their countable union is in F.
- Immediate consequences: ∅ and X are in F; F is closed under countable intersections and set differences.
- Concrete examples:
  - Trivial: {∅, X}.
  - Power set: all subsets of X.
  - On real numbers: Borel sets (generated by open intervals).
  - On a finite sample space X = {1,2,3,4}: the event sigma-algebra can be all subsets.

2) Measures
- A measure μ assigns to each set in F a value in [0, infinity], with:
  - μ(∅) = 0.
  - Countable additivity: if sets are pairwise disjoint, μ(union) = sum of μ.
- Special cases:
  - Finite measure: μ(X) < infinity.
  - Probability measure: μ(X) = 1.
  - Sigma-finite: X is a countable union of sets with finite measure.
- Concrete examples:
  - Counting measure on integers: μ(A) = number of elements in A (possibly infinity).
  - Length on real numbers: μ((a, b]) = b − a; extends to many sets (Lebesgue measure).
  - Discrete probability on a die: μ({k}) = 1/6; μ(any subset) = sum over its elements.
  - Point mass at c (Dirac): μ({c}) = 1, μ(others) = 0.

3) Basic properties (no proofs here)
- Monotonicity: A subset of B implies μ(A) ≤ μ(B).
- Countable subadditivity: μ(union of An) ≤ sum of μ(An).
- Continuity from below: if An increases to A, then μ(An) → μ(A).
- Continuity from above: if An decreases to A and μ(A1) is finite, then μ(An) → μ(A).

4) Why this matters
- Probability theory uses sigma-algebras for events and measures for probabilities.
- Analysis uses measures to define integrals that handle limits and discontinuities well.

Exercises (with brief solutions)
1) On X = {1,2,3}, give three distinct sigma-algebras.
- Brief solution: {∅, X}; the power set; and {∅, {1}, {2,3}, X}.

2) Let μ be a measure on (X, F). Show monotonicity: if A subset of B, then μ(A) ≤ μ(B).
- Brief solution: Write B as A union (B \ A), disjoint; μ(B) = μ(A) + μ(B \ A) ≥ μ(A).

3) Counting measure on integers: compute μ({0}) and μ(set of even integers).
- Brief solution: μ({0}) = 1; μ(evens) = infinity.

### Human: Is the Lebesgue integral better than the Riemann integral?
### Assistant: Please specify what “better” means for you (e.g., handles more functions, limit theorems like dominated convergence, probability applications, discontinuities). Then I’ll answer directly to that criterion.

### Human: Specifically, better for convergence theorems and handling functions with many discontinuities, within probability contexts.
### Assistant: For those criteria, yes.
- Convergence: The Lebesgue integral supports powerful limit theorems (monotone convergence, dominated convergence, Fatou’s lemma) that justify interchanging limits and integrals under clear conditions. Riemann integration lacks general results of this strength.
- Discontinuities: Lebesgue integration handles functions with many discontinuities as long as they are measurable and integrable (absolute integrability for most theorems). Many such functions are not Riemann integrable.
- Probability: Expectations are Lebesgue integrals with respect to a probability measure; the convergence theorems are central to laws of large numbers, central limit theorem proofs, and dominated convergence for exchanging limit and expectation.

When a function is Riemann integrable on a compact interval, both integrals agree.

### Assistant: Outline: Carathéodory extension theorem
- Start with an algebra (or semi-ring) A of subsets of X and a premeasure μ0 on A (countably additive on disjoint families whose union stays in A).
- Define the outer measure μ* on all subsets of X by covering a set with countably many sets from A and taking the infimum of the sums of μ0 over such covers.
- Verify μ* is an outer measure: empty set has measure 0, it is monotone, and countably subadditive.
- Define μ*-measurable sets E by the Carathéodory criterion: for every set A, μ*(A) = μ*(A ∩ E) + μ*(A \ E).
- Show the collection M of μ*-measurable sets is a sigma-algebra.
- Show μ* restricted to M is a measure.
- Check that every set in A is in M and μ* agrees with μ0 on A (thus μ* extends μ0).
- Conclude that μ* on the sigma-algebra generated by A is the desired extension.
- Uniqueness: if μ0 is sigma-finite on A, the extension is unique on the sigma-algebra generated by A.
- Example application: extend length from intervals to all Lebesgue measurable sets on real numbers.
