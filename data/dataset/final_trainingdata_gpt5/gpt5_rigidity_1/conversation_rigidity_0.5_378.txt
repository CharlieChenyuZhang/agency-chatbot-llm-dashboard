### Human: Health insurance for a 32-year-old, single, in Austin, TX. Pretty healthy. I want to keep costs low.
### Assistant: Great—thanks. For Austin, and prioritizing low monthly costs, here are strong approaches:

Option A: Bronze HSA-eligible EPO
- Why: Usually the lowest premiums and lets you put pre-tax money in an HSA for future medical costs.
- Good for: Rare doctor visits, healthy adults who can handle a higher deductible.
- Watchouts: You’ll pay most costs until you hit the deductible; EPO networks don’t cover out-of-network care (except emergencies).

Option B: Low-cost Bronze (non-HSA) with Rx copays before deductible
- Why: Keeps premiums low but still gives predictable copays for common drugs or visits.
- Good for: If you take any meds occasionally and want some costs covered before the deductible.
- Watchouts: Deductible can still be high; read the fine print on what’s covered pre-deductible.

Option C: Silver plan (if you expect a few visits a year)
- Why: Better balance of premium vs. out-of-pocket; lower deductible and better coverage for visits.
- Good for: If you visit doctors or specialists a few times per year.
- Watchouts: Costs more monthly; at your age/health, you might overpay if you rarely use care.

In Austin, common on-exchange carriers often include Blue Cross and Blue Shield of Texas, Ambetter, Oscar, Aetna CVS, and sometimes Cigna or UnitedHealthcare (availability varies by year/county). I can help you narrow by network size and drug coverage if you share any prescriptions or doctor preferences.

### Human: I really want a PPO with nationwide coverage.
### Assistant: I get the preference—true nationwide PPOs for individual (ACA) plans are very rare in Texas now. Most options are EPO or HMO, which won’t cover non-emergency care out of state.

If a PPO is a must-have, here are realistic alternatives:
- Employer or union plan: Often the simplest path to a true PPO.
- Small-group plan (if you run a business/LLC): Some carriers offer PPOs on small-group, but this comes with setup/eligibility requirements.
- Short-term or limited-duration PPO-like plans: Lower premiums and broad networks, but they can exclude preexisting conditions, have annual caps, and don’t cover all ACA benefits—so they’re riskier.

If you can flex on PPO, a broad EPO from a major carrier is the practical route. You’ll have robust in-network options in Texas and full emergency coverage anywhere.

### Human: I sometimes take Adderall. Does that change your recommendation?
### Assistant: That’s helpful to know. It nudges the choice slightly:
- Many HSA Bronze plans make you pay full price for prescriptions until you meet the deductible. With Adderall (often generic amphetamine-dextroamphetamine), costs and availability can vary.
- You might prefer either:
  - A Bronze plan with prescription copays before the deductible, or
  - A lower-deductible Silver plan with better formulary coverage.

Either way, check:
- The formulary: Make sure your specific medication (generic or brand your pharmacy can fill) is covered and at what tier.
- Prior authorization requirements: Stimulants frequently require PA.
- Preferred pharmacy network: Using the plan’s preferred pharmacies can cut your costs.

### Human: I travel to New York a lot. Will an EPO work?
### Assistant: It depends on how you use care:
- Emergencies: Covered anywhere as in-network emergency services.
- Routine/urgent care: EPOs typically won’t cover out-of-network non-emergency care. Some plans offer national telehealth, which can cover many needs when traveling.

Options if you want more flexibility:
- Choose an EPO with a very broad telehealth benefit plus nationwide urgent-care partnerships.
- If you truly need in-person routine care in New York, consider:
  - A small-group PPO (if eligible) or an employer plan, or
  - Keeping the EPO for major coverage and using cash-pay/retail clinic options while traveling for minor needs.
- As a supplement, some people add low-cost virtual primary care memberships to handle routine issues on the road.

Given availability in Texas, the EPO + robust telehealth is the most practical approach for frequent travel, unless you can access a group PPO.

### Human: What about dental and vision?
### Assistant: For adults, dental/vision are usually separate from medical:
- Dental:
  - If you mainly want cleanings and the occasional filling: a basic PPO dental plan ($20–$40/month) can make sense.
  - If you might need major work (crowns, root canals): pick a plan with higher annual maximums and be mindful of waiting periods.
  - If you rarely use dental: a discount plan (not insurance) can be cheaper than monthly premiums.
- Vision:
  - Plans like VSP or EyeMed run roughly $10–$20/month and can lower costs for exams, frames, and lenses.
  - If you buy glasses every 1–2 years, these often pay for themselves.

You can add dental/vision on Healthcare.gov or directly through carriers.
