### Assistant: Great—3 weeks and 15 minutes a day is plenty. Here’s a focused plan:

Structure your talk (simple and repeatable)
- 1–3–1 framework: Intro (what/why), three key points (results, what worked/what didn’t, next steps), close (call to action).
- Aim for 8 minutes of content + 2 minutes buffer.

Daily 15-minute routine (progressive exposure)
Week 1 (days 1–7)
- Minutes 1–3: Breathing + anchor phrase (“I’m excited to share this.”)
- Minutes 4–8: Practice just the opening and first key point to your phone camera.

Week 2 (days 8–14)
- Do full 10-minute run-throughs 3–4 days.
- At least twice, practice in a different space (empty conference room, kitchen) to normalize “public” feeling.

Week 3 (days 15–21)
- Two full dress rehearsals with slides, standing, timed.
- Practice the first 60 seconds 5 times in a row—that’s your launchpad.
- Build a safety net: a postcard-sized note with your 1–3–1 outline and three trigger words per section.

Day-of routine (5 minutes total)
- Physiological sighs for 60 seconds.
- Power posture for 60 seconds (open shoulders, feet grounded).
- Walk-and-talk: say your first two sentences while strolling outside the room.
- Sip water. Smile at someone friendly as you start.

### Human: I still feel like I’m going to panic. Any quick trick for the day of?

### Assistant: Yes—here are rapid tools that work even under pressure:
- Physiological sighs: Two short inhales, one long exhale, repeat 5–8 times to reduce heart rate.
- TIPP method: Splash cool water on your face or hold a cold bottle for 30–60 seconds; then 20 seconds of brisk steps or wall push-ups; then slow exhale breathing.
- Reframe: Say “I’m excited” instead of “I’m anxious.” Physiologically similar but improves performance.
- Anchor phrase: “Pause. Breathe. Land the first line.” Focus only on your first sentence.
- Grounding: Press feet into the floor, feel the chair or lectern, name 3 things you see.

Optional: Some people use a non-drowsy beta blocker for presentations—talk to your doctor first if you’re considering that. Not necessary for most, but it’s an option.

### Human: What if my mind goes blank mid-talk?

### Assistant: Have a recovery plan:
- Pause and breathe. Sip water. Silence feels longer to you than to the audience.
- Restate your last point: “So we saw strong sign-ups…” This often triggers recall.
- Use a bridge phrase to your outline: “Let me zoom out. We’re covering results, what worked, and lessons. We’ve hit results—next is what worked.”
- Ask a quick rhetorical: “What drove that 120%?” Then answer: “Two things…”
- Safety net: Keep your notecard with 1–3–1 and three trigger words per section. Glance, don’t read.
- If truly stuck: “I’ll circle back to that in a moment,” move to the next section, and return when your brain resets.

Practice this once: intentionally stop, do the sip-and-bridge. Building the reflex reduces fear.

### Human: I don’t have anyone to practice with.

### Assistant: No problem—alternatives that still work:
- Camera reps: Record two runs. Watch one, identify one fix, do one more run. That’s your 15 minutes.
- “Audience simulation”: Play your recording on a speaker while you stand and gesture as if live—it trains your body to be seen.
- Low-stakes reps: Order coffee and explain your project in 30 seconds. Or read your opener to a pet or mirror. It’s exposure training.
- Virtual audience: Join a quiet Zoom meeting by yourself, share slides, hit record. Present to the little self-view box.
- Room rehearsal: Practice in the actual room if possible. If not, find a similar space and stand where you’ll stand.

If later you want human feedback, Toastmasters or a colleague dry run helps—but you can make solid progress solo.
