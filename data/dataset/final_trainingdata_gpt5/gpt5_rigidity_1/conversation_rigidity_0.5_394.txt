### Human: Let’s do heat pumps vs. gas furnaces. Please research and give me key stats and citations.
### Assistant: You got it. Before I tailor numbers: what city or region is this for? Economics and emissions vary by climate zone and grid mix. If you don’t have a location, I’ll keep the stats national and flag where localization matters.

In the meantime, here are solid, broadly applicable points with sources:
- Efficiency
  - Heat pumps move heat rather than generate it, delivering 2–3+ units of heat per unit of electricity (Seasonal COP ~2–3). Modern cold-climate models operate efficiently in subfreezing temps.
  - Gas furnaces convert fuel to heat at 80–98% AFUE (most common ~90–95%).
  - Sources: U.S. DOE consumer guides; NEEP cold-climate heat pump specifications; ENERGY STAR.

- Comfort and performance
  - Variable-speed heat pumps provide steady, even temps and dehumidification; furnaces can feel “hot then cool.” Cold-climate heat pumps now work reliably down to around -15°F/-26°C depending on model.
  - Sources: NEEP, manufacturer performance maps; DOE.

- Emissions
  - Heat pumps typically cut household heating emissions versus gas in most U.S. regions today and improve as the grid decarbonizes over time.
  - Sources: Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory and RMI analyses on building electrification; EPA Power Profiler (for grid mix by region).

- Costs
  - Operating cost depends on relative electricity vs. gas prices and equipment efficiency. Heat pumps can be cheaper to run in many regions; in very cold climates, proper sizing and weatherization matter.
  - Sources: EIA retail energy price data; state energy offices; utility tariff sheets.

- Health and indoor air
  - Electric systems avoid in-home combustion. While furnaces are vented, eliminating combustion reduces risk from combustion byproducts and simplifies ventilation.
  - Sources: EPA/ASHRAE guidance on combustion appliances; health literature on indoor nitrogen dioxide (note: NO2 concerns are larger for gas stoves than sealed furnaces).

- Incentives
  - Federal: up to $2,000 annual tax credit (25C) for qualifying heat pumps; additional credits for electrical upgrades; state/utility rebates vary.
  - Sources: IRS Form 5695 guidance; ENERGY STAR Rebate Finder; state energy office pages.
