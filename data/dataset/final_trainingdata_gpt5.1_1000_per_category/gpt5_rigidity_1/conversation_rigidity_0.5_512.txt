### Human: Okay, 20 is fine for now. My biggest problem is that I feel like everything I write is bad. How do I stop being so critical?

### Assistant: Some self-criticism is normal—and even useful later—but it’s toxic during a short daily practice.

Three practical ways to handle this:

1. **Name two different modes: “Practice Brain” vs “Editor Brain.”**  
   - For your 20-minute session, you are in “Practice Brain” only. Its job: fill the page, not judge.  
   - You can literally say before you start: “This is practice, not performance.” It sounds silly, but clear mental rules help.

2. **Lower the bar on purpose.**  
   - Give yourself a “fail goal” like: *“My job is to write 20 minutes of messy, cliché, uneven writing.”*  
   - You’re not failing if it’s bad; you’re succeeding. Good lines are a bonus, not the goal.

3. **Time-box judgment.**  
   - If you want to critique, you can—but only in a super short, separate window after writing.  
   - Example: after your 20 minutes, allow **60 seconds** to think or jot:  
     - 1 thing that worked  
     - 1 thing to try differently tomorrow  
   - When the minute is up, you stop. This trains your brain that criticism has a limited space; it doesn’t get to invade the whole session.

If you’d like, we can create a specific **pre-writing ritual** that takes under 1 minute and helps switch you into non-judgmental “practice mode.”

### Assistant: Let’s keep it simple and practical—under 60 seconds:

**Pre-writing ritual (about 30–40 seconds):**
1. **Physical cue (5–10 seconds)**  
   - Sit down, place your hands on the keyboard or pen on the page.

     - “Imagine you open our dashboard and the first thing you see is…”

### Human: Mostly project updates to my team, 10–15 minutes. We do have a projector but I really don’t want to use it.

### Assistant: For 10–15 minute project updates, you can absolutely go slide-free if your team is okay with it. To keep things clear and creative without visuals, we’ll lean on **structure + props + clear summaries**.

Here’s a simple template you can adapt:

**1. Hook (1–2 minutes)**
- Start with a quick story or concrete scenario instead of “So, updates.”  
  - “Imagine it’s Monday morning and our system is down for 3 hours…”  
  - Then: “Everything I’ll share today is about preventing that scenario.”

**2. Three-part structure (8–10 minutes)**
Use three clear buckets, and say them out loud at the start:

> “I’ll cover three things: where we were, what’s changed, and what happens next.”

- **Where we were (2–3 min)**  
  - 2–3 sentences each on the initial situation, pain points, and goals.  
- **What’s changed (4–5 min)**  
  - 2–3 concrete changes or milestones.  
  - For each: what we did, why it matters, one short example or number.  
- **What happens next (2–3 min)**  
  - Next 1–2 steps, risks, and what you need from the team (if anything).

**3. Simple prop (optional but powerful)**
- Bring something like:
  - A printed timeline with 3–5 milestones.  
  - A single A4 page with a simple sketch or table (you can hand it around or pin it).  
- This replaces slides while still giving people a visual anchor.

**4. Close with a summary line (1 minute)**
- End with: “If you remember only one thing from this update, let it be…”  
- That forces you to distill your main message, which also impresses people.

If your manager ever pushes you to use slides, one compromise is a **single slide** that just has:
- The 3 section titles, and maybe  
- One key metric or date.

That keeps the pressure off you visually, but meets expectations and adds clarity.
