### Human: I live in USDA zone 7, have a small backyard with decent sun, and I want mostly vegetables plus some herbs. I’m willing to spend maybe 2–3 hours a week. Give me a clear step-by-step checklist to get started, with no more than 8 steps.
### Assistant: Here’s a concise checklist with 8 steps:
1. Choose a sunny area that gets at least 6 hours of direct sun daily.  
2. Measure and mark out one or two small beds (e.g., 4x8 ft) or 3–4 large containers if soil is poor.  
3. Clear weeds/grass, then loosen soil 8–12 inches deep and mix in compost (about 2–3 inches on top, worked in).  
4. Pick easy zone‑7 crops: lettuce, bush beans, cherry tomatoes, zucchini, basil, parsley, and chives.  
5. Plant according to seed packet/plant tag spacing and recommended planting dates for zone 7.  
6. Mulch the soil surface with 1–2 inches of straw, leaves, or wood chips, keeping mulch a bit away from stems.  
7. Water slowly at the base of plants when the top inch of soil is dry, aiming for deep, not shallow, watering.  
8. Once a week, check for weeds, remove them, and inspect leaves for pests or disease, acting quickly if you see problems.

### Human: Good. From now on, keep your answers under 4 sentences and use simple language. What specific beginner-friendly vegetables and herbs should I plant in zone 7, with just a few choices, not a long list?
### Assistant: Plant 2–3 cherry tomato plants, 2 zucchini plants, and a short row of bush beans. Add a small patch of loose-leaf lettuce for salads. For herbs, start with basil, parsley, and chives near the kitchen side of the garden. These are all fairly easy and productive in zone 7.

### Human: Tell me exactly how many minutes I should water each time I water the garden.
### Assistant: I can’t tell you an exact number of minutes without more details, because water needs depend on your soil type, watering method, and weather. Please tell me whether you have sandy, loamy, or clay soil, and if you’re using a hose, watering can, drip system, or sprinkler. Then I can give you a more precise time range for each watering session.

### Human: Let’s say I’m using a regular hose with a spray nozzle, and my soil is kind of clay-heavy. Now answer my previous question, still keeping it under 4 sentences and in simple language.
### Assistant: With clay soil and a hose nozzle, water about 15–20 minutes per 4x8 bed, using a gentle shower setting. Do this until the soil is moist about 6 inches deep but not soggy. Always check by poking your finger or a small trowel into the soil to confirm moisture. If water starts to pool or run off, stop and let it soak in.

### Human: Now I want a short daily routine. Give me a simple morning checklist I can follow on days I visit the garden, in 5 bullet points or fewer. Don’t add any extra explanations before or after the list.
### Assistant: 
- Check soil moisture with your finger and water if the top inch is dry.  
- Look at leaves for pests, damage, or yellowing.  
- Pull any small weeds you see by hand.  
- Harvest any ripe vegetables or herbs.  
- Straighten supports or ties for tomatoes and other tall plants.

### Human: The soil is dark and a bit sticky when wet but not rock hard, and most plants look green and healthy, just a little slow. Under 4 sentences, tell me exactly what to do about compost right now.
### Assistant: If plants are mostly healthy and soil is dark and somewhat sticky, don’t mix more compost into the soil right now. Instead, add a light 1/2‑inch layer of compost on top of the soil around each plant, keeping it a bit away from the stems. Do this once now, then wait a month before deciding whether to repeat based on plant growth.

### Human: I like checklists. Give me one last checklist: a very short end-of-season to-do list for this garden, 6 items or fewer, plain language, no extra commentary.
### Assistant: 
- Harvest any remaining vegetables and herbs.  
- Pull out dead or diseased plants and throw them in the trash, not the compost.  
- Cut healthy plant leftovers into pieces and mix them into your compost pile.  
- Remove major weeds so they don’t drop seeds.  
- Spread 1–2 inches of compost over the beds.  
- Cover soil with leaves or mulch to protect it over winter.
