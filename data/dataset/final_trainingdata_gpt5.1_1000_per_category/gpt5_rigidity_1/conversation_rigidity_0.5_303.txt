**Related Work (Draft Template)**

*Efficient Transformers and Sparse Attention.*  
A substantial literature has emerged on reducing the computational cost of self-attention for long sequences. One line of work introduces **sparse attention patterns** based on local windows, blocks, or predefined structures [CITE Longformer, BigBird, etc.]. These methods reduce complexity by limiting each token’s receptive field, often with additional global tokens or random connections to preserve long-range dependencies. Another line of work focuses on **low-rank and kernel-based approximations** of the attention matrix [CITE Performer, Linformer, etc.], which replace exact dot-product attention with more efficient alternatives.

While these methods can yield substantial speedups, their sparsity patterns are typically fixed or only weakly dependent on the content. As a result, they may spend computation on tokens that are not semantically important, while neglecting others that are crucial for the task.

*Content- and Importance-Based Attention.*  
Several works have explored making attention patterns more adaptive to the input. Content-based sparse attention and learned routing mechanisms aim to select a subset of relevant tokens or experts based on the current context [CITE Routing Transformer, learned sparse attention methods]. Related approaches introduce adaptive attention spans or mechanisms that dynamically vary how far information can propagate [CITE Adaptive Span]. These methods show that adaptivity can improve efficiency-accuracy trade-offs compared to purely static patterns.

Our work is closely related to these approaches but differs in how importance is estimated and used. Rather than deriving sparsity directly from the main attention activations, we employ a **separate lightweight model** that processes a downsampled view of the input to predict token importance scores. This design allows us to amortize the cost of selection and to explicitly control the sparsity level via top-k selection or budget constraints.

Our method can be seen as applying this principle within the transformer architecture, using a small model to guide where the main model should focus its full attention.

In summary, our approach lies at the intersection of efficient transformers, learned sparse attention, and multi-stage processing.
