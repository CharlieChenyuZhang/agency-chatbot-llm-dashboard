The observation deck of the Imperial Embassy hung over the city like a glass blister, its curved walls framing the alien skyline of Kethar Prime. Twin suns smeared orange light across tiers of crystalline spires and ribboned transit ways. “Ambassador?” someone murmured behind her.

“I’m not the ambassador,” she said. “Just the latest decorative attachment.”

The reception hall behind them was a study in managed tension: clusters of Imperial officials in dark uniforms, alien delegates in ceremonial carapace or iridescent fabrics, trays of floating drinks drifting between them on low-grav discs. Make use of it. The phrase crawled under her skin, joining the others that had piled up since she’d traded a cockpit for a conference room. Asset. Optics. Narrative.

In theory.

Probably. The word did nothing to ease the tightness in her shoulders. Rhea had flown missions with targeting systems “probably fine” and watched them fail in the first volley.

Combat band.

“Envoy,” she said, aware that a dozen cameras were capturing this. “It’s an honor.”

“Rhea Calder,” Tsalek said, their voice filtered through a translator node that rendered it smooth and neutral. “Pilot designation: Rhea-Three-Four-Seven. Carrier: Valiant’s End. Engagements: Crosswind, Makor Belt, Siege of Halom.”

Rhea’s throat tightened. The aliens did their homework. They always had.

“You have my record,” she said. “I hope my future can be less…eventful.”

A few diplomats chuckled politely. Tsalek’s bioluminescence flickered, an alien sign Rhea had learned to read as amusement.

“We remember those who fought,” Tsalek said. “And we remember those who choose not to fight again, when fighting is all they have known.”

The pulse in Rhea’s spine kicked, hard. Her vision pixellated for a heartbeat—then a flood of data hammered against her neural interface, a cascade of symbols and scrambled code.

“Problem?” Tsalek asked softly, in a tone too low for the nearest microphone.

Silence answered. Then: ACCESS DELAYED. NETWORK CONGESTION.

“Of course there’s congestion now,” she breathed.

“Calder?” Hadrin’s voice cut in. “Smile. The photographers are—”

The lights flickered.

It was nothing, just a fractional dimming, but in a room full of high-strung officials and twitchy security, it scraped everyone’s nerves raw. Conversations stuttered. The trade concord emblem above them glitched, rings shuddering out of alignment for half a second before snapping back.

Rhea heard the soft whine of powered armor warming in the hall’s hidden recesses. That wasn’t supposed to be audible.

Her neural brace flared hot.

WARNING, it flashed across her inner vision in stark red. UNAUTHORIZED ACCESS ATTEMPT. SOURCE: LOCAL.

Local. Not an external attack. Not the Kethari grid or some distant hacker. Someone inside the embassy was poking at the combat band.

“Rhea.” Kade’s voice this time, a low thread on a tight-beam channel. “Do you see—”

The catering drone in the corner suddenly lurched, tray tilting. Glasses slid, spilled, then shattered in a spray of glittering shards as the drone dropped to the floor like a puppet with its strings cut. The holo-emblem above them spasmed, its rings twisting into jagged shapes that looked, just for an instant, like targeting brackets.

Gasps, a few startled cries. Security staff stepped forward, hands near concealed weapons, then hesitated—no one wanted to be the first to overreact in front of alien media.

“Minor systems fault,” someone from tech called out, too loudly. “We have it contained.”

Rhea’s implant disagreed.

UNAUTHORIZED ROUTING ESTABLISHED, it flashed. COMBAT CHANNEL HANDSHAKE COMPLETE.

A narrow band of audio cut through the noise, barely more than a whisper at the back of her skull. It was an old frequency, one she hadn’t heard since the war, loaded with muscle memory and ghosts.

“Rhea Calder,” a voice said inside her head, crisp and unfamiliar. “If you want to keep this peace you’re selling, walk away from the cameras. Now.”

Her pulse spiked. She kept her face neutral, jaw unclenched, every instinct screaming for a cockpit, for a HUD, for something she could aim.

Instead, she stood in a room full of dignitaries and hidden guns, with an alien envoy watching her too closely.

And someone, somewhere inside her own embassy, had just used a war-band channel to talk in her ear.

Chapter 2 – Ghost Frequencies (opening)

The official explanation arrived faster than any real diagnosis could have.

By the time Rhea reached the security operations room, the embassy’s internal net was already pushing out a statement to all authorized channels: minor power fluctuation due to localized grid interference, no threat detected, systems stability confirmed.

The words pulsed in the corner of her vision, trying to overlay themselves on reality. She blinked them away and stepped through the door.

Ops was a windowless ring of consoles and floating displays, the air cool and faintly metallic. A textured holo of the embassy’s layout hung in the center like a ghostly cross-section, levels stacked and color-coded. Tonight it throbbed with more red alerts than she’d ever seen outside a drill.

“Calder,” the shift supervisor said as she entered. “Didn’t expect you down here.”

“Funny,” Rhea said. “I didn’t expect our trade ceremony to glitch like a battlefield either.”

A few heads turned. Most quickly turned back, busy pretending not to listen.

The supervisor, Lieutenant Jarrow, wore standard security black, his hair cut to stubble, his expression carefully neutral. He’d done three tours planetside during the war, if Rhea remembered right. They’d never served together, but veterans learned to spot one another.

“We have it contained,” Jarrow said. “As the advisory states, there was a localized—”

“If you quote the memo at me, Lieutenant, we’re going to have a problem,” Rhea said, stepping closer. “Something rode our combat band in the middle of a diplomatic ceremony. That’s not a ‘localized interference.’ That’s hostile action.”

The room seemed to hold its breath. Jarrow’s jaw worked, but his gaze flicked involuntarily toward the ceiling cam in the corner before he spoke.

“Ma’am,” he said, defaulting to the polite form, “our logs don’t show any unauthorized connections to combat frequencies. I’ve got three techs combing the data as we speak. If there was a breach, we’ll find it.”

Rhea tapped the back of her neck where the brace sat under her collar. “My implant disagrees with your logs.”

A second voice cut in before Jarrow could answer. “Implants can misread. Especially older combat models.”

Rhea turned. A woman had risen from one of the rear consoles, dark hair pulled back, sleeves rolled up to reveal a forearm crisscrossed with faint white scars from welding or lab work. Her badge read JAYA SONTI – SYSTEMS.

“Calder,” Jaya said, nodding once. “Your brace is a Mark-7K, right? Made to prioritize battlefield input over everything else. They’re twitchy as hell in civilian nets.”

Rhea studied her. “You’re saying I imagined a handshake request with the exact encryption structure of an Imperial carrier group channel?”

Jaya’s mouth twitched. “I’m saying if someone wanted to make you doubt your senses, that’d be a good way to do it.”

Jarrow shot her a warning look. Rhea didn’t miss it.

“Walk me through it,” Rhea said. “From your end. What did you see when the lights flickered?”

Jaya hesitated, then gestured toward her console. “Fine. Lieutenant?”

Jarrow sighed, but nodded. “Keep it high-level.”

Jaya wiped a hand across the surface; the main display split, one half showing a time-synced feed of the ceremony hall, the other a staggered cascade of code and graphs.

“Twenty-two hundred hours, thirty-one seconds,” Jaya said, tapping a marker. The image froze a beat before the drone fell. “We get a spike in cross-band noise. First theory was bleed-through from the Kethari local grid—”

“First theory?” Rhea said.

“First polite theory,” Jaya corrected. “The spike tries to hit several embassy subsystems at once. Most of them reject it. One doesn’t.”

She highlighted a thread of code in angry red. It diverged from the main streams, wriggling like something alive.

“That’s your implant,” Jaya said. “The system detected a compatible military-grade receiver. It…reached for it.”

Rhea’s shoulders went cold. “So you did see the handshake attempt.”

Jarrow cleared his throat. “What we see is a malformed ping that got bounced by standard firewalls. No data transfer, no breach of protected systems. Which is what I reported to Ambassador Hadrin.”

Rhea stepped closer to the display. “Malformed how?”

Jaya zoomed in. The red thread became a lattice of symbols. Even half-abstracted, Rhea recognized the structure: carrier call sign header, unit identifier, engagement code.

“It’s like someone tried to use an old war frequency as a keycard,” Jaya said. “Only part of it was wrong. Or deliberately obfuscated.”

“Part of it was right,” Rhea said. “Right enough to trip a response on my side.”

“You sure?” Jarrow asked. “No offense, ma’am, but—”

Rhea pulled up her own interface, keying a sequence against the inside of her wrist. The brace hummed; a private field unfolded in front of her eyes, visible only to her. She opened the last temporary buffer and replayed the cached sensory layer.

The room blurred, replaced by an overlay of that moment in the hall: the flicker of lights, the stutter in the holo emblem. Over it, raw data scrolled—signal strength, packet headers, a line of code that flashed just before the warning.

CALLSIGN QUERY: VALIANT-THREE-FOUR-SEVEN.

She froze the frame and pushed it to the external display.

The text appeared in the air above the console. Jarrow’s composure slipped; Jaya’s brows shot up.

“Well,” Jaya said softly. “That’s not standard interference.”

Jarrow recovered fast. “Could be a forged header,” he said. “Spoofed. We used similar tricks during the war.”

“Exactly,” Rhea said. “Which means whoever sent it knows our old protocols intimately, and knew I’d be in that room.”

She let that sink in. Ops hummed around them, other consoles continuing their silent work, but a thin, brittle tension had settled over their corner.

“Who has access to these frequencies?” Rhea asked. “Officially. In this embassy.”

“Off the record?” Jaya said, glancing at Jarrow again. When he didn’t immediately shut her down, she went on. “Very few. Combat bands are supposed to be hardwalled. We keep them active only as a failsafe—last-resort defense if things go catastrophically wrong.”

“Today wasn’t ‘catastrophically wrong’ enough?” Rhea asked.

“Today was embarrassing,” Jarrow said. “There’s a difference. And we don’t escalate embarrassment to catastrophe by broadcasting that our systems are compromised when our logs don’t support that conclusion.”

Rhea heard what he wasn’t saying: the Ambassador doesn’t want that conclusion.

“You prefer ‘minor glitch,’ then,” she said. “Nice and tidy.”

“I prefer not giving hardliners on either side an excuse to walk away from the table,” Jarrow said, the patience thinning in his voice. “Ma’am, with respect, your experience is invaluable, but we’re not on a carrier anymore. Perception is as much a weapon as any railgun.”

Rhea thought of the voice in her head, the calm certainty when it had told her to walk away from the cameras. Weapon, yes. She also thought of three million dead when perception had been used to justify every escalation.

“If we pretend this didn’t happen,” she said, “we’re giving someone inside our perimeter all the time they need to try again. Maybe next time, their header will be perfect.”

Jaya’s fingers drummed a silent rhythm against her console. “Lieutenant, at minimum we should flag this for deep audit,” she said. “Cross-reference internal access logs, see who was tied into legacy bands—”

Jarrow cut her off with a raised hand. “I’ll note your recommendation, Technician Sonti.”

He turned back to Rhea. “Ambassador Hadrin has already instructed us to treat this as a technical hiccup unless and until we find concrete evidence of tampering. If you have that evidence, I’ll prioritize it. If not…”

His shrug finished the sentence.

Rhea stared at the frozen header on the display. CALLSIGN QUERY: VALIANT-THREE-FOUR-SEVEN. Her old carrier, her old identifier, reaching out of nowhere like a hand from a grave.

“I’ll find the evidence,” she said.

Jarrow held her gaze a moment longer, then nodded once, an acknowledgment or a challenge. She wasn’t sure which.

“Understood, ma’am.”

As she turned to leave, Jaya’s voice followed her, pitched low enough not to carry.

“If you pull the rest of that buffer,” Jaya murmured, “there might be routing metadata attached. Junk to most eyes. But maybe not to mine.”

Rhea paused. “You’re offering to look?”

Jaya’s mouth quirked. “Off the record, of course. I hate unexplained code almost as much as I hate senators.”

“Senators?” Rhea said.

“Long story.” Jaya tapped the console. “Send me what you’ve got when you’re somewhere the walls aren’t listening.”

Rhea stepped back into the corridor, the door sliding shut behind her with a soft hiss. The official advisory blinked again in her peripheral vision: MINOR POWER FLUCTUATION. SYSTEMS STABLE. NO THREAT DETECTED.

She dismissed it with a thought.

If there was no threat, she wondered, why did it feel so much like the silence before the first shot?
