### Human: How do I choose between family medicine and internal medicine? I’m 34, pretty healthy.
### Assistant: For a 34-year-old, generally healthy adult, both can work well. The difference is more about scope and style:

**Family Medicine**
- Trains to care for: **children + adults + sometimes prenatal care**.
- Good if:
  - You might want **one doctor for your whole family**.
  - You like a “whole-life” perspective (from pediatrics to older age).
- Often more common in community/primary care settings.

**Internal Medicine**
- Trains to care for: **adults only (18+)**, often with more emphasis on complex adult conditions.
- Good if:
  - You’re mainly focused on **your own adult care**.
  - You have—or may develop—chronic conditions (e.g., high blood pressure, diabetes).
  - You prefer someone who may be more used to coordinating with specialists for adult issues.

For a healthy 34-year-old with no kids:
- If you want simple, adult-only care: **Internal Medicine** is a very solid choice.
- If you like the idea of continuity for a future family, or you’re used to a “family doctor” style: **Family Medicine** is also perfectly fine.

### Human: When I look at reviews, how do I know which ones to trust? Everyone has a mix of good and bad.
### Assistant: The trick is to focus on **patterns**, not individual reviews. Here’s a quick way to interpret them:

1. **Look for repeated themes**
   - Good signs if multiple people mention:
     - “Listens and doesn’t rush.”
     - “Explains things clearly.”
     - “Follows up with results quickly.”
     - “Friendly and efficient staff.”
   - Concerning if you see the same complaint over and over:
     - “Always late / long waits.”
     - “Hard to get a call back.”
     - “Billing errors all the time.”
     - “Feels rushed, doesn’t listen.”

2. **Check review dates**
   - Prefer doctors with:
     - Several reviews from the **last 6–12 months**.
   - If all the good reviews are 5+ years old and the recent ones are worse, that’s a subtle red flag.

3. **Ignore extremes without detail**
   - A 1-star review that just says “Terrible doctor” with no explanation is less useful.
   - A 3–4 star review with detailed pros and cons can be more informative than a generic 5-star.

4. **Consider what *you* care about**
   - If you hate waiting, multiple complaints about wait times might matter a lot.
   - If you value thorough explanations, prioritize comments about how well they explain issues.

### Human: Okay, this makes sense. One last thing: what should I ask on the first visit to make sure this doctor is a good fit?
### Assistant: A first visit is a good time to “interview” the doctor a bit. You don’t have to grill them, just ask a few targeted questions. Here are 5 concise ones that work well:

1. **“How do you usually like to handle communication—messages through the patient portal, phone calls, or follow-up visits?”**  
   - You’re checking if their style matches how you prefer to communicate.

2. **“If I have a non-urgent question, how quickly do patients usually hear back?”**  
   - Clarifies expectations about responsiveness.

3. **“How do you usually manage ongoing issues like my asthma—do you handle most things, or do you refer to specialists early?”**  
   - Tells you their philosophy on primary care vs referrals.

4. **“If I ever need same-day or urgent care, what’s the usual process?”**  
   - Helps you know if they offer same-day slots or if you’ll be pushed to urgent care/ER.

5. **“What do you recommend I focus on to stay healthy over the next few years?”**  
   - Good doctors will give personalized, practical advice, not just generic “diet and exercise.”

After the visit, ask yourself:
- Did they listen without interrupting?
- Did you feel rushed or comfortable asking questions?
- Did they explain things in a way you understood?
