Lena Ortiz knew something was wrong from the way the map turned red.

San Francisco glowed on the wall of the incident room—blocks and streets breathing with color. Most of the city was calm blue. The Mission was bleeding.

“Why is Mission spiking?” someone asked.

Lena’s fingers were already on the keyboard. She pulled up the last ten minutes of activity. Numbers scrolled by, too fast for the rest of the room. Her mouth went dry.

“Nothing’s scheduled there,” she said. “No planned ops, no alerts.”

“Doesn’t matter,” said Randall Hayes behind her. “What matters is what the system sees.”

On the main display, a banner flashed amber.

LEVEL 4 RISK — MISSION DISTRICT — NEXT 12 MINUTES.

Her stomach dipped. Level 4 meant “high probability of violence involving law enforcement.” It usually came with sirens.

“That has to be a mistake,” she said.

Randall stepped closer to the screen, hands behind his back. “Run the confidence check.”

She already had. The result glowed in the corner of her monitor: 0.83. Eighty-three percent.

“Too high for a glitch,” someone murmured.

Lena forced herself to breathe. “Our primary police feed for Mission is clean,” she said. “Traffic cams are normal. No spike in incident keywords. This is all coming from a new setting. It hasn’t been validated in live conditions.”

Another banner cut across the top of the wall.

RECOMMENDED ACTION: PROACTIVE DEPLOYMENT — 2 UNITS.

The room quieted the way it always did before a decision. You couldn’t hear the opinions, but you could feel them tilt toward the numbers.

“Compliance?” Randall asked.

A woman in a blazer looked up from her tablet. “Protocol allows proactive deployment at Level 4 with confidence above 0.8,” she said. “Projected casualty reduction is sixty-seven percent.”

It sounded neat, contained, like a slide in a deck.

“We don’t have supporting data,” Lena said. “People are just—moving around. Late shifts, deliveries, rideshares. We’re extrapolating from noise.”

Randall turned toward her. “You tuned this model,” he said. “Are you telling me not to act?”

The question tightened something in her chest. The countdown in the corner of the display read 00:04:39.

“We can flag it as an advisory,” she said. “Ask for a drive-through, eyes-on. We don’t have to trigger a Level 4 recommendation.”

Randall checked his watch. “If we underreact and something happens, we’ll all be sitting somewhere less comfortable explaining why we ignored an eighty-three percent warning.”

He tapped his tablet.

“Ops to SFPD Command,” he said when the line opened. “System’s flagging Level 4 risk in Mission. Recommend two units, low profile. Confirm dispatch.”

Lena’s hands hovered, useless, over her keyboard.

SFPD’s voice crackled through. “Copy. Two units. Any specific target?”

“Negative. Area sweep. System will update.”

On the map, two blue icons appeared at the edge of the Mission, turning toward the red.

“Log my objection,” Lena said.

The word hung there for a second.

Randall frowned slightly. “Come again?”

“Under governance policy, engineers can file technical objections to live recommendations,” she said. She kept her eyes on the screen. “I’m filing one.”

“That’s meant for clear malfunctions,” he said. “Not for… disagreements.”

“This is a malfunction,” she replied. Her pulse thudded in her ears. “New settings, unverified input, no corroboration. If we’re honest, we don’t know what’s driving this spike.”

The compliance officer shifted in her chair. “Policy does say objections must be recorded,” she said. “It doesn’t require a change in recommendation, but it goes in the log.”

For a moment, no one spoke. Then Randall nodded once. “Fine. Type it.”

Lena pulled up the form.

ENGINEER TECHNICAL OBJECTION — LIVE REC #448219

Her fingers steadied as she typed:

Unverified input. No support from primary feeds. Elevated risk of false positive. Recommend downgrade to advisory.

She hit submit. Time stamped: 00:06:12.

On the wall, the cruisers moved into the Mission’s red grid.

“Traffic cam, 24th and Bryant,” someone called.

A black-and-white feed snapped open on the big display. Empty street. A car at the light. Two hooded figures walking on the sidewalk, shoulders brushing.

One of the cruisers turned onto the same block.

The room leaned toward the screens.

The patrol car slowed. Lights came on—red and blue flaring in the grainy footage. The two figures froze.

“Zoom,” Randall said.

The camera pushed in. One of the figures reached into his jacket. Lena leaned forward without realizing it.

Her monitor overlaid a small number in the corner of the feed—weapon probability ticking up as the motion registered.

“It’s a phone,” she said under her breath. “It looks like a phone.”

On screen, an officer stepped out, shouting something the audio didn’t catch. The figure yanked an object out quickly, like he’d been reminded it was there.

“Gun!” The shout came from the street, distorted by the speaker.

A single shot cracked through the room.

The figure dropped.

No one in the incident room moved. On the wall, the Mission grid cooled a shade, as if nothing more needed to be done there.

Projected Casualty Reduction: 0%, the overlay now read.

SFPD’s voice came back, thinner. “Ops, we’ve got one down. Units want to know if the system saw a weapon. Cameras are fuzzy. We need to know what to say.”

Every head turned, not quite openly, in Lena’s direction.

She looked at the frozen frame: the officer’s outstretched arm, the dark blur in the fallen man’s hand. Her own reflection hovered faintly over the glass, eyes wide.

“Dr. Ortiz?” Randall said quietly. “They’re waiting.”

Her objection notice blinked in the corner of her screen, a small block of text against the dark.

“The incident is under technical review,” she said. Her voice sounded unfamiliar to her. “Inputs were unstable. I recommend preserving all footage and avoiding definitive statements about system guidance until we complete analysis.”

There was a pause.

“…Copy,” the voice said. The line dropped.

Chairs creaked. Screens began to dim. Someone’s whispered comment died halfway out of their mouth when they caught Lena’s expression.

“Legal and Comms at nine,” Randall said. “We’ll go over it then.”

He left first.

Lena added one more line to the objection before she closed it:

Recommend review of deployment thresholds and external oversight.

She saved it.

On the wall, the city map slowly shifted back toward blue. The Mission settled into a muted color that, at a glance, looked almost like calm.

Lena stayed in her chair long after the others had gone, watching the place on the map where the red had been.
