CRISPR (Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats) is a family of DNA sequences found in the genomes of bacteria and archaea. **Ethical concerns (paraphrased style)**  
- **Human germline editing:** One major concern is editing the human germline (embryos, eggs, sperm, or early-stage embryos), because changes can be inherited by future generations. This raises questions about consent (future individuals can’t consent), long-term safety, and potential social consequences.  
- **“Designer babies”:** There is public and academic debate about the possibility of using CRISPR to select or enhance traits such as appearance, intelligence, or athletic ability, which raises issues of inequality, eugenics, and social pressure.  
- **Off-target effects and safety:** CRISPR can introduce unintended changes (off-target edits). Ethical discussions emphasize the risk of harm if such effects occur in clinical contexts. Thorough testing and oversight are considered necessary.  
- **Access and equity:** There are worries that advanced gene-editing therapies could only be accessible to wealthy individuals or countries, potentially increasing global and social inequality.  
- **Use in non-human organisms:** Ethical considerations also apply to modifying animals and plants, including animal welfare, ecological impacts, and the consequences of releasing genetically modified organisms into the environment (for example, via gene drives).

**Regulation and governance (paraphrased style)**  
- **Varied national policies:** Different countries regulate human gene editing in different ways. Some prohibit germline editing for reproductive purposes, while allowing tightly controlled research on embryos up to a certain developmental stage.  
- **Professional guidelines:** Scientific and medical organizations have issued guidelines urging caution, transparency, and public engagement, especially around human germline editing.  
- **International discussions:** There have been international summits and committees (often mentioned on Wikipedia) that review the scientific state of CRISPR and recommend principles for responsible use, such as limiting clinical applications to serious diseases with no good alternatives and ensuring proper oversight.  
- **Somatic vs. germline distinction:** Many regulatory frameworks distinguish between somatic editing (non-inheritable changes in specific tissues of an individual) and germline editing (heritable changes), with somatic therapies generally viewed as more acceptable under strict regulation.
