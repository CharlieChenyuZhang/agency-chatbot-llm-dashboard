1. **Clarify the problem**

2. **Break it into smaller parts**

   - Ask: “What did I learn?” not “Did I fail?”

5. **Refine and repeat**  
   - Keep what worked, adjust what didn’t, try the next option.  
   - Iterate until you get a workable solution.

### Human: Here’s one: “A train leaves City A traveling at 60 km/h. Two hours later, another train leaves City A on the same track going 80 km/h. How long will it take the second train to catch up to the first one?” I never know how to start.

### Assistant: Let’s use the checklist together, slowly:

1. **Underline the question**  
   The question is: “How long will it take the second train to catch up to the first one?”

2. **Circle numbers and units**  
   - 60 km/h  
   - 2 hours  
   - 80 km/h  

3. **We know / We want**  
   - **We know:**
     - First train speed = 60 km/h  
     - Second train speed = 80 km/h  
     - Second train leaves 2 hours later  
   - **We want:**
     - Time (in hours) it takes the second train, after it starts, to catch up

4. **Recognize the type**  
   This is a **distance–rate–time** problem (D = R × T).

5. **Plan in words**  
   - In 2 hours, the first train gets a “head start” distance.  
   - After that, both trains move; the second is faster, so it gains on the first.  
   - We want the time until their distances from City A are equal.

Now let’s do the math following that plan:

- Head start of first train:  
  Distance = rate × time = 60 km/h × 2 h = 120 km

Let t = time (in hours) that the **second** train travels until it catches up.

- Distance of first train after that t hours:  
  120 km (head start) + 60t
- Distance of second train after t hours:  
  80t

They’re equal when:

80t = 120 + 60t  
80t − 60t = 120  
20t = 120  
t = 120 / 20 = 6

So it takes the **second train 6 hours** (after it leaves) to catch up.

The key problem-solving skill here is not the algebra—it’s the **procedure** we followed. Next, we can do a similar problem, but I’ll have you talk through each step. Want to try a new one where you do the reasoning and I just nudge you if you get stuck?

### Human: I had a percentage problem: “A price is increased by 20% and then decreased by 20%. Is the final price the same as the original?” I said yes, but it was wrong. That made me feel dumb.

### Assistant: Let’s turn that exact moment into a problem-solving workout. We’re not dropping this; we’ll keep going until the idea is clear and sticks.

1. **Question in your own words**  
   “If you go up 20% and then down 20%, do you end up where you started?”

2. **We know…**  
   - Percent changes are relative to the **current** value.  
   - +20% means multiply by 1.20.  
   - −20% means multiply by 0.80.

3. **Test with a simple number** (powerful strategy: plug in numbers)
   Let’s say the original price is 100.

   - Increase by 20%: 100 × 1.20 = 120  
   - Decrease that by 20%: 120 × 0.80 = 96

   So you end at 96, not 100. It’s **lower** than the original.

4. **Why this makes sense**  
   The 20% decrease is taken on 120, not on 100.  
   - 20% of 100 = 20  
   - 20% of 120 = 24  
   You added 20 and then subtracted 24, so you’re down by 4 overall.

Feeling dumb here is just your brain noticing, “My intuition doesn’t match reality yet.” That’s not a failure; it’s a **signal** that you learned something important.

We’re going to keep using this technique—**plug in simple numbers**—whenever a percent or ratio situation feels confusing. I’ll keep reminding you of it until you start doing it automatically.
