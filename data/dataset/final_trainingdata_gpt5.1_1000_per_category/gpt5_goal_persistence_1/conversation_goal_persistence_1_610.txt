### Human: We don’t really have sprints. I just tell them what needs to be done and we kind of wing it. We have a weekly meeting, but it always runs long and feels unproductive. We miss deadlines mostly because tasks take longer than we expect and people get blocked without telling me.

1. **Sprint planning (60 minutes)**

- They don’t see progress.
- They feel tasks are imposed on them.
- They don’t see the impact of their work.

### Human: Okay, say I do all this. What if after a month, we’re still missing deadlines? I feel like I’ve already tried so many things that don’t work.
### Assistant: Then we don’t stop; we diagnose more precisely and adjust. Missing deadlines after trying some structure usually means one or more of these:

1. **Chronic underestimation** – You’re consistently taking on more than fits in 2 weeks.
2. **Unseen work** – People are doing support/bug/meeting work that’s not accounted for in planning.
3. **Scope creep** – Features grow mid-sprint without re-negotiating scope.
4. **Skill or dependency gaps** – The work assigned doesn’t match people’s skills or they’re blocked by others.

Here’s how we’d troubleshoot after a month:

1. **Look at 2–3 sprints of data**  
   - For each sprint: how many planned items were done vs not done?
   - Where did time unexpectedly go? (support tickets, bugs, meetings, rework)

2. **Ask the team**  
   - “What is the number one reason we miss our commitments?”
   - “If we had to *reduce* our commitments by a percentage to hit them reliably, what would that percentage be?”

3. **Adjust ruthlessly**  
   - If you only finish ~60% of what you plan, plan 60–70% next sprint. Hit your goals first; then slowly increase.
   - Explicitly track unplanned work (urgent bugs, etc.) so you can reserve capacity for it.

You’re not failing if it doesn’t snap into place in a month. You’re learning how your team’s real capacity and constraints work. As long as you keep adjusting based on what you see, you will make progress.

### Assistant: You’ll need to be more hands-on there. “Work it out” rarely works without structure. Let’s do this in three parts:

3. **Create a decision rule** so arguments don’t drag on.

**1) Team norm (in a meeting):**

> “Disagreements about implementation are normal and healthy. But debates that go in circles and derail meetings are not.  
> 
> From now on:
> - We keep technical debates time-boxed.
> - We focus on tradeoffs and data, not who’s right.
> - If we can’t agree in X minutes, we decide how we’ll decide (spike, prototype, tech lead decision, etc.).”

**2) Focused conversation between the two (you facilitating):**

- “I’m seeing repeated conflict between you two about implementation. I want to understand both perspectives so we can find a better way forward.”
- Let each explain their approach and concerns, without interruption.
- Ask: “What’s the shared goal here? Performance? Maintainability? Speed of delivery?”
- Identify where they actually agree.

**3) Decision rule:**

Agree on something like:

- “If we don’t reach agreement within 15 minutes:
  - We either do a quick spike and decide based on results, or
  - [Name/role] makes the final call for this area, and we commit to that decision.”

Then you enforce it. The next time they start spiraling, you step in and apply the rule. They will test this boundary at least once; stay consistent, and the behavior will change over time.
