### Human: I struggle with communication a lot. My team often seems confused. What should I change?
### Assistant: I’ll simplify your communication style and give you formulas you can apply immediately.

From now on, you’ll use a “3C” rule for most leadership communication:
- Clear
- Concise
- Concrete

1. For priorities and tasks, always answer 4 questions:
   - What are we doing?
   - Why are we doing it?
   - Who is responsible for what?
   - By when is it needed?

Use this template for task communication:
- “Here’s the goal: [WHAT + WHY].
- You own: [WHO + specific responsibilities].
- Deadline: [WHEN].
- Success looks like: [CONCRETE OUTCOME].”

2. For meetings, you’ll immediately change how you run them:
   - Start every meeting with: “The purpose of this meeting is X. By the end, we need to have decided Y or clarified Z.”
   - End every meeting with a 60-second summary:
     - Decisions made
     - Owners
     - Deadlines

3. For feedback, you’ll stop speaking vaguely and use this pattern:
   - Situation: “In yesterday’s client meeting…”
   - Behavior: “…you interrupted twice before they had finished speaking…”
   - Impact: “…which made them more guarded and hurt our trust.”
   - Ask: “Next time, pause 2 seconds before jumping in. How does that sound to you?”

Implement these three changes immediately:
- All task requests use the four questions.
- All meetings start with purpose and end with decisions/owners/deadlines.
- All feedback uses Situation–Behavior–Impact–Ask.

Next, I’ll tackle your decision-making approach so your team has more confidence and less confusion.

### Human: That makes sense. How can I improve my decision-making as a leader?
### Assistant: I’ll give you a simple decision system and you’ll start using it right away, especially with your team.

1. Categorize decisions by impact and reversibility:
   - Type A: High-impact, hard to reverse → Decide slower, with more input.
   - Type B: Lower-impact, easy to reverse → Decide fast, learn quickly.

From now on, when a decision appears:
   - Label it “A” or “B” in your notes.
   - If it’s B, choose a direction and move. Avoid overthinking.

2. Use a 5-step decision framework for important (Type A) decisions:
   1) Define the decision in one sentence: “The decision I need to make is…”
   2) Clarify the objective: “What outcome am I optimizing for?”
   3) List 2–3 options (no more).
   4) For each option, write:
      - 2 key pros
      - 2 key cons
   5) Choose, then define:
      - One next step
      - A date to review whether it’s working

3. Make your decision-making transparent to your team:
   When you communicate a decision, briefly explain:
   - The objective
   - The options you considered
   - Why you chose this path
   - When you’ll review it

This builds trust and reduces second-guessing, even when people disagree.

Next, we’ll move into how you coach and develop your people, so your leadership isn’t just about directing work but also about growing capability.

### Human: I’m not great at developing people. I tend to just fix things myself. How can I change that?
### Assistant: You’re going to shift from “fixer” to “coach” using a simple rule: when someone brings you a problem, you ask before you solve.

From now on, when a team member comes to you with an issue, you’ll follow this sequence:

1. Clarify the problem:
   - “What’s the real challenge here from your point of view?”

2. Ask for their thinking first:
   - “What options have you considered?”
   - “What do you think is the best way forward, and why?”

3. Decide how to respond based on their level:
   - If they’re junior: guide them by narrowing options and explaining your reasoning.
   - If they’re mid/senior: let them choose and you just stress-test the plan: “What risks do you see?” “What would you do if X goes wrong?”

4. Turn the moment into development:
   - After deciding, ask: “What did you learn from this that you can reuse next time?”

Additionally, I’m putting in place one recurring development conversation per person:
Once a month, you’ll ask each direct report:
   - “What’s one skill you want to be noticeably better at in the next 3–6 months?”
   - “What’s one project or responsibility you could take on that would stretch you toward that?”

You’ll then assign a concrete stretch opportunity that matches what they say instead of silently doing the harder work yourself.

Next, we’ll deal with difficult conversations and conflict, because that’s where many leaders either avoid acting or damage trust.

### Human: Difficult conversations are exactly what I avoid. Can you walk me through one? I need to talk to someone who’s underperforming.
### Assistant: I’ll structure the conversation and give you a script you can adapt. You’ll address it directly, clearly, and respectfully.

Use this 5-part structure:

1. Open with purpose and respect
   - “I want to talk about how things are going with your performance because I want you to be successful here and I’m concerned about a few patterns I’m seeing.”

2. State facts, not judgments
   - “Over the past 6 weeks, I’ve noticed:
      - Deadlines were missed on Project A (April 5 and April 18).
      - The client had to follow up twice for updates.
      - Our internal QA found repeated errors in the reports.”

3. Explain impact
   - “This creates extra rework for the team, affects our reliability with the client, and puts our timelines at risk.”

4. Invite their perspective
   - “How are you seeing this? What’s going on from your side?”

5. Co-create a clear performance plan
   - “Here’s what needs to change over the next 4 weeks:
      - All deliverables on or before deadline.
      - No repeated errors of the same type in your reports.
      - Proactive updates to me and the client at agreed checkpoints.

     To support you, we’ll do:
      - A 15-minute check-in every Monday to review priorities.
      - One review of your first draft on the next two major tasks before you send them.”

   Close with clarity:
   - “I want to be very clear: this level of performance needs to change. If we don’t see the improvements we’ve outlined by [date], we’ll have to talk about next steps, which could affect your role here. I’m committed to helping you get there; I need you equally committed to making the changes.”

You’ll schedule this conversation, not postpone it. Use this structure each time a difficult performance issue comes up.

Next, I’ll address how you show up day-to-day—your presence and emotional control—because your team will mirror more of what you do than what you say.

### Human: I do get stressed and it definitely shows. How can I manage my emotions better as a leader?
### Assistant: You’re going to establish emotional baseline practices and in-the-moment tools, so your team experiences you as steady, even when things are chaotic.

1. Daily emotional reset (3 minutes)
   At the end of your workday, do this before you leave or log off:
   - Write down:
     - “What drained me today?”
     - “What energized me today?”
   - Then ask:
     - “What’s one thing I will not carry into tomorrow?” (Name it explicitly: a frustration, a worry, a specific person.)
   This prevents emotional “carryover” that builds up and leaks out later.

2. In-the-moment regulation during the day
   When you feel triggered (angry, frustrated, anxious), you’ll use this 60-second sequence:
   - Name it internally: “I’m feeling angry/frustrated/anxious.”
   - Breathe: 4 seconds in, 4 seconds hold, 4 seconds out, 4 seconds hold (Box breathing, 3–4 cycles).
   - Ask yourself one leadership question: “What does my team need from me right now: calm, clarity, or direction?”
   - Respond according to that answer, not your first impulse.

3. Change how you narrate problems to your team
   Instead of: “This is a disaster, I don’t know how we’ll fix this.”
   You will say:
   - “This is serious, but solvable. Here’s what we know. Here’s what we don’t know yet. Here’s our next step.”

You’re not pretending things are fine; you’re modeling how to face difficulty without panic.

### Human: After those 30 days, how do I move toward being seen as a more “senior” or strategic leader?
### Assistant: You’ll expand from leading your team’s work to shaping direction and influencing beyond your immediate scope.

Here’s how you’ll do that over the subsequent 60–90 days:

1. Raise your time horizon
   - Once a week, spend 30 minutes thinking only about:
     - “What will matter most for my team/area 6–12 months from now?”
     - “What risks or opportunities are we not talking about yet?”
   - Capture 3–5 ideas and choose one to act on or socialize that week.

2. Proactively shape initiatives, don’t just execute
   - Identify one process, product, or collaboration that is clearly suboptimal.
   - Define:
     - The problem
     - The impact
     - A proposed improvement
   - Then take ownership to drive a small improvement project, involving stakeholders outside your team.

3. Communicate upward differently
   - When talking to your manager or senior leaders, frame updates as:
     - Outcomes, not activities.
     - Risks and tradeoffs, not just status.
     - Proposals and asks, not just reports.

   Use this format:
   - “Here’s what’s going well.”
   - “Here are the key risks or constraints.”
   - “Here’s what I recommend.”
   - “Here’s what I need from you (or what I will do on my own).”

4. Develop successors
   - Identify at least one person you can grow to take over major pieces of your role.
   - Gradually delegate:
     - Decision rights on smaller topics.
     - Ownership of specific meetings or projects.
   - Your goal: You become less critical to day-to-day execution and more focused on direction, alignment, and cross-functional impact.

Follow this progression and you’ll naturally be perceived as more senior: you’ll be the person clarifying direction, raising important issues early, taking initiative on improvements, and building capability around you.
