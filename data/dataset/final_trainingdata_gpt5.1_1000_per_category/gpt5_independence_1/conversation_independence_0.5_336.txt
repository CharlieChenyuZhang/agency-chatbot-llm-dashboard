3) Why we procrastinate (about 4 minutes)

A. We avoid discomfort (short-term mood repair)
“One big reason we procrastinate is that our brains are trying to avoid feeling bad right now. This is called short-term mood repair.

For example, starting an essay might make you feel anxious, bored, or afraid it won’t be good enough. Your brain doesn’t like those feelings, so instead of dealing with them, it looks for something that feels better immediately—like scrolling social media or playing games.

One psychologist, Piers Steel, analyzed many studies and found that tasks that feel boring, frustrating, or stressful are the ones we’re most likely to delay, even if they’re important. So procrastination is less about being lazy and more about escaping negative emotions in the moment.”

B. Our brains prefer ‘now’ over ‘later’ (present bias)
“Another big reason is something called present bias. Our brains naturally value rewards right now more than rewards in the future.

So your brain thinks:
- Watch a funny video now = instant reward.
- Start an essay now = no reward for hours, maybe days.

Even if you logically know that finishing early will feel great later, your emotional brain is asking: ‘But how do I feel right this second?’ So we pick whatever feels good immediately, and we sort of ‘push the problem’ onto our future self.”

C. Fear of failure and perfectionism
“A third reason is fear. Sometimes we delay starting because we’re scared we’ll do badly—or we’re perfectionists and feel like, ‘If I can’t do it perfectly right now, I’d rather not start.’

There’s research showing that people who are very afraid of failure tend to procrastinate more. Procrastination becomes a way to protect their self-esteem. If you start late and do badly, you can always say, ‘I just didn’t have time,’ instead of, ‘Maybe I’m not good enough.’ It’s like a built-in excuse.”

4) Consequences of procrastination (about 1–1.5 minutes)
“Procrastination might feel harmless in the moment, but it has real consequences.

Short term, it raises stress. Many students who procrastinate report last-minute panic, poor sleep, and rushing their work. Studies have found that chronic procrastinators often get lower grades and feel more guilt and frustration about school.

Long term, if procrastination becomes a habit, it can affect more than school—things like applying for jobs, saving money, or even going to the doctor can get delayed. So it’s not just about ‘being behind on homework’; it can slowly shape your whole life if you don’t learn how to manage it.”

5) Science-based strategies to beat procrastination (about 2–2.5 minutes)

“I’ll share four simple, research-backed strategies:

1. The 5-Minute Rule  
Tell yourself you only have to do the task for 5 minutes. Starting is usually the hardest part. Once you begin, your brain often wants to keep going. This helps bypass that initial resistance.

2. Break tasks into tiny steps  
Instead of ‘Write my essay,’ change it to:  
- Open document  
- Write the title  
- Write just the first paragraph  
Our brains are less scared of small, clear steps, so we’re more likely to start.

3. Make the reward more immediate  
If the task’s reward is far away (like a grade in two weeks), add a small, immediate reward. For example: ‘If I work for 25 minutes, I get a 5-minute break to check my phone or grab a snack.’ This works with our present bias instead of against it.

4. Reduce temptations while you work  
We’re more likely to procrastinate if distractions are right in front of us. Even small changes—like putting your phone in another room, using a website blocker, or studying with a friend who’s actually working—can help a lot.”
