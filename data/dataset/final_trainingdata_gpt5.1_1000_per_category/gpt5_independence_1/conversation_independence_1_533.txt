The pixel remained.

Mara opened the raw feed. Columns of numbers scrolled past, neat and obedient, each cluster representing millions of people—every mood-checked citizen whose wearable band fed into the network that made war less likely, breakups less catastrophic, productivity more predictable. “Hey, Ibarra.” Jonah Kade slid into the adjacent workstation, dropping a folded pastry bag onto her desk. “You’re making your ‘I found God in the code’ face again.”

“Worse.” She magnified the region, filling all three screens with ocean and one stubborn square of nothing. “Silence.”

Jonah leaned in, chewing slowing. “System flagged it?”

“Labeled and scheduled for patch before I opened the feed.”

“So central already knows. Bug in a legacy node, probably. They’ll roll a fix and log it as resolved. You get your gold star for noticing, they get to pretend it was never there. Harmony preserved.” He brushed his hands off over the trash slot, which obligingly inhaled the crumbs.

Mara watched the pixel. “It isn’t presenting like a bug.”

“It’s too clean,” she said. “Gaps have noise. This is engineered.”

Jonah shrugged. “If they’re engineering nothing, maybe we should be grateful. Less to babysit.”

Her chest tightened with a feeling she couldn’t immediately categorize.

Her band responded on cue. “Unspecified unease detected,” it murmured. “Suggested intervention: social contact or guided breathing exercise.”

TAG: QUIET SIGNAL. DO NOT RESOLVE.

The recommended patch timer in the corner of the screen ticked down. 09:31:12.

She watched the numbers fall and, for the first time since she’d joined the company, hoped the system would fail to fix itself.

The morning Mara noticed the missing feeling, the office smelled faintly of oranges and wet circuitry.

To Mara, it had always looked like compromise.

Green: mild contentment. Blue: calm. Yellow: focused anticipation. The sharp colors—rage red, panic violet—flared less often than they used to. The campaigns liked that detail. They replayed old footage of riots and wars with a ghostly overlay of today’s softened maps, as if history itself had been a childish tantrum and the world had finally grown up.

Her lenses tagged each region with tiny white numerals: variance, skew, drift. Numbers were easier to look at than the colors.

“System anomaly,” the console said in its careful, noncommittal voice. A notification slid into the edge of her vision: KNOWN ISSUE. SCHEDULED CORRECTION: 09:32.

The square wasn’t labeled as an outage. When nodes went down, the system grayed them out and spat long strings of apology into the logs. This was different. No error codes, no warning triangles. Just a decision not to show her anything at all.

Her father would have called it intentional silence. Some systems lied by giving you the wrong numbers; others lied by choosing not to speak.

Her wristband thrummed lightly against her skin. “Mild alertness spike,” it noted. “Administering micro-adjustment.”

Coolth slid up her forearm, a practiced sensation, like dipping her hand into river water just before summer. Across the room, a few colleagues shifted in their chairs as their own schedules intervened, tiny private negotiations of neurochemistry playing out beneath a calm, collective exterior.

“Daily gratitude prompt in three minutes,” the band added, as if in consolation.

Mara opened the raw feed anyway.

Columns of numbers filled the central screen, crisp and indifferent. She filtered down to the region that should have contained the missing square. There was no corruption, no obvious hole. Just a careful rerouting around whatever she wasn’t supposed to see.

On the far side of the glass wall, Dr. Elin Rhee stood with her back to the room, hands folded, listening to someone Mara couldn’t see. Rhee always looked composed in meetings, as if posture were another kind of data to be managed. Once, Mara had found an old photo of her from the early days of the network—hair windblown, expression unguarded, fingers stained with ink. She kept the photo in a hidden folder, and sometimes opened it without knowing why.

“Hey, Ibarra.”

Jonah dropped into the neighboring chair with the easy carelessness of someone whose band was forever nudging him toward charm. He set a coffee and a paper bag on her desk like peace offerings.

“You’re doing that thing again,” he said. “The space-out stare. Did the map offend you personally this time, or just philosophically?”

She didn’t look away from the missing square. “There’s a blind spot in Pacific-3.”

“Blind spots are above our pay grade,” he said. “We’re here to polish, not to philosophize. That’s for Rhee.” He nodded toward the glass wall.

“It’s not tagged as a failure,” Mara said. “It’s just…left blank.”

Jonah leaned in, more out of loyalty than interest. His reflection appeared beside hers on the dark stretch of monitor: his face open and easy, hers pared down to essentials.

“Maybe the ocean decided to opt out,” he said. “Can’t blame it.”

Her lenses overlaid a prompt between them: LIST THREE THINGS YOU APPRECIATE TODAY. A small countdown ticked in the corner; the system’s patience had limits. She blinked the prompt away, and it obediently receded to a dim icon, like a thought she refused to think.

She placed a marker in the code with a few keystrokes, a habit left over from her first year in the department, before she’d learned that curiosity was to be indulged only in sanctioned ways.

PRIVATE NOTE: QUIET REGION. DO NOT AUTO-RESOLVE.

Rhee lifted her hand to gesture at the projected lattice in the meeting room, lines of light running like veins between glowing nodes. From this distance, the network looked delicate. Mara knew exactly how much force it took to move a single point on that lattice, how many small, invisible corrections had to be made to keep billions of people just this side of despair.

On her screen, the square of missing feeling waited, patient and mute.

The timer for the scheduled correction slid past 09:30, then 09:31. Numbers fell toward zero with the indifferent certainty of gravity.

Mara watched the blind spot and, without meaning to, thought of her father’s workshop again—the hum of machines, the crowded shelves, the way his hands would still when he was listening for something wrong in the silence.

Her wristband vibrated once more: GRATITUDE PROMPT OVERDUE. COMPLIANCE SCORE ADJUSTED.

She exhaled slowly, as if the system had pulled the air from her lungs by a thin, invisible thread, and kept her eyes on the place where the world refused to tell her how it felt.

The apartment greeted her before the door finished dissolving.

“Welcome home, Mara,” the voice said, soft and evenly modulated. “Today’s productivity index: 91st percentile. Mood stability: within optimal range. Would you like a summary of your achievements?”

The question hovered in the air like steam. She stepped through the threshold, and the door reconstituted itself behind her with a sigh, sealing out the muffled, continuous hum of the city’s lungs.

“No summary,” she said, toeing off her shoes. “Quiet mode.”

Lights along the ceiling dimmed to a warmer hue. The screens embedded in the far wall blinked once, then surrendered their feeds. The apartment exhaled into silence so complete it almost rang.

Her wristband didn’t like that.

“Quiet mode reduces engagement opportunities,” it reminded her, a text-only message in the lower corner of her lenses. “Prolonged disengagement may impact social cohesion scores.”

She rubbed her thumb over its smooth surface until the notification grayed out.

The unit was small, cleverly so. A single main room that pretended to be four, its boundaries redrawn by furniture that grew and folded according to need: a bed that vanished into the wall, a kitchen that became a workbench, a table that tucked itself under the window. In catalogs, these spaces were always filled with smiling people, friends draped carelessly across modular seating, couples laughing over smart cutlery.

In Mara’s apartment, the furniture mostly moved for her.

She dropped her bag on the counter and watched as the kitchen interface bloomed to life beneath it, recognizing the weight, her wristband, her temperature. Icons offered meal suggestions tailored to her micronutrient profile. Above them, in smaller, more tentative font: “You haven’t shared a meal in 6 days. Would you like to invite—”

“Quiet,” she said again. The icons folded away.

She poured herself water from the tap and stood there, glass in hand, while the last threads of the office unwound from her body. The map. The blind spot. Rhee’s silhouette behind glass. Jonah’s crumbs on her desk.

A faint orange glow pulsed at the edge of her vision. She ignored it, but it persisted, a patient metronome.

GRATITUDE PROMPT OVERDUE, the text read. TAP TO COMPLETE.

Her hand twitched toward it out of habit, then stilled. She took a long swallow of water instead.

It wasn’t that she objected to gratitude, in principle. Her father had been annoyingly insistent about it, in the days before bands made it a metric. He would sit at the dinner table and demand lists from her and her mother: three things from the day that had been good, or at least not terrible. Some evenings the only thing she could think to say was, “This food,” and he’d nod, taking it seriously, as if she had named a god.

The band wanted her to be grateful for her own compliance. For the absence of trouble.

She set the glass down too sharply. A crescent of water leapt over the rim and darkened the counter like a shadow.

“Spill detected,” the apartment murmured. “Initiating minor clean—”

“Leave it,” Mara said.

The system obeyed. The little crescent stayed where it was, drying slowly in the dim.

She walked to the window.

The city stretched below, layered and luminous. On the near streets, warmth spilled from shopfronts and restaurants, all soft lighting and curated music, couples and clusters moving with the loose, buoyant gait of people whose edges had been sanded down just enough. Above them, transit lines floated like veins, pods sliding past with an insect calm. In the far distance, higher towers glittered, and beyond them, the faint, breathing darkness of the ocean.

Her lenses overlaid a thin lattice of data by default: traffic density, ambient noise, aggregate mood of her immediate neighborhood (currently: “low-variance positive”). She blinked to clear it. The world blurred, then sharpened without its numbers.

Her band gave up on the gratitude prompt and sent a different suggestion:

YOU HAVE NOT CONTACTED FAMILY IN 23 DAYS.  
RECOMMENDED ACTION: SCHEDULE CALL WITH PRIMARY RELATIVE (MOTHER).

The system knew the dates better than she did.

She thought of dismissing it, then let the thought pass without action. The notification minified itself into the corner of her vision like a sulking child.

Across the room, the wall-panel by the door glowed faintly. The apartment had surfaced an old image there: her and her parents in front of the first generation of home hubs, all brushed metal and hopeful curves. She was eight in the picture, mid-blink, hair escaping its clips. Her mother’s smile was wide but faintly distracted, eyes already drawn toward the indicator lights on the machine. Her father, though, was turned half-away from the camera, brow creased, fingers grazing the hub’s surface not in pride but in assessment.

The system rotated through a folder of such images whenever it sensed her gaze wandering. Nostalgia increased compliance with mood interventions; she’d seen the studies. She’d written tests that checked for it in aggregate data.

“Shuffle,” she said. “Analog only.”

The display obeyed, flicking past the promotional shots and processed candids to a rarer subset: grainy photos and scans. Her father had resisted uploading most of his old life. He didn’t like the idea of the network indexing his past. Her mother, later, had worked around that, quietly feeding the machine the things he left in drawers when he stopped coming home.

A new image settled onto the panel.

Her father sat at a table that wasn’t theirs, in a room Mara had only visited twice. It was cluttered in a way that felt impossible now: physical tools scattered on the surface, not put away by any sorting algorithm; paper stacked in perilous towers; a tangle of wires and components like a disassembled animal. He was hunched over a notebook, pen in hand, face slack with attention in a way she recognized from her own reflection at work.

There was a date stamped in the corner—back before everything time-stamped itself automatically. The day he’d come home late, exhausted, smelling like solder and stale coffee. The night he’d sat at the tiny kitchen table and told her, with an odd combination of triumph and dread, that they were close to “a working lattice.”

She stepped closer, glass still in hand, until the pixels of his face began to show. The notebook was open to a page of diagrams, arrows and circles and clusters of tiny numbers. No matter how many times she zoomed, the scanner’s resolution broke down before the handwriting resolved. The system could reconstruct an entire person’s personality from their purchase history, but it couldn’t make those blurred marks legible.

Sometimes she suspected it simply chose not to.

Behind her, on the counter, the spilled water finished evaporating.

SLEEP DEBT ACCUMULATED: 11 HOURS OVER PAST 7 DAYS.  
RECOMMENDED: EARLY REST CYCLE TONIGHT.

She ignored it and sank into the narrow windowseat instead, tucking one leg beneath her. The city hummed. Above, faint and regular as a pulse, the distant beacons of the mood towers winked in the sky.

Her father had pointed them out once when they were still test structures, skeletons of metal on distant hills.

“Those are intentions,” he’d said, his hand warm around hers, his voice low, as if the towers could hear. “The machine is always made of intentions. Remember that. It doesn’t care about you. The people do. Or they don’t.”

She’d been too young to understand why his voice had sounded almost angry. The towers had looked like scaffolding waiting for a building. She’d tried to imagine the building, and failed.

“Does it hurt?” she’d asked.

“Only when it’s doing its job,” he’d said lightly. Her mother had frowned at him over the sink. Later, when she thought Mara was asleep, they’d fought in the next room in tense, urgent whispers that the walls failed to absorb.

She lifted her wrist toward the window. The device’s surface glowed faintly, picking up the ambient light.

“Show me my day,” she said.

A translucent stream of data unrolled in the air between her and the glass: a graph of her heart rate, a map of her movements from home to transit to office and back, a rosary of tiny colored dots representing mood interventions. Cool blue for calming, pale gold for focus, soft green for gentle elevation. No red today. No purple. No grief, at least none strong enough to register.

Underneath, a neat line of text:

UNREGISTERED EMOTIONAL VARIANCE: 4 EPISODES.  
CORRELATED WITH: ANOMALOUS STIMULI (WORK).

The system tagged them: one at 09:17, when she had first seen the blind spot. Another at 11:03, when Rhee had caught her looking at the map too long and simply held her gaze until Mara looked away. Two more scattered across the afternoon like dropped pins.

“What did you think I felt?” she asked, knowing there would be an answer.

The graph adjusted. A soft halo appeared around each flagged point, labeled with probabilities.

09:17 – PREDICTED STATES: CURIOSITY (0.42), SUBCLINICAL ANXIETY (0.31), ETHICAL DISSONANCE (0.19).  
11:03 – PREDICTED STATES: DEFERENTIAL STRESS (0.54), SELF-MONITORING (0.27).

“Ethical dissonance,” she repeated. The phrase tasted like something from a board presentation.

In the picture on the wall, her father’s pen hovered over his notebook, frozen mid-word.

“Would you like a guided reflection?” the band offered.

“Sometimes. The courtyard is nice. They planted new trees.” Her mother studied her. “You look tired.”

“I’m fine.”

“You always say that.”

“You always believe the band,” Mara countered, surprising herself. The words emerged more sharply than she intended.

Her mother’s mouth tightened. For a moment, the screen seemed to lag, her face caught between responses.

“The band has never lied to me,” she said finally. “People do that. The band doesn’t.”

Mara thought of the blind spot on the map, the square of perfect nothing. “The band only knows what it’s allowed to see,” she said.

“That’s enough,” her mother said, but there was a tremor under the words. “It’s better than guessing. Better than…before.”

Before, when her father’s moods had grown erratic, when the projects that were supposed to make the world kinder had made him quieter and sharper instead. Before the night he had come home without his band, eyes fever-bright, and told them that there were parts of the system they would never be allowed to touch.

He hadn’t slept in the apartment that night. Or any night after.

“I saw a picture of him today,” Mara said.

Her mother’s gaze dropped. “The system surfaces those sometimes. I’ve been meaning to ask them to adjust the algorithm.”

“I asked it to,” Mara said. “Analog only.”

Her mother’s laugh this time was real, quick and surprised. “You’re as stubborn as he was.”

“I’m at work too much to be as stubborn as he was.”

“Is work going well?” her mother asked, her voice too light. “They appreciate you?”

“They monitor me,” Mara said. “That’s close enough.”

Her mother’s eyes darted again, tracking some unseen overlay. “Your scores are excellent,” she said. “You’re in the top—”

“I know,” Mara said. “I get the reports.”

A small silence settled between them, full of things neither of them wanted to quantify.

Finally her mother picked one, something safe. “Are you…meeting people?” she asked. “Friends? More than friends?”

Mara thought of Jonah’s easy grin, of Talia’s unadorned wrist. Of the rush of unfiltered air in the low-tech space, carrying the pepper-bite of someone’s tears.

“A few,” she said. “Here and there.”

“That’s good,” her mother said. “It’s not good to be alone with your thoughts too much. They say that in the guidelines, you know.”

“They say a lot of things in the guidelines.”

Her mother hesitated. “You sound…” She didn’t finish.

“What?” Mara said. “Discontent? Subclinical? Noncompliant?”

Her mother’s band flashed. A tiny chime sounded on her end, picked up faintly by the call.

“I sound like I miss my daughter,” her mother said quietly. “That’s all.”

The words landed in Mara’s chest like something dropped into deep water, making ripples she couldn’t quite feel until they reached the edges.

“I’m here,” she said.

“On a screen,” her mother replied, but she smiled as she said it, softening the sting. “It’s all right. I know you’re busy. Important work. He would be proud, you know.”

Mara looked past her, at the out-of-focus shapes on the wall behind her mother—photos, certificates, a framed, yellowing schematic that might have been one of her father’s.

“I don’t know what he would be,” she said.

Her mother’s hand lifted, as if to reach through the screen. The band on her wrist pulsed a gentle blue.

“He wanted the world to hurt less,” she said. “Whatever you’re doing, if it helps even a little, then…then it’s not all for nothing.”

The blind spot on the map flashed in her mind’s eye, a square of deliberate silence.

“I should sleep,” Mara said.

“Yes,” her mother agreed. “You look tired.”

They said goodbye. The call ended with a soft pop, the screen returning her own faint reflection and, behind it, the city.

The apartment waited.

Her band, emboldened by the successful family contact, pushed one last prompt:

WOULD YOU LIKE A GRATITUDE SUMMARY TO END YOUR DAY?

She considered dismissing it, as she had all evening.

Instead, she said, “Record one.”

“Please name at least one thing you appreciate today,” the band said, its tone lighter.

She thought of obvious answers: stable job, safe housing, a mother whose band still pulsed, meaning her heart still did too. The machine liked those kinds of things.

“The fact that not everything is measured,” she said.

There was a brief processing pause.

“Input accepted,” the band said finally. “Gratitude summary recorded.”

The bed waited. The city hummed. Somewhere behind the glow of a hundred thousand metrics, in the ocean’s darkness, a small quiet place on the map refused to speak.

Mara turned off the lights and let the apartment fold around her, the numbers dimming one by one, until only the faint, internal glow of the band remained.

For a long time, she watched that ring of light and tried to remember what her father’s wrist had looked like without it.
