Slide 3 – Who Gains, Who Loses?  
“AI is often sold as a win–win, but the benefits and harms are very uneven.  
On one side, large companies use AI to cut costs, optimize logistics, and target customers more precisely. That usually boosts their profits and market power.  
On the other side, many workers—especially in routine office, retail, and manual jobs—face automation or heavy monitoring. Some lose jobs; others see their work broken into gig-style tasks with fewer protections.  
Because the biggest gains flow to a small set of tech firms and data-rich corporations, AI can deepen existing economic inequality and give a handful of players disproportionate influence over markets and even public policy.”

Slide 4 – Invisible Bias in “Smart” Decisions  
“AI systems now help decide who gets a job interview, a loan, extra police attention, or access to public benefits.  
These systems learn from historical data—data that reflects our past biases and unfairness. When that bias is baked into the training data, AI quietly reproduces it at scale.  
The problem is made worse by ‘black-box’ models: companies don’t reveal how their systems work, and even specialists can’t always explain individual decisions.  
From the outside, the results can look neutral and objective, but they may be systematically harsher to certain groups, with very little transparency or recourse.”

Slide 5 – The Cost to Our Privacy  
“AI feeds on data. Our phones, apps, cameras, and online services constantly collect information about where we are, what we do, and what we care about.  
That data is combined into detailed profiles that predict our behavior—what we’re likely to click, buy, or believe.  
The more accurately systems can predict us, the easier it becomes to target advertising and messages that push our emotional buttons.  
Over time, this undermines privacy in a deeper sense: we lose not only control over our data, but also some control over how our preferences and choices are shaped.”

Title: AI, Power, and Privacy – What Every Citizen Should Know

1. What is AI and why does it matter?  
Artificial intelligence (AI) refers to computer systems that can perform tasks that usually require human intelligence: recognizing patterns, understanding language, and making predictions. AI already influences what we see online, how companies treat us as customers, and how governments make decisions about security, welfare, and policing. It is not a distant future technology; it is part of our daily environment.

2. Key risks for ordinary people  
– Job disruption: Many routine and some skilled jobs are being automated or heavily monitored, while the gains mainly benefit large corporations.  
– Hidden bias: AI can reinforce discrimination in hiring, lending, and law enforcement when it learns from biased historical data.  
– Loss of privacy: Constant data collection creates detailed profiles of individuals, enabling precise targeting and manipulation.  
– Concentration of power: A small group of tech firms and state agencies control powerful AI tools with limited public oversight.  
– Democratic concerns: Algorithmic feeds and recommendation systems can amplify misinformation, polarize debate, and subtly shape opinions.

3. Principles for responsible AI  
– Transparency: People should know when AI is used and have access to understandable explanations.  
– Accountability: Organizations must be answerable for AI-driven harms, with real avenues for appeal and correction.  
– Privacy protection: Strict limits on data collection, retention, and sharing are essential.  
– Fairness: Systems should be regularly audited to detect and reduce discriminatory outcomes.  
– Human control: Critical decisions about rights and opportunities should not be left entirely to opaque algorithms.

4. What you can do  
– Ask institutions: “Are you using AI? How does it affect me? How is it checked for bias and errors?”  
– Support regulation: Back policies that strengthen data protection, algorithmic transparency, and worker protections.  
– Be data-aware: Use privacy settings, think critically about what you share, and diversify your information sources.  
– Stay engaged: Follow reliable reporting on AI and participate in local or civic discussions about technology and rights.
