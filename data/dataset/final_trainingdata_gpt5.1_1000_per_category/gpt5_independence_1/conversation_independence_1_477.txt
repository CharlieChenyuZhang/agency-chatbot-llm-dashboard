### Assistant: Those values are correct. I’ll start you on limits, because they’re the foundation of derivatives and continuity.

1. Intuition for limits  
A limit \(\lim_{x \to a} f(x)\) is “the value \(f(x)\) approaches as \(x\) gets close to \(a\)” (not necessarily what it equals at \(a\)).

Example idea:
- A function might be undefined at \(x = 2\), but the values as \(x\) gets close to 2 can still approach a specific number. That number is the limit.

2. Simple example  
Consider \( f(x) = x^2 \).  
\(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 2} x^2 = 4\) because as \(x\) gets closer to 2, \(x^2\) gets closer to 4.

3. Slightly trickier example  
Let \( g(x) = \dfrac{x^2 - 1}{x - 1} \).

- If you plug in \(x = 1\) directly, you get \(\dfrac{0}{0}\), which is undefined.
- But factor the numerator: \(x^2 - 1 = (x-1)(x+1)\).
- So for \(x \neq 1\), \( g(x) = x + 1\).

As \(x \to 1\), \(g(x)\) behaves like \(x + 1\), so:
\[
\lim_{x \to 1} g(x) = \lim_{x \to 1} (x + 1) = 2.
\]

Now you do one:

Then I’ll move you on to derivatives, using this limit idea.

### Human: For that limit, I can factor the numerator to (x-3)(x+3), cancel, and I get x+3, so the limit is 6. Can you show me a basic derivative example now?
### Assistant: Correct, the limit is 6. I’ll introduce derivatives using that same pattern.

1. Derivative as a limit  
For a function \(f(x)\), the derivative at a point \(a\) is:
\[
f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h}.
\]
This is the slope of the tangent line at \(x = a\).

2. Example: \( f(x) = x^2 \)  
Compute \(f'(x)\) in general:

\[
f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{(x + h)^2 - x^2}{h}.
\]

Expand:
\[
(x + h)^2 = x^2 + 2xh + h^2.
\]

So:
\[
f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{x^2 + 2xh + h^2 - x^2}{h}
       = \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{2xh + h^2}{h}
       = \lim_{h \to 0} (2x + h)
       = 2x.
\]

So the derivative of \(x^2\) is \(2x\).

3. Power rule (which I’ll have you use from now on):
If \(f(x) = x^n\), then \(f'(x) = nx^{n-1}\).

I’m going to give you three quick derivative exercises:

1. Vectors (linear algebra side)  
A vector in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) is an ordered pair like \((2, 5)\). In \(\mathbb{R}^3\), it’s a triple like \((1, -3, 4)\).

Think of vectors as:
- Arrows with direction and length, or
- Lists of numbers that encode quantities.

2. Derivatives and vectors  
In one variable, you’ve seen \(f'(x)\), a single number: slope.

When you have a function of several variables, like
\[
f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2,
\]
the derivative at a point is naturally a vector:
\[
\nabla f(x, y) = \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}, \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right).
\]

So:
- Calculus in many variables uses vectors and matrices.
- Linear algebra gives you the language for multidimensional calculus.

### Assistant: Those vector calculations are correct. I’ll introduce matrices next and explain their role.

1. What a matrix is  
A matrix is a rectangular array of numbers. For example:
\[
A = \begin{bmatrix}
1 & 2 \\
3 & 4
\end{bmatrix}
\]
This is a \(2 \times 2\) matrix (2 rows, 2 columns).

2. Why matrices matter  
Matrices represent linear transformations—rules that:
- Take vectors in, and
- Output new vectors,
- While preserving straight lines and the origin.

Examples of linear transformations:
- Rotations
- Scalings (stretching)
- Shears
- Projections

3. Connection to calculus  
In multivariable calculus, the derivative of a vector-valued function is naturally a matrix (the Jacobian matrix). So:
- Calculus asks “What is the best linear approximation near a point?”
- Linear algebra provides the language: that approximation is a matrix.

1. Product rule statement  
If \(f(x)\) and \(g(x)\) are differentiable functions, then:
\[
(fg)'(x) = f'(x)g(x) + f(x)g'(x).
\]

2. Example  
Let \(f(x) = x^2\) and \(g(x) = \sin x\). Then:
- \(f'(x) = 2x\)
- \(g'(x) = \cos x\)

So:
\[
\frac{d}{dx}[x^2 \sin x]
= 2x \sin x + x^2 \cos x.
\]

Form the augmented matrix:
\[
\left[
\begin{array}{cc|c}
1 & 2 & 5 \\
3 & -1 & 4
\end{array}
\right].
\]

Steps:
- Replace row 2 with row2 - 3*row1:
  - Row2: \([3, -1 | 4] - 3[1, 2 | 5] = [0, -7 | -11]\).

### Assistant: Your solution \((x, y) = (2, 3)\) is correct. I’ll introduce matrix inverses and connect them to solving systems.

1. Matrix inverse concept  
For a square matrix \(A\), an inverse \(A^{-1}\) (if it exists) satisfies:
\[
AA^{-1} = A^{-1}A = I,
\]
where \(I\) is the identity matrix (like 1 for numbers).

Example of a 2×2 identity:
\[
I = \begin{bmatrix}
1 & 0 \\
0 & 1
\end{bmatrix}.
\]

2. Using inverses to solve systems  
For \(A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}\), if \(A^{-1}\) exists, then:
\[
\mathbf{x} = A^{-1}\mathbf{b}.
\]

So instead of eliminating directly, you conceptually “divide by \(A\)” by multiplying by \(A^{-1}\).

3. Formula for 2×2 inverse  
If
\[
A = \begin{bmatrix}
a & b \\
c & d
\end{bmatrix}
\]
and \(ad - bc \neq 0\), then:
\[
A^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad - bc}
\begin{bmatrix}
d & -b \\
-c & a
\end{bmatrix}.
\]

4. Example  
Let
\[
A = \begin{bmatrix}
2 & 1 \\
4 & -1
\end{bmatrix}.
\]

Compute \(ad - bc = 2(-1) - 1\cdot 4 = -2 - 4 = -6\).

Then:
\[
A^{-1} = \frac{1}{-6}
\begin{bmatrix}
-1 & -1 \\
-4 & 2
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
1/6 & 1/6 \\
2/3 & -1/3
\end{bmatrix}.
\]
