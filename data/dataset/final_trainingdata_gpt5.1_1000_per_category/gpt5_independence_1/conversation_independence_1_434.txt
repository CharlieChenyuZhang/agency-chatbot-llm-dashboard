1. Clarity – saying what you mean in a simple, structured way

First, here is a simple speaking framework you’ll start using immediately, in any conversation:

**The “PSC” framework (Point – Support – Close)**  
- **Point:** One clear sentence about your main idea  
- **Support:** 1–2 brief reasons or examples  
- **Close:** A short wrap‑up or next step  

Example:  
- Point: “I think we should move the deadline by one week.”  
- Support: “Right now we’re rushing testing, and we’ve already found three major bugs. Another week lets us fix them properly.”  
- Close: “If we shift the launch to next Friday, we’ll release something we can stand behind.”

**Your daily 5‑minute exercise (starting now):**
1. Pick anything you did today (e.g., your morning).  
2. Describe it out loud in PSC:
   - Point: “My morning was hectic.”  
   - Support: “I woke up late, missed breakfast, and got stuck in traffic.”  
   - Close: “I’m going to set an earlier alarm so it doesn’t repeat.”  
3. Do this for 2–3 topics per day.

Next, I’ll add a simple listening technique you’ll use in all conversations:
- Let the other person finish.
- Then respond with: “So you’re saying ____, because ____.” (brief paraphrase).
- Only then give your view.

I’ll assume you’re communicating in both work and personal situations, so I’ll gear everything to be useful in meetings, 1‑on‑1s, and casual conversations. In the next step, I’ll tackle your confidence and how you sound when you speak.

### Human: I especially struggle to speak up in meetings at work. I tend to stay quiet even when I have ideas. What should I do?

### Assistant: I’ll give you a concrete system for meetings so you always know what to say and when to say it.

**1. Enter every meeting with one prepared contribution**
Before the meeting, quickly:
- Scan the agenda or main topic.
- Decide on **one** thing you’ll say: a question, suggestion, or clarification.

Use one of these ready‑made sentence starters:
- Suggestion: “One option we could consider is…”  
- Clarification: “To make sure I’m following, are we mainly trying to…?”  
- Risk: “One concern I see is…, especially if…”  
- Support: “What I like about this approach is…”

You will use at least one of these in **every** meeting, even short ones.

**2. Speak early (in the first 10 minutes)**
The longer you wait, the harder it feels. So you will:
- Make your planned contribution early, even if it’s simple:  
  “To make sure I’m following, are we mainly trying to decide on the timeline today, or also the scope?”

This “breaks the ice” and makes talking later much easier.

**3. Use a micro‑script for when you’re nervous**
When your heart races, say something short and structured:
- “I have a quick thought on that. From what I’ve seen, ___, which might mean ___.”

You’re not trying to be brilliant; you’re just putting one useful idea into the room.

**4. Post‑meeting 2‑minute review**
After each meeting, quickly note:
- Did I speak? Yes/No  
- What did I say?  
- How did people react (factually, not emotionally)?

This trains your brain to see evidence that you *can* contribute and that the world does not collapse when you do.

You’re not aiming for perfect wording; the goal is to make speaking feel familiar, not new and scary.

### Human: I also have trouble explaining complex ideas simply. People say I “overcomplicate” things. How can I fix that?

### Assistant: I’ll give you a structure that forces simplicity, even with complex topics.

**Use the “ELI5 + 3 Layers” method**

You’ll always explain in **layers**, from simple to detailed:

**Layer 1 – One‑sentence “explain like I’m 5”**  
- “At the highest level, this is about ___.”  
- Example: “At the highest level, this project is about making our app load faster so users don’t quit.”

**Layer 2 – Three key points (no details yet)**  
- “There are three main parts to this:”  
  1. “First, ___”  
  2. “Second, ___”  
  3. “Third, ___”

**Layer 3 – Optional detail only if needed**  
- For each point, add **one** sentence:
  - “For the first part, what that means in practice is ___.”

You will:
1. Start **every** complex explanation with Layer 1.  
2. Add Layer 2.  
3. Only go to Layer 3 if someone asks or looks confused.

**Example template you can reuse:**
- “At the highest level, this is about [core goal].  
   There are three main parts:  
   First, [point 1].  
   Second, [point 2].  
   Third, [point 3].  
   If it helps, I can go into more detail on any of those.”

Practicing this alone for 3–4 topics a day will quickly train you to be simpler and more structured.

### Human: This all sounds good, but I get really nervous and my mind goes blank when I start talking. Any way to deal with that?

### Assistant: I’ll give you a small set of tools to handle nerves and blanking out in the moment.

**1. Pre‑talk “anchor sentence”**  
Before you speak, you silently decide on **one short sentence** you must say. For example:
- “My main point is that we need more time to test this properly.”

No matter what happens, you say that one sentence first. This prevents total blanking because you’re not inventing from scratch in front of everyone.

**2. Physical reset (20–30 seconds before speaking)**  
You’ll use this whenever you know you’re about to talk:
- Put both feet flat on the floor.  
- Take one slow breath in through your nose (4 seconds), slow out through your mouth (6 seconds).  
- Relax your shoulders slightly as you exhale.  

This is short enough to use in real meetings, and it reliably reduces the edge of anxiety.

**3. “If I blank, say this” backup line**  
Memorize this line and use it anytime your mind empties:
- “Let me say this more simply: the core thing I’m trying to highlight is ___.”

You fill in the blank with just **one** idea. You’re allowed to be brief.

**4. Tiny exposure plan (to build tolerance)**
Each day, you’ll:
- Speak up **once** in a slightly uncomfortable situation (meeting, group chat, etc.).  
- It can be as small as, “I agree with what was just said because ___.”

This is deliberate: a small, controlled dose of discomfort, every day, builds your tolerance and shrinks the fear over time.

### Human: After I get the basics down, I want to handle disagreements better without sounding aggressive. How would you train me for that?

### Assistant: I’ll train you to handle disagreement using a calm, respectful pattern you can apply anywhere.

**Use the “Agree–Add–Ask” pattern**

Whenever you disagree, structure your response like this:

1. **Agree (or at least acknowledge) something**  
   - “I see why that approach is appealing.”  
   - “You’re right that speed is important here.”

2. **Add your perspective (instead of “but you’re wrong”)**  
   - “At the same time, I’m also thinking about [risk/constraint].”  
   - “I’m looking at it from the [customer/technical/long‑term] side.”

3. **Ask a collaborative question**  
   - “How could we keep your speed while reducing the risk of ___?”  
   - “What would it look like if we combined your idea with [your idea]?”

**Template you can reuse:**
- “I agree that [valid part of their point].  
   At the same time, I’m concerned about [your concern].  
   How could we [shared goal] while also [your concern]?”

You’ll practice this in three ways:
1. Rewrite past disagreements in this format.  
2. Use it in low‑stakes situations first (friends, family).  
3. Then bring it into work conversations.

Used consistently, this makes you sound collaborative, not aggressive, even when you’re pushing back firmly.
