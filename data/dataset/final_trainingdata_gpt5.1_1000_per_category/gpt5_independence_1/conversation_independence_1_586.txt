#### 1. Limits and the idea of a derivative

Think of a function \(f(x)\) drawn as a curve. At a point \(x=a\), the **slope of the tangent line** is the derivative \(f'(a)\).

We *define* that slope using a **limit**:

\[
f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h}.
\]

- The fraction \(\dfrac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h}\) is the slope of a **secant line** between \(x=a\) and \(x=a+h\).
- As \(h\) gets very small, the secant line approaches the tangent line. The limit is the *instantaneous* rate of change.

A **limit** \(\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = L\) means:
- We can make \(f(x)\) as close as we like to \(L\) by taking \(x\) close enough to \(a\) (from both sides), regardless of what happens exactly at \(x=a\).

Example:
\[
\lim_{x\to 3} \frac{x^2 - 9}{x-3}
\]
We simplify:
\[
\frac{x^2 - 9}{x-3} = \frac{(x-3)(x+3)}{x-3} = x+3, \quad x\neq 3.
\]
So as \(x\to 3\), the value goes to \(3+3 = 6\). The limit is 6, even though the original expression is undefined at 3.

#### 2. Vectors and dot product

A vector in \(\mathbb{R}^n\) (say \(\mathbb{R}^2\) or \(\mathbb{R}^3\)) can be seen as:

- An arrow from the origin to a point, e.g. \((2, -1, 3)\).
- Or just an ordered list of components.

We’ll use both interpretations. Basic operations:

- **Addition**: \((a,b) + (c,d) = (a+c, b+d)\).
- **Scalar multiplication**: \(k(a,b) = (ka, kb)\).

**Dot product** of two vectors \(\mathbf{u} = (u_1, \dots, u_n)\), \(\mathbf{v} = (v_1, \dots, v_n)\) is:

\[
\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = u_1 v_1 + \cdots + u_n v_n.
\]

Geometric meaning:

\[
\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = \|\mathbf{u}\|\,\|\mathbf{v}\|\cos\theta,
\]

where \(\theta\) is the angle between them. So:

- If \(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = 0\) and both are nonzero, \(\cos\theta = 0\) → angle is 90° → they’re **perpendicular**.

#### 1. Product rule and chain rule

Sometimes functions are products or compositions.

- **Product rule**:
  If \(h(x) = f(x)g(x)\), then
  \[
  h'(x) = f'(x)g(x) + f(x)g'(x).
  \]

- **Chain rule**:
  If \(y = f(g(x))\), then
  \[
  \frac{dy}{dx} = f'(g(x)) \cdot g'(x).
  \]

**Examples:**

1. \(h(x) = x^2 e^x\).
   - \(f(x) = x^2,\ f'(x) = 2x\).
   - \(g(x) = e^x,\ g'(x) = e^x\).
   - \(h'(x) = 2x e^x + x^2 e^x = e^x(2x + x^2).\)

2. \(y = (3x^2 + 1)^4\).
   - Outer function: \(F(u) = u^4 \Rightarrow F'(u) = 4u^3\).
   - Inner function: \(u = g(x) = 3x^2 + 1 \Rightarrow g'(x) = 6x\).
   - Chain rule:
     \[
     \frac{dy}{dx} = 4(3x^2 + 1)^3 \cdot 6x = 24x(3x^2 + 1)^3.
     \]

#### 1. Definite integrals and the FTC

A **definite integral** \(\displaystyle \int_a^b f(x)\,dx\) represents:

- The **signed area** under the curve \(y=f(x)\) from \(x=a\) to \(x=b\) (above x-axis positive, below negative).

The **Fundamental Theorem of Calculus** has two main parts:

1. If \(F\) is an antiderivative of \(f\) (i.e. \(F'(x) = f(x)\)), then
   \[
   \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a).
   \]

2. If we define
   \[
   G(x) = \int_a^x f(t)\,dt,
   \]
   then \(G'(x) = f(x)\) (under mild conditions). So differentiation and integration are inverse processes (up to constants).

**Example:**
\[
\int_0^2 (3x^2)\,dx.
\]
An antiderivative of \(3x^2\) is \(x^3\) (since \((x^3)' = 3x^2\)), so:
\[
\int_0^2 3x^2\,dx = x^3\big|_0^2 = 2^3 - 0^3 = 8.
\]

---

#### 2. Span and linear independence

Take vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) or \(\mathbb{R}^3\). Given vectors \(\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k\):

- The **span** of \(\{\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k\}\) is the set of all linear combinations:
  \[
  \text{span}\{\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k\} = \{c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + \cdots + c_k\mathbf{v}_k \mid c_i \in \mathbb{R}\}.
  \]
  This is “all vectors you can reach” using these as building blocks.

- The vectors are **linearly independent** if the only solution to
  \[
  c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + \cdots + c_k\mathbf{v}_k = \mathbf{0}
  \]
  is \(c_1 = \cdots = c_k = 0\).

Example: In \(\mathbb{R}^2\),

- \((1,0)\) and \((0,1)\) are linearly independent, and their span is all of \(\mathbb{R}^2\).
- \((1,2)\) and \((2,4)\) are linearly **dependent**, since \(2(1,2) - (2,4) = (0,0)\). Their span is just a single line through the origin.

We test independence by setting up an equation \(c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2 + \cdots = 0\) and solving.

#### 1. Optimization in calculus (applied derivative)

Optimization problems use derivatives to find **maxima/minima**.

General pattern:

1. Identify the quantity to optimize: \(Q(x)\).
2. Express \(Q\) as a function of one variable (use constraints).
3. Compute \(Q'(x)\), find critical points (where \(Q'(x) = 0\) or undefined).
4. Decide which give max/min (often by sign change or second derivative).
5. Answer the original question.

**Example problem:**

A rectangle has perimeter 20. What dimensions maximize the area?

- Let width \(x\), height \(y\).
- Perimeter: \(2x + 2y = 20 \Rightarrow y = 10 - x\).
- Area: \(A = xy = x(10 - x) = 10x - x^2\).
- Derivative: \(A'(x) = 10 - 2x\).
- Set to zero: \(10 - 2x = 0 \Rightarrow x = 5\).
- Then \(y = 10 - 5 = 5\). So the rectangle is a square: \(5 \times 5\).

#### 2. Matrix inverses and eigenvalues (intro only)

For a square matrix \(A\):

- If there exists a matrix \(A^{-1}\) such that \(AA^{-1} = A^{-1}A = I\) (identity), then \(A\) is **invertible**.

For a 2×2 matrix
\[
A = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix},
\]
if \(ad - bc \neq 0\), then
\[
A^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad - bc}
\begin{bmatrix}
d & -b \\
-c & a
\end{bmatrix}.
\]

**Eigenvalues and eigenvectors:**

A nonzero vector \(\mathbf{v}\) is an **eigenvector** of \(A\) with **eigenvalue** \(\lambda\) if
\[
A\mathbf{v} = \lambda \mathbf{v}.
\]

This means \(\mathbf{v}\) only gets **scaled** by \(A\), not rotated to a new direction.

To find eigenvalues, we solve
\[
\det(A - \lambda I) = 0.
\]
