**Handling repeated missed commitments (without killing morale)**

1. **Assume good intent, check the system first**
   - Ask: Are we overcommitting as a team? Are tasks under-specified?
   - If *everyone* misses commitments, fix planning and scoping first.
   - If it’s mainly 1–2 people, lean into individual conversations.

2. **Have a 1:1 conversation focused on curiosity, not blame**

You can adapt something like:

> “I’ve noticed a pattern I want to talk through with you.  
> In the last few sprints, a lot of your tickets have slipped or carried over.  
> This isn’t about blaming you—I want to understand what’s going on so we can fix it together.  
> From your perspective, what’s making it hard to hit the commitments we set?”

Then listen:
- Are they overloaded?
- Are requirements unclear?
- Are they getting pulled into unplanned work?
- Are they blocked but hesitant to speak up?

3. **Clarify expectations and co-create a fix**

Follow up with:

> “Here’s what I need going forward: when we commit to work in sprint planning, I need a very high chance that it gets done, or that you flag risks early.  
> How can we change things—either in how we plan or how you work—so those commitments are realistic?”

Possible actions:
- Reduce their sprint load for a few weeks but expect higher reliability.
- Have them break work into smaller tickets.
- Add a quick mid-week check-in with you.

4. **Set a clear check-in point**

> “Let’s try these changes for the next 2 sprints. After that, we’ll review together how it’s going and whether you’re consistently hitting what we commit to.”

5. **If the pattern doesn’t change**

You stay calm but firmer:

> “We’ve tried a few adjustments over the last couple of sprints, but I’m still seeing the same pattern of missed commitments.  
> At this point, this is affecting the team’s ability to trust our plans.  
> To stay on this team, I need you to reliably follow through on the work we agree to, or proactively renegotiate commitments before they slip.  
> Do you feel you can meet that expectation? If yes, what will you do differently starting this sprint?”

This keeps morale by:
- Framing it as shared problem-solving.
- Being explicit about expectations and consequences without hostility.

### Assistant: Understood. I’ll propose a lightweight, explicit model that keeps speed but reduces thrash:

- One person is the **Technical Decision Owner (TDO)** for a given decision.
- Everyone can give input, but the TDO decides after a short, structured discussion.
- Once decided, everyone “disagrees and commits” unless there’s truly new information.

Here’s how you can implement it:

---

**1. Assign a Technical Decision Owner per major decision**

- Default: The **Engineering Lead** is TDO for cross-cutting architecture.
- For feature-level decisions, the **area owner** is TDO.

You can state this to the team:

> “For each significant technical decision, we’ll have one clear Decision Owner. You can all give input, but that person is responsible for making the final call and moving us forward.”

---

**2. Use a simple “Decision Record” format**

You don’t need formal ADRs everywhere; a short template in Slack/Notion is enough:

> **Decision:** [e.g., Use X framework for our messaging layer]  
> **Decision Owner:** [Name]  
> **Context:** [2–3 bullets: constraints, goals]  
> **Options considered:** [Option A, Option B, maybe C]  
> **Decision & Why:** [1–2 sentences]  
> **Date:** [ ]

This keeps debates focused and makes it easier to move on.

---

**3. Structure the debate with time-boxes**

For contentious decisions:

1. Time-box discussion: e.g., 30 minutes meeting or 24 hours of async comments.
2. Ask each person to:
   - State their preferred option and why.
   - State what they see as the main risk of the other option.
3. At the end of the timebox, the TDO decides and documents it.

You can say:

> “We’ll give this 30 minutes of focused discussion. After that, [Name] will decide and document. Once that’s done, we’ll move forward unless new critical info appears.”

---

**4. Introduce “disagree and commit” explicitly**

After a decision:

> “I know not everyone got exactly what they wanted here.  
> We’ve made a call: we’re going with [decision].  
> I’m asking everyone to disagree and commit—support this decision in code and behavior so we can move fast.  
> If we hit serious issues, we’ll revisit with data.”
