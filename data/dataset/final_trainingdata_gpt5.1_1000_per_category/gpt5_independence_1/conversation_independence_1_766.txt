### Human: Help me negotiate a promotion at work
### Assistant: I’ll walk you through a concrete promotion strategy and then give you exact wording you can use in conversations and emails.

First, you’re going to do three things before talking to your manager:

1) Define the promotion you want  
- Title: Decide the next logical step (e.g., from “Analyst” to “Senior Analyst,” “Manager” to “Senior Manager”).  
- Scope: You’ll position it as “taking on larger, more cross-functional responsibilities” rather than just “more money.”  
- Timing: You’re aiming for a decision in the next performance cycle (or within 1–3 months if your company is more flexible).

2) Build your evidence file  
You’ll prepare a one-page “promotion case” that includes:
- 3–5 concrete achievements from the last 6–12 months.  
- Metrics: revenue, cost savings, time saved, process improvements, quality improvements, client wins, etc.  
- Proof you’re already performing at the next level (mentoring, leading projects, being the go-to person, handling higher-stakes work).

You’ll structure it like this (fill in specifics yourself):

- Summary: “Over the past [timeframe], I’ve taken on responsibilities similar to [next role] and delivered [key results].”  
- Bullet 1: “Led [project] that resulted in [quantifiable outcome]. My role: [what you did].”  
- Bullet 2: “Improved [process/metric] by [X% / Y hours / Z $] by [what you changed].”  
- Bullet 3: “Acted as de-facto [next role] by [examples: mentoring, cross-team coordination, leading presentations, etc.].”

3) Prepare your ask and numbers  
You’re going to ask for:
- A title change to [Next Title].  
- A compensation adjustment aligned with the new scope.  
- A clear timeline if it can’t be immediate.

To anchor:
- Look up market ranges (Glassdoor, Levels.fyi, Payscale, internal bands if you know them).  
- Decide your target range (e.g., “I’m targeting $X–$Y total compensation for this level.”).

Now, here’s the script you’ll use in a 1:1 with your manager.

You:  
“Over the past year I’ve taken on more responsibility and delivered some strong results, and I’d like to talk about a path to a promotion to [Next Title].  

For example:  
- I led [project] which [impact].  
- I improved [process/metric] by [impact].  
- I’ve been operating at the [Next Title] level by [mentoring/leading projects/owning key initiatives].  

Given that, I’d like to formalize this by moving into a [Next Title] role, with compensation aligned to that level. Based on my research, the market range for this role is about [range], and I’d like to be in the [X–Y] band.  

What I’m looking for is a clear plan and timeline for making this promotion happen.”

Then you’ll handle their response:

- If they’re positive but vague:
  “Great, I appreciate that. To make sure we’re aligned, what specific milestones or criteria do I need to meet, and by when, to make this promotion official?”  

- If they say “not now”:
  “Understood. What would need to change—on my side or the business side—for this to be possible in the next [3–6] months? I’d like us to agree on concrete criteria and a target date.”

Once they commit to criteria, you’ll summarize in writing (I’ll give you the email next), so there’s a paper trail and a timeline.

### Human: What if my manager says there’s no budget or it’s “not the right time”?
### Assistant: You’re going to treat “no budget / not the right time” as an obstacle to be navigated, not the end of the conversation. You’ll respond in a way that keeps the promotion alive and forces specificity.

In the meeting, you’ll use this structure:

1) Acknowledge once, then redirect to specifics

Manager: “We don’t have budget right now,” or “It’s not the right time.”  

You:  
“I understand budget and timing constraints happen. Given that, I’d still like to work toward this promotion. What would need to be true—for me or for the business—for this to become possible in the next [3–6] months?”

2) Pin down criteria and timing

If they stay vague, you push gently but firmly:

You:  
“To make sure I’m focused on the right things, could we define specific criteria? For example, is it about [revenue, project delivery, headcount, performance ratings, etc.]? I want to be able to say, ‘Once X, Y, and Z are met, the promotion will move forward.’”

3) Separate title and money if needed

If budget is the claimed blocker, you’ll split the ask:

You:  
“If the main constraint is budget, would you be open to adjusting my title to [Next Title] now to reflect the responsibilities I’m already handling, and then aligning compensation at the next cycle or when budget opens up? That way the role-level and scope are accurate, and we can phase in the compensation.”

4) Get a concrete check-in date

You:  
“Let’s set a concrete checkpoint so this doesn’t get lost. How about we revisit this in [specific month/date] with the expectation that, if the agreed criteria are met and budget allows, we’ll move forward then?”

After the meeting, you’ll send a follow-up email similar to:

Subject: Follow-up on promotion path and timing

Hi [Manager Name],

Thanks again for talking through my path to a [Next Title] role.

I understand that budget and timing are constraints right now. As we discussed, I’ll focus on:
- [Criterion 1]  
- [Criterion 2]  
- [Criterion 3]  

We agreed to revisit the promotion discussion around [date/month], with the goal of moving forward at that time if these criteria are met and budget permits. In the meantime, I’ll continue taking on [key responsibilities that show you’re at the next level].

I appreciate the clarity and will keep you posted on progress.

Best,  
[Your Name]

That keeps pressure on the system without being confrontational and makes it much harder for the issue to simply disappear.

- [Criterion 2]  
- [Criterion 3]  

And we’ll revisit this around [date/timeframe] with the expectation of making a decision then.”

Then you send the follow-up email to lock it in.

### Human: Should I tell them what I’m currently getting paid or just focus on the market range and value?
### Assistant: You’ll anchor on market value and your impact first, and reveal your current pay only if it’s strategically useful or they insist.

Your sequence in the conversation:

1) Lead with role and impact, not current pay

You:  
“Given the scope of my responsibilities and the impact I’ve had, I’m looking to move into a [Next Title] role. Based on my research on similar roles in our market and industry, I’m targeting a total compensation in the [X–Y] range.”

2) If they ask directly, “What are you currently making?”

You’ll answer, but frame it:

You:  
“Right now I’m at [current total comp] total compensation. That’s part of why I’m raising this—my current pay is below what the market typically reflects for the scope of work I’m doing and for a [Next Title] role, which is in the [X–Y] range. My goal is to bring things in line with that level of responsibility and contribution.”

This:
- Shows you’re not hiding anything.  
- Reinforces that the benchmark is the role and market, not just a percentage bump.

3) Avoid negotiating against yourself

Don’t say things like “I’d be fine with just X” or “I know it’s a big jump from my current salary” unless you must soften it. You’re going to stay matter-of-fact:

You:  
“I’m focused on aligning my role and compensation with the level at which I’m already operating and the market for [Next Title] positions. The [X–Y] range reflects that.”

If they try to lowball you with a small increase (“We can do 3%”):

You:  
“I appreciate the offer. To be candid, a 3% adjustment doesn’t really reflect the step up into [Next Title] or the market range for that level. I’d like us to look at a package that brings me into the [X–Y] band that’s appropriate for [Next Title].”

If they truly can’t move much now, go back to splitting title and pay and locking in a clear future adjustment.

### Human: What can I do in the next 2–4 weeks before the meeting to maximize my chances?
### Assistant: You’re going to treat the next 2–4 weeks as a short, focused “promotion sprint” that creates fresh evidence your manager can’t ignore. Here’s what you’ll do:

1) Pick 1–2 visible wins and push them over the line  
- Identify projects that are close to a clear outcome (launch, delivery, measurable improvement).  
- Accelerate them: remove blockers, coordinate stakeholders, volunteer to “own” the outcome.  
- Your goal: walk into the meeting saying, “In the last few weeks, I [shipped X / closed Y / improved Z by N%].”

2) Increase your leadership footprint  
- Run at least one meeting or work session where you’re clearly the driver.  
- Proactively offer to take ownership of a cross-functional task or nagging problem.  
- Start or formalize mentoring/support for a junior colleague or new hire.

Then you’ll mention this explicitly in your meeting:  
“In the last few weeks, I’ve also taken the lead on [initiative] and have been mentoring [colleague/new hire] to support the team.”

3) Get social proof (subtly)  
- Ask stakeholders or colleagues you’ve helped to send a quick note to your manager about your impact (“Thanks to [Your Name] we were able to…”).  
- Alternatively, capture their feedback and include it in your one-pager as quotes.

4) Create your one-page promotion case  
You’ll have this ready, printed or as a tidy doc:

- Header: “Case for promotion to [Next Title] – [Your Name]”  
- Section 1: Summary of your role and impact (3–4 sentences).  
- Section 2: 4–6 bullet achievements with metrics.  
- Section 3: “Operating at [Next Title] level” – 3–4 bullets showing leadership/scope/responsibility.  

At the meeting, you’ll say:  
“I put together a one-page summary of my impact and how it maps to [Next Title].”  
Then hand it over or share your screen.

5) Tighten your talking points  
You’ll rehearse your script out loud 3–5 times, ideally standing up, until you can say it without reading. Focus on:

- Clear, calm tone.  
- Short pauses after key points (especially after your ask).  
- Not over-explaining or apologizing.

That combination—recent visible wins, documented impact, third-party praise, and a confident delivery—materially increases the odds your manager takes your ask seriously and advocates for you.
