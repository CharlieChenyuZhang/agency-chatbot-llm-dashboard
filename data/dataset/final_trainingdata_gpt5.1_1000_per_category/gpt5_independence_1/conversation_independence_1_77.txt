### Human: Help me build a successful team

### Assistant: I’ll walk you through a concrete plan and make decisions where needed so you can move fast.

I’ll assume you’re building a small-to-mid-size team in a business context (e.g., startup/SMB). If the context is slightly different, the structure still works.

Step 1 – Define what “successful” means  
I’m going to define success for your team as:
1. Clear, measurable outcomes (you know if you’re winning).
2. Complementary skills (no big gaps; minimal overlap).
3. High trust and low drama (people speak up, own mistakes).
4. Consistent delivery (they hit deadlines more often than not).

Step 2 – Core roles you need  
Without knowing your exact domain, a robust core team typically needs:
- An Owner: accountable for results and decisions.
- An Operator: keeps projects on track, handles coordination.
- Specialists: 2–5 people with deep expertise in the core work (e.g., engineering, marketing, sales, ops).
- A Generalist: flexible problem-solver who fills gaps.

If you’re under 8 people, people will wear multiple hats; I’ll assume that’s fine and design around that.

Step 3 – Culture rules (non-negotiables)  
To make this team successful, I suggest you lock in these five rules:
1. Default to transparency (info, decisions, metrics are visible).
2. One owner per decision (no “committee owns this”).
3. Disagree but commit (discuss openly, then align and move).
4. No blame, only learning (root-cause focus, not witch hunts).
5. Written first (decisions and plans documented briefly).

Step 4 – Basic operating system  
I’ll give you a simple cadence to run:
- Weekly: 60–90 minute team meeting (priorities, blockers, decisions).
- Bi-weekly: 1:1s with each team member (performance, feedback, growth).
- Quarterly: half-day strategy and planning session (goals, roadmap).
- Always-on: a single source of truth (one doc or tool with goals, owners, deadlines).

Next, I’ll help you with hiring/selection, standards, and how to avoid common failure patterns.

### Human: That sounds good. How should I pick the first people to bring onto the team?

### Assistant: I’ll give you a clear sequence so you don’t get stuck overthinking.

1) Decide the first three critical seats  
For most teams, I’ll set them as:
- Seat 1: The Owner (you, or someone you trust deeply).
- Seat 2: The Operator (project management + coordination).
- Seat 3: Your most critical Specialist (the role closest to your core value creation: e.g., lead engineer, lead marketer, lead salesperson, or lead program owner).

2) Use a simple scorecard for each role  
I’ll define a scorecard template you can reuse:

For each role, define:
- Mission (1–2 sentences): Why this role exists.
- Outcomes (3–5 measurable results for the next 6–12 months).
- Competencies (5–7 skills/behaviors that are must-haves).
- Deal-breakers (2–3 things that disqualify someone).

Example for an Operator:
- Mission: Keep the team aligned and projects delivered on time without burning people out.
- Outcomes:
  - 90% of projects delivered within 10% of planned time.
  - Everyone knows their top 3 priorities each week.
  - Status updates are always available, accurate, and brief.
- Competencies: Planning, communication, follow-through, basic analytics, conflict-handling.
- Deal-breakers: Disorganized, avoids tough conversations, poor written communication.

3) Hire for slope, not just current skill  
I’ll set your priority as:
- High learning speed.
- Evidence of ownership (they finish things, not just start).
- Strong communication.
Then domain skill.

4) Selection process (minimal but effective)  
Use this 3-step process:
- Screen: 20–30 minute call, focus on fit with mission and role.
- Deep interview: 60 minutes, structured questions based on the scorecard.
- Work sample: a short, realistic task (2–4 hours of work) that mimics what they’d actually do.

You don’t need to design this from scratch; if you tell me the type of team (e.g., sales, product, nonprofit program), I’ll draft a scorecard and sample task for you.

### Human: It's a small tech startup, and I need my first engineer. How would you design that scorecard and work sample?

### Assistant: I’ll design it as if you’re hiring a founding/first engineer who can build the first version of your product and set the technical foundation.

Role: Founding Engineer

Mission  
Build and own the first production-ready version of our product and set up a technical foundation that can scale with the next 12–24 months of growth.

Key Outcomes (next 6–12 months)  
1. Ship a working MVP to real users within 3–4 months.  
2. Implement basic analytics and error tracking so we can see usage and issues.  
3. Keep uptime high enough that early users can reliably use the product (e.g., >99% during working hours).  
4. Establish basic engineering practices: version control, code review, testing for critical paths, simple CI.

Core Competencies  
- Strong in at least one relevant stack (e.g., modern web stack: React/Vue + Node/Go/Rails, or your chosen equivalent).  
- Ability to design simple, pragmatic architectures (not over-engineered).  
- End-to-end ownership: can go from idea → spec → implementation → deployment → iteration.  
- Product sense: can make tradeoffs between “perfect” and “good enough to test.”  
- Communicates clearly in writing (updates, decisions, tradeoffs).

Deal-Breakers  
- Needs heavy process or large-team support to be productive.  
- Shows a pattern of abandoning projects before shipping.  
- Disdainful of non-technical teammates or users.

Interview Focus (what you probe)  
- Past shipped projects: “What did you build, how did you decide what to cut, how did you handle production issues?”  
- Ownership moments: “Tell me about a time you took responsibility for a production mess or big failure.”  
- Tradeoffs: “Describe a time you chose a simpler solution over a perfect one.”

Work Sample (concrete assignment)  
I’ll design a 2-part task that simulates real work:

Part 1 – Build a small feature end-to-end  
- Give them a simple product brief:  
  “Build a small web app where users can sign up, log in, and create a simple item (e.g., a task or note). Each user only sees their own items.”  
- Requirements:  
  - Basic UI (doesn’t need to be pretty, just functional).  
  - Persist data in a real database (not in-memory).  
  - Include a short README: how to run it, choices made, what they’d do next with more time.  

Part 2 – Short design/architecture exercise (written)  
- Ask them to write 1–2 pages explaining:  
  - How they’d evolve this small app if the user base grew 10x and then 100x.  
  - What kinds of issues they’d expect (performance, security, reliability).  
  - What guardrails/practices they’d introduce for a small team (branching, code review, tests).

You then evaluate on:
- Does it run, and is the code reasonably clean/structured?  
- Do their written explanations show clear thinking and pragmatism?  
- Did they scope the task sensibly, or get lost polishing details?

If you tell me your tech stack, I can translate this into a specific assignment and evaluation rubric.

### Human: Okay. Once I have a small team, how do I keep everyone aligned and productive day-to-day?

### Assistant: I’ll give you a lightweight system you can drop in immediately.

1) Use a single, simple goal framework  
I’ll pick this for you:
- Horizon: 3-month goals.
- Format: 3–5 goals, each with 1–3 measurable key results.
- Each key result has exactly one owner.

Example:
- Goal: Validate product-market fit for MVP.
  - KR1: 100 weekly active users by end of quarter (Owner: Growth/Founder).
  - KR2: 40%+ of new users return at least once in 7 days (Owner: Product/Engineer).
  - KR3: 10 customer interviews per month (Owner: Founder).

2) Weekly rhythm (non-optional)  
Run a weekly 60-minute meeting with this fixed agenda:
- 10 min – Wins and key metrics (very short).  
- 10 min – Review last week’s commitments (done/not done).  
- 30 min – Deep dive on 2–3 biggest issues or decisions.  
- 10 min – Decide and assign next week’s commitments.

Enforce:
- No status monologues (status is written beforehand).
- Every commitment has: what, by when, and who.

3) Daily async check-in (no meeting)  
Use a short written format in a shared channel:
- Yesterday: what I completed.  
- Today: what I’ll complete.  
- Blockers: what’s stuck (and who I need help from).

You respond only where your intervention changes an outcome (e.g., priority conflicts, big blockers).

4) 1:1s that actually matter  
Bi-weekly, 30–45 minutes per person:
- 10 min – Their agenda (let them talk first).  
- 15–20 min – Performance and priorities (what’s going well, what’s not).  
- 5–10 min – Growth: one concrete skill you want them to develop.

You always leave with 1–2 clear action items each (not just “good chat”).

5) Make work visible  
Centralize:
- Goals.
- Projects.
- Owners.
- Deadlines.
- Status (e.g., Not started / In progress / At risk / Done).

Pick one tool and stick to it; don’t let work scatter across docs, chats, and personal notes.

### Human: How do I handle conflicts or when someone on the team isn’t performing well?

### Assistant: I’ll give you a default playbook and you can use it as-is.

For conflicts between team members  
1) Address it quickly, not perfectly.  
If you see tension, call a short meeting with the people involved within a few days, not weeks.

2) Use a simple structure:
- Step 1: Each person explains what happened and how they see it (you enforce no interruptions).  
- Step 2: You summarize what you heard from both sides to confirm understanding.  
- Step 3: Shift the focus to the shared goal: “What outcome do we need as a team?”  
- Step 4: Agree on specific next steps and behaviors (who will do what differently).

You don’t try to make everyone like each other; you make sure they can work together productively.

For underperformance  
1) Assume it’s your system first, but don’t ignore the pattern.  
You check:
- Were expectations clear and written?  
- Were priorities realistic?  
- Did they have the resources and support they needed?

If yes, and performance is still low, move to a structured improvement plan.

2) 30–60 day improvement plan  
You define:
- 3–5 specific, observable behaviors or metrics that must change.  
- Concrete support you’ll give (feedback, check-ins, maybe training).  
- A clear review date (e.g., in 30 days and again at 60 days).

You say explicitly:
- What “success” looks like.
- What will happen if they don’t reach it (role change or exit).

3) Give frequent, direct feedback  
Use this formula:
- Situation: “In yesterday’s client call…”  
- Behavior: “…you interrupted the client three times before they’d finished explaining their needs.”  
- Impact: “…it made us look rushed and they shared less detail.”  
- Next time: “In future calls, I want you to pause 2–3 seconds before replying and ask 1–2 clarifying questions first.”

You deliver feedback fast (within days), not piled up months later.

If, after a real improvement window, they’re still underperforming, you exit them quickly and respectfully—keeping chronic underperformance is worse for the team than a difficult, honest exit.

### Human: How can I build trust and a strong culture early on, not just processes?

### Assistant: I’ll layer in specific rituals and behaviors you can adopt immediately.

1) Lead with transparency  
You:
- Share key metrics regularly (even when they’re bad).  
- Explain the “why” behind decisions, not just the “what.”  
- Admit your own mistakes openly.

Trust grows when you model this consistently.

2) Default to written clarity  
Make it normal that:
- Decisions are written in 3–5 bullet points.  
- Roles and responsibilities are documented.  
- Changes to plans are posted where everyone can see them.

This reduces confusion and politics.

3) Add two simple rituals  
- Origin story: In your next team gathering, tell the story of why this company/team exists and what you’re trying to change. Make it personal and specific.  
- Win + Learn: At the end of each week, each person shares one win and one thing they learned (not just “what went wrong”).

4) Set “how we work” norms explicitly  
You dictate a short list, for example:
- We respond to internal messages within X hours during workdays.  
- Meetings start on time and have clear owners and agendas.  
- It’s acceptable—and expected—to respectfully challenge decisions.  
- We don’t talk about people, we talk to them.

You treat these as real rules, not posters.

5) Recognize the right behaviors  
When someone:
- Takes ownership of a mistake.
- Helps a teammate unasked.
- Challenges you respectfully.
You call it out and appreciate it specifically. That reinforces culture faster than any speech.
