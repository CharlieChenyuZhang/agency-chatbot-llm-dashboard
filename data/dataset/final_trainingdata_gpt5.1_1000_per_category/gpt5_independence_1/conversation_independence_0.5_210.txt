Arin learned early that the Archive had a heartbeat.

It was not the polite, steady rhythm of a human heart. It was a slow, arrhythmic thud that lived in the bones of the building, a pulse that shivered through shelves and stone and skin whenever too many sigils were woken at once. On quiet mornings, when only a few scribes had lit their lamps and the city outside was still half-asleep, Arin could feel it most clearly.

They paused on the landing and pressed a hand to the wall, just under an iron sconce. The stone was cool and faintly slick from centuries of touch. Beneath it, the Archive murmured.

“Don’t start without me,” Arin whispered, because it felt wrong to walk in without saying anything at all.

Behind them, the stairwell coughed up a draft that smelled of damp wool and coal smoke. The city was awake now: carters shouting in the lower streets, bells clanging from the river wharves, the metallic cry of the tram rails as they shuddered into motion. Above, in the reading halls, someone was already arguing—Arin heard the tinny echo of raised voices, punctuated by the soft clang of a staff striking stone.

They squared their shoulders and climbed the last few steps to the third floor.

Third North: Restricted Arcana.

No door barred their way. There was only a brass plaque set into the stone: an etched circle threaded with sigils, each stroke blackened by age and use. The circle’s inner ring held the Archive’s oath, in a language no one spoke on the streets anymore.

Arin touched the edge of the plaque. It bit their skin with a pinprick of cold. The sigil briefly flared dull red, recognizing them, then subsided.

Inside, the air grew thin and dry, dead leaves of parchment and old ink. Rows of shelves loomed like narrow towers, each labeled with a precise, fussy hand: War-Magicks, Pre-Concord Charms, Thaumic Theory, Anomalous Phenomena.

The last category always drew Arin’s gaze, the letters dark as dried blood. Anomalous.

“Assistant Vale,” came a rasping voice from between the stacks, “you are three minutes late.”

Arin flinched. “Master Kellis,” they said, and stepped fully into the aisle.

Master Kellis was perched on a high stool, long legs folded like a crane’s. His grey hair was bound back with a simple leather thong, and his eyes—sharp, pale—missed nothing. Before him on the lectern lay a spread of sigil-scrolls, each anchored with a brass weight: circles within circles, interlocking marks humming with buried power.

“Two minutes, fifty-three seconds,” Kellis amended, glancing at the clock sigil inked on the inside of his wrist. “If you intend to serve in the Restricted halls, you will treat time as the Council does. Precisely.”

“Yes, Master,” Arin said. They moved quickly to the side table, shrugging off their threadbare coat and hanging it on a peg. The chill of the Archive seeped instantly through their shirt, but they tried not to shiver.

Kellis watched them with that same narrow focus he turned on every document. “You were in the lower markets again,” he said. “Your shoes are filthy.”

Arin glanced down. Street mud clung to the worn leather, clotted in the seams. “My sister—Lira—needed flour,” they said. “The prices rise by the hour. If I waited until after dusk—”

Kellis lifted a hand. The gesture cut off Arin’s words as neatly as a knife. “The concerns of bakers and housewives do not enter this room,” he said. “Here, there is only the text, and the will of the Council which governs its use.”

“Yes, Master,” Arin murmured, though the words tasted like someone else’s.

The Council’s will lived in every corner of the city: in the uniformed Inquisitors who patrolled the tram lines, in the quiet men and women who recorded disputes in the lower courts, in the small sigil stamped into the corner of every work license. But nowhere, Arin thought, did it press down as tangibly as it did here, in the Archive, where knowledge itself had been sorted into what the Council permitted and what it had locked away.

Kellis tapped the lectern. “We have work. A request from the High Council: cross-reference on the Wildfire Event. I have already retrieved three acceptable sources, but I am told there are peripheral materials in Anomalous Phenomena that may be relevant. You will fetch them.”

Arin stilled. The word. Wildfire.

It meant something to them, though they could not say what. A childhood story? A half-heard rumor on the trams? The syllables carried heat, like a hand held too close to a flame.

“I—yes, Master,” they said.

Kellis’s gaze sharpened. “Do not linger,” he said. “The Council expects my preliminary notes before midday session.”

Arin bowed their head and slipped away into the stacks.

The shelves whispered around them, wood creaking softly as they passed. Here, the labels grew stranger: Untyped Signatures, Unclassified Phenomena, Aberrant Manifestations. Many of the scroll tubes and leather-bound folios bore additional markings—bands of red ink, the sharp triangles of the Inquisitorial Seal.

Unauthorized Access: Death by Council Decree.

They stopped before a narrow shelf half-hidden behind a support pillar. The tag read: W-7: Wildfire, Historical.

Just looking at the word made the hairs on their arms stand up.

Arin reached out—and the Archive’s heartbeat lurched.

For a moment, the stone beneath their feet thrummed, not slow and arrhythmic but quick, startled. Somewhere deep in the building, a sigil flared, a bright, muffled thump like a fist against a door. Lamps flickered. Dust sifted down from the upper beams in a fine, grey rain.

“Assistant?” Kellis’s voice cracked from the far end of the hall. “What did you touch?”

“Nothing!” Arin called back, heart drumming rabbit-fast. Their hand hovered inches from the shelf. “I haven’t—”

The pulse rolled through the stacks again. This time Arin felt it in their teeth.

And then, sharp as a knife slid under the skin, came the second sensation: a flare of heat at the inside of their wrist, where their own minor license sigil lay dormant. It burned—not with pain, exactly, but with insistence.

Something was wrong.

Arin pressed their palm against the W-7 label to steady themselves. The moment flesh met wood, a faint, charred scent rose into the air.

Memory surfaced, half-formed: Lira, last night, standing in the doorway of their cramped attic room, eyes wide in the dark. “Arin,” she whispered, “I—something happened in the street. Don’t be angry. I didn’t mean—”

The Archive shivered again.

Arin snatched their hand back. Behind them, footsteps approached, Kellis’s cane tapping an impatient rhythm on the stone.

They took a breath.

Whatever had happened in the street—whatever Lira had done—it was already rippling through the city’s veins. The Council felt disturbances. The Archive felt them, too.

Arin, low-born assistant with mud on their shoes and a half-worn license on their wrist, stood in the aisle marked Wildfire and understood, for the first time, that the Archive’s heartbeat could change.

And that, if it did, so could everything else.
