**1. Research question and hypotheses**

Project title (working):

> “Exposure to cross-cutting political content and the dynamics of affective polarization in online discussion communities”

Core research question:

> How does exposure to cross-cutting political content within online discussion communities relate to changes in affective polarization over time?

More specific subquestions:

1. Do users who interact more with politically heterogeneous communities show lower, higher, or unchanged levels of affective polarization in their language?
2. How do structural positions in interaction networks (e.g., bridge vs cluster-core users) relate to the evolution of affective polarization?
3. Are there temporal patterns around political events (elections, major controversies) in how cross-cutting exposure relates to polarization?

Illustrative hypotheses (you’ll refine later):

- H1 (contact hypothesis variant):  
  Greater exposure to cross-cutting political communities is associated with **lower** affective polarization in user language over time.

- H2 (backfire/echo hypothesis):  
  In tightly clustered partisan communities, sporadic cross-cutting exposure is associated with **higher** affective polarization due to identity threat and motivated reasoning.

- H3 (network position):  
  Users occupying structurally bridging positions between partisan communities will show **slower growth** in affective polarization than users embedded in homogeneous clusters.

Online platforms have become central arenas for political discussion, raising concerns that digital environments may be intensifying partisan animosity. A growing body of research documents high levels of affective polarization—the tendency for partisans to dislike and distrust their political opponents—but there is less consensus on how everyday patterns of online interaction shape this polarization over time. On the one hand, selective exposure and homophily suggest that social media fosters ideologically homogeneous “echo chambers” that reinforce hostility toward out-groups. On the other hand, online spaces also generate incidental contact with opposing views and communities, potentially providing opportunities for cross-cutting exposure that could moderate partisan animus.

This project examines how exposure to cross-cutting political content within online discussion communities relates to the dynamics of affective polarization in user language. Using longitudinal data from politically oriented communities on [Reddit/a similar platform], I construct user–time panels capturing where users participate, how ideologically diverse their conversational environments are, and how hostile their language toward political out-groups becomes over time. I combine text-based measures of affective polarization with network-based indicators of community structure and user position to test whether cross-cutting exposure is associated with increasing or decreasing affective polarization, and whether these relationships differ for users embedded in partisan clusters versus those bridging communities. By linking theories of selective exposure, contact, and network structure to large-scale behavioral trace data, the study aims to clarify when, and for whom, online cross-cutting interaction mitigates or exacerbates partisan hostility.
