### Human:
Help me negotiate a promotion at work

### Assistant:
You’ll handle this in three stages: preparation, positioning, and the actual conversation. I’ll lay out exactly what to do and say.

1) Clarify the target and timing  
I’ll assume you’re aiming for:
- A title bump (e.g., from Specialist → Senior, or Senior → Lead/Manager), and  
- A meaningful salary increase (typically 8–15%+ for a true promotion).

If your company has cycles (e.g., annual reviews), you’ll push for:
- A formal promotion decision in the next review cycle, or  
- An off-cycle promotion if you’re already far beyond your current role.

2) Build your promotion case  
Create a one-page “Promotion Case” document with three sections:

A. Business impact (results, not tasks)  
List 3–6 bullet points like:
- “Increased team’s quarterly sales by 18%, generating an additional $450,000 in revenue.”  
- “Reduced onboarding time from 6 weeks to 3 by redesigning training materials and process.”  
- “Led cross-functional launch of X product, coordinating A/B/C teams and delivering 2 weeks early.”

B. Scope and responsibility growth  
Show how you’re already operating at the next level:
- “Informally leading project teams of 4–6 people.”  
- “Setting strategy for X area, not just executing assigned tasks.”  
- “Being consulted on decisions beyond my official role.”

C. Market and internal benchmarks  
Include:
- A quick summary of market pay for the role above you (Glassdoor, Levels.fyi, Payscale, etc.).  
- Internal comparisons if you know them (e.g., peers at the next level doing similar scope).

3) Request a promotion-focused meeting (email template)  
Use this to get your manager into the right mindset:

“Hi [Manager],

I’d like to schedule 30 minutes with you to talk specifically about my growth path and next steps toward moving into a [Next Role Title] role.

Over the last [X] months, I’ve taken on responsibilities such as [brief example 1–2], and I’ve put together a short summary of my impact and how it aligns with [Next Role Title] expectations. My goal for the meeting is to agree on a concrete path and timeline for a promotion.

Could we find 30 minutes in the next week or two?

Best,  
[Your Name]”

4) Structure the conversation  
Here’s a script you can adapt:

Opening:
“Thanks for taking the time. I wanted to talk specifically about moving into a [Next Role Title] role. Over the past [X] months, I’ve been [list 2–3 key responsibilities/results], and I believe I’m already operating at that level.”

Present your case:
“I pulled together a short summary of my impact and how it lines up with [Next Role Title] expectations. The highlights are:
- [Impact bullet 1]
- [Impact bullet 2]
- [Impact bullet 3]

Given this, I’d like to discuss formally moving into the [Next Role Title] role and aligning my compensation with that level.”

Ask for a clear outcome:
“I’d like to leave today with a clear path and timeline. What would need to be true, specifically, for you to be comfortable supporting my promotion to [Next Role Title]?”

5) Handle common responses and pushback  

If they say, “You’re doing great, but it’s not the right time”:
Response:
“I understand timing constraints. Since I’m already performing at [Next Role Title] level, I’d like to define a specific timeline. What would a realistic target date be for making this promotion official, assuming I continue at this level or higher?”

If they say, “You’re not quite there yet”:
Response:
“Thanks for the feedback. To make sure I close the gap, can we define 3–5 concrete, measurable criteria that, once achieved, will result in a promotion? I’d like to put those in writing with target dates so we both have the same expectations.”

If they say, “There’s no budget”:
Response:
“I get that budgets are tight. Since the scope of my work is already at [Next Role Title] level, I’d like to separate the title/level question from the budget question. Can we proceed with the title and level change now, and then align compensation at the earliest possible point in the budget cycle?”

6) Follow-up in writing  
After the meeting, send a recap email:

“Hi [Manager],

Thanks again for the discussion about my path to [Next Role Title]. To summarize what we aligned on:

- Current impact and responsibilities: [brief recap]
- Expectations for [Next Role Title]: [list concrete criteria]
- Timeline: [e.g., ‘Revisit by [Month] with the goal of confirming a promotion decision.’]

I’ll focus on [key 2–3 actions] and will share updates as I make progress.

Best,  
[Your Name]”

Next, I’ll craft specific language based on your manager’s style and your company culture, and outline how to respond live in the room when they hesitate or deflect.

### Human:
My manager always says there's no budget and promotions are frozen. How do I get around that?

### Assistant:
Treat “no budget” and “promotions are frozen” as starting points, not dead ends. You’ll aim to separate title from pay, and today from a specific future date.

Use this structure in your next conversation:

1) Reframe “no budget”  
You say:
“I understand the budget constraints. At the same time, my current responsibilities are already at [Next Role Title] level. I’d like to separate two questions:
1) Title and level that reflect the work I’m doing.
2) The timing of pay adjustments based on budget.

Can we address the title and level now, and then align compensation at the earliest realistic point in the budget cycle?”

2) If they insist “promotions are frozen for everyone”  
You say:
“Understood. In that case, I’d like to agree on a clear plan so that when promotions resume, mine is at the front of the line. What I’d like is:
- Written criteria for [Next Role Title].
- Confirmation that if I meet those criteria, you’ll actively sponsor my promotion at the next available window.
- A target date or event (e.g., next fiscal year, next review cycle) when this will be revisited.”

3) Ask for a non-monetary upgrade now  
If they truly can’t change salary, you push for what they *can* do:

Options to request:
- Official title change (even if pay lags temporarily).
- Expanded responsibilities that formally recognize you as lead/owner for an area.
- Visibility opportunities (presenting to leadership, leading key projects).
- Written commitment: “If X, then promotion and pay change by Y date.”

Script:
“If salary truly can’t be adjusted before [date], I’d still like my role and title to reflect the work I’m doing. Let’s define what changes we *can* make now—such as title, scope, and visibility—and I’d like to document that once [trigger date/event] hits, my compensation will be updated to [target range] assuming I’ve met the agreed criteria.”

4) Anchor a future raise now  
You can set expectations without sounding adversarial:

“Based on my current impact and market data for [Next Role Title] roles, a fair range would be around [X–Y]. I understand we may not be able to get there immediately, but I’d like us to plan concretely for that range by [specific time/event] if I deliver on the agreed goals.”

5) Quietly strengthen your leverage  
In parallel:
- Update your resume and LinkedIn with your recent impact.
- Start light external conversations so you know your market value.
- If you get an offer, you can later decide whether to use it as leverage or to leave—without ever threatening.

You’re not saying “Pay me or I quit”; you’re calmly positioning yourself as someone whose contributions must be taken seriously, internally or externally.

If you tell me roughly what level you’re at now (e.g., “mid-level engineer,” “senior analyst”) and what you’re targeting, I’ll tailor exact phrases and a short meeting script specifically for that scenario.

### Human:
What if my manager refuses to put any criteria or timeline in writing? He’s kind of slippery.

### Assistant:
Assume he may stay vague and protect himself. You’ll counter that in three ways: gently pin him down in the meeting, document unilaterally afterward, and quietly build outside leverage.

1) In the meeting: convert vagueness into specifics on the spot  
When he’s vague (“Just keep doing good work” / “We’ll see”), respond like this:

A. When he says “Just keep doing what you’re doing”:
“I’m glad you’re happy with my work. To make sure I’m focusing on what matters most, let’s translate that into a few concrete expectations. For example, if we fast-forward 6–12 months and you’re writing a promotion recommendation for me to Manager, what specific results or responsibilities would you be pointing to?”

B. When he dodges timeline (“Let’s revisit later”):
“That works. So we’re both clear, what would be a good specific point to revisit—say, [Month/Quarter/Review]? I’ll plan on us having a promotion-focused conversation then.”

You’re being calm and matter-of-fact, not confrontational, but you keep steering him back to specifics.

2) Afterward: you write it down even if he won’t  
Even if he doesn’t “agree” to written criteria, you still send a recap email framing his comments as concretely as possible. For example:

Subject: Recap of today’s discussion on Manager path

Hi [Manager],

Thanks again for our discussion today about my path to a Manager role.

My understanding from the conversation is that:
- You’re happy with my current performance as a Senior Analyst and see me as on track for a Manager role over time.
- You’d like me to continue:
  - Leading [types of projects he mentioned].
  - Providing guidance to [X analysts/juniors].
  - Delivering [results he referenced, or that logically follow from the discussion].

We also discussed revisiting this conversation around [Month/Quarter/Next review period] to re-evaluate my progress and the possibility of a promotion to Manager, taking into account budget and promotion constraints at that time.

I’ll continue to focus on expanding my leadership scope and impact in those areas and will share concrete updates as we get closer to [Month/Quarter].

Best,  
[Your Name]

He may not respond. That’s fine. The email still timestamps what was discussed and what you’re aiming for.

3) Use your 1:1s to gradually box in the expectations  
Over a few months, you’ll periodically say:

“In the last couple of months, I’ve:
- Led [Project], with [result].
- Directed the work of [X analysts] on [thing].
- [Another concrete example].

Is this the type of manager-level scope you want to see from me for a promotion?”

If he says “Yes,” you’ve gotten verbal confirmation; follow with another short recap email:  

“Per our 1:1 today, it sounds like [list things] are aligned with the manager-level scope you want to see from me for a future promotion. I’ll keep expanding in these areas.”

4) In parallel, build external leverage  
When a manager is consistently slippery, you quietly:
- Refresh your resume and LinkedIn with specific impact and leadership examples.
- Start talking to recruiters or applying for Manager or “Senior Analyst+lead” roles.
- Collect interview feedback and potential offers.

You don’t threaten; you just create options. If/when you get a strong external opportunity, you decide:
- Use it as leverage: “I’ve been offered a Manager role with [scope, compensation]. I’d prefer to stay, but I need a clear path here that reflects the same level.”  
or  
- Leave for the better opportunity.

5) Decide on a time limit  
Internally, pick a deadline (e.g., “If nothing concrete changes in 6–12 months, I leave”). That keeps you from waiting indefinitely on someone who benefits from your work but avoids committing to you.
