## Limits (intuitive version)

We want to understand what happens to \(f(x)\) as \(x\) gets closer and closer to some value \(a\).

Example: \(f(x) = x^2\).  
We ask: what happens to \(f(x)\) as \(x \to 2\)?

- At \(x = 1.9\), \(f(x) = 3.61\)
- At \(x = 1.99\), \(f(x) \approx 3.9601\)
- At \(x = 2.01\), \(f(x) \approx 4.0401\)

The values are getting closer to 4 from both sides, so we say:
\[
\lim_{x \to 2} x^2 = 4
\]

This “approaching” idea is how we define derivatives precisely.

Instead of going straight into the formal epsilon-delta stuff (which is important but not urgent for your ML goal), I’ll focus on the geometric idea next: **slope of a curve at a point**.

## Derivative as a slope at a point

For a straight line, slope = “rise over run”:
\[
\text{slope} = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}
\]

For a curved function \(f(x)\), the slope *changes* from point to point.  
At a point \(x = a\), we define the derivative \(f'(a)\) as the slope of the **tangent line**—the line that just “kisses” the curve at that point.

We approximate that slope by looking at **secant lines**:

\[
\text{slope between } x=a \text{ and } x=a+h = \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h}
\]

Then we let \(h \to 0\). That limit (if it exists) is the derivative:

\[
f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h}
\]

Let’s do one concrete example step-by-step, and I’ll have you do part of the algebra.

Take \(f(x) = x^2\). We want \(f'(a)\).

1. Compute \(f(a+h)\).
2. Form the difference \(f(a+h) - f(a)\).
3. Divide by \(h\).
4. Then let \(h \to 0\).

I’ll walk through 1–3 and ask you to simplify.

1. \(f(a+h) = (a+h)^2\)
2. \(f(a+h) - f(a) = (a+h)^2 - a^2\)
3. \(\dfrac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h} = \dfrac{(a+h)^2 - a^2}{h}\)

**Your turn:**  
Can you expand \((a+h)^2 - a^2\) and simplify the fraction \(\dfrac{(a+h)^2 - a^2}{h}\) as much as possible?

(Don’t worry if it’s a bit messy, just show the intermediate step.)

### Assistant: Perfect.

So we have:
\[
\frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h} = 2a + h
\]

Now we take the limit as \(h \to 0\):
\[
f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} (2a + h) = 2a
\]

So for \(f(x) = x^2\), the derivative is:
\[
f'(x) = 2x
\]

This is our first general derivative rule: the slope of \(x^2\) at any point \(x\) is \(2x\).

## Product and chain rules (brief introduction)

Instead of doing every detail at once, I’ll introduce the **idea** and then give you a small practice.

### 1. Product rule

If \(F(x) = u(x) \cdot v(x)\), then:
\[
F'(x) = u'(x)v(x) + u(x)v'(x)
\]

Example:
\(F(x) = x^2 \cdot \sin x\)

- \(u(x) = x^2,\ u'(x) = 2x\)
- \(v(x) = \sin x,\ v'(x) = \cos x\)

So:
\[
F'(x) = 2x \cdot \sin x + x^2 \cdot \cos x
\]

I’ll assume basic trig derivative facts for now:
\[
\frac{d}{dx} \sin x = \cos x,\quad \frac{d}{dx} \cos x = -\sin x
\]
If you want, we can later derive these more carefully.

### 2. Chain rule

If \(y = f(g(x))\), then:
\[
\frac{dy}{dx} = f'(g(x)) \cdot g'(x)
\]

Example:
\(y = (3x^2 + 1)^5\)

- Outer function: \(f(u) = u^5 \Rightarrow f'(u) = 5u^4\)
- Inner function: \(g(x) = 3x^2 + 1 \Rightarrow g'(x) = 6x\)

So:
\[
\frac{dy}{dx} = 5(3x^2 + 1)^4 \cdot 6x = 30x(3x^2 + 1)^4
\]
