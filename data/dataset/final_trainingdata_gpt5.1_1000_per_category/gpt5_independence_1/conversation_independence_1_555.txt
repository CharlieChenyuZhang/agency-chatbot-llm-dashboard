### Human: Help me overcome my fear of public speaking
### Assistant: You’re going to approach this as a skill-building project, not a personality flaw. I’ll lay out a structured plan and you’ll follow it step by step.

First, a quick mental reset:
1. Your goal is not to be perfect; it’s to be clear and useful.
2. Anxiety is a body reaction, not a verdict on your ability.

Now here’s your 7-day starter plan:

**Day 1–2: Get comfortable hearing yourself speak**
- Pick a simple topic you know well (e.g., your favorite hobby or how your workday usually goes).
- Record a 2-minute voice memo explaining it as if to a friend.
- Play it back once, without critiquing yourself. Just get used to your voice.
- Do this twice a day.

**Day 3–4: Short video, no audience**
- Record a 2–3 minute video on your phone about a simple topic.
- Stand or sit upright, look roughly at the camera, and speak in full sentences.
- Ignore how you look; you’re training your brain to realize “I can speak and nothing terrible happens.”

**Day 5–7: Micro “public” speaking**
- Choose one low-stakes daily interaction (ordering coffee, talking to a colleague, etc.).
- Add 1–2 extra sentences to what you’d normally say. For example:
  - Instead of “Latte, please,” say “Hi, could I get a medium latte? I’m trying to stay awake for a long afternoon of meetings.”
- This teaches your brain that speaking a little more than usual is safe.

Alongside this, you’ll use a quick pre-speaking routine:
1. **Box breathing (1 minute):** Inhale 4 seconds, hold 4, exhale 4, hold 4; repeat.
2. **Body reset (30–60 seconds):** Roll shoulders back, shake out hands, stand tall.
3. **One clear intention:** “My job is to help them understand X.” Say it out loud.

Run this 7-day routine, then you’ll layer on actual presentation skills.

### Human: I actually have a presentation in two weeks at work. How should I prepare for that specifically?
### Assistant: I’ll structure a concrete 14-day plan around that presentation so you’re not guessing.

#### Step 1: Lock in your core message (Today)
- Define one sentence: “By the end of this talk, I want people to understand/decide/do ___.”
- Everything in your presentation must support that sentence. Remove anything that doesn’t.

#### Step 2: Simple, clear outline (Today–Tomorrow)
Use a 3-part structure:
1. **Opening (1–2 minutes)**
   - A very short story, problem, or bold statement related to your topic.
2. **Body (3–5 key points)**
   - For each point: what it is, why it matters, one example.
3. **Closing (1–2 minutes)**
   - Summarize key points.
   - End with a clear next step or takeaway.

Write this as bullet points, not a script.

#### Step 3: Two-week practice schedule

**Days 1–3**
- Practice your talk out loud once a day for 5–7 minutes using your outline.
- Don’t worry about being smooth; just get through it.
- Record one run-through (audio or video) each day.

**Days 4–7**
- Refine:
  - Shorten long explanations.
  - Replace jargon with simple words.
- Practice once a day standing up, as if in the real room.
- Add a deliberate pause after each major point—count “1…2” in your head.

**Days 8–10**
- Introduce “pressure”:
  - Time yourself and keep it within your allotted time.
  - Practice as if someone just called on you: start talking within 3 seconds.
- Do one “messy” run and one “best effort” run per day.

**Days 11–13**
- Focus on calm and presence:
  - Do your pre-speaking routine before each run.
  - Practice making eye contact with imaginary people in three areas (left, center, right).
- One focused run-through per day is enough.

**Day 14 (Presentation day)**
- One light run-through in the morning, then stop.
- Before you speak: pre-speaking routine + remind yourself, “My job is to be helpful, not perfect.”

This plan removes guesswork and gives you clear daily actions right up to your presentation.

### Human: I always start shaking and my voice trembles when I speak. How do I stop that?
### Assistant: You’ll tackle this on two fronts: physiology and technique. You won’t aim to eliminate all symptoms; you’ll aim to reduce them and function well despite them.

#### 1. Control the physical side

**A. Pre-talk warmup (3–4 minutes)**
Do this *every time* before you practice or present:
1. **Big muscle movement (60–90 seconds)**
   - March in place, do 20 jumping jacks, or briskly walk around.
   - This burns off excess adrenaline that causes shaking.
2. **Hands and shoulders (30–60 seconds)**
   - Shake out your hands.
   - Roll shoulders forward and backward 10 times each.
3. **Breathing (1–2 minutes)**
   - Inhale through nose for 4 seconds, exhale through mouth for 6 seconds.
   - Longer exhale signals your nervous system to calm down.

**B. Voice steadiness**
- Before speaking, hum gently for 30 seconds.
- Then say a few sentences slowly, exaggerating articulation:
  - “Today I’m going to walk you through three simple steps.”
- This stabilizes your vocal cords and warms them up.

#### 2. Technique that hides the symptoms

- **Use pauses deliberately.** When you feel shaking or a wobbly voice, pause, look at your notes or the audience, breathe once, then continue. Pauses look intentional, not like failure.
- **Anchor your body.**
  - Keep both feet flat on the floor, about shoulder-width apart.
  - Lightly press your toes into the ground; this reduces visible shaking.
- **Use your hands on purpose.**
  - Keep one hand lightly resting on the table/lectern or lightly clasped at your waist when you’re not gesturing.
  - Use simple, slow gestures (e.g., counting on fingers, open palms).

You’ll build these into your daily practice so your body learns: “I know what to do when I’m nervous.”

### Human: What if I completely blank out in the middle of my talk?
### Assistant: You’re going to prepare for blanking out *beforehand* so it feels like a recoverable event, not a disaster.

Put three safety nets in place:

#### 1. A “rescue line” for each section
For every main point, write one short sentence you can say if your mind goes blank. Examples:
- “The key idea here is that we need a simpler process.”
- “Let me step back and summarize this part in plain language.”
- “The most important thing to remember about this is…”

During practice, occasionally *pretend* to blank, glance at your outline, and use your rescue line. That trains the recovery.

#### 2. A simple reset sequence
If you blank:
1. Stop talking. (Silence is okay.)
2. Look briefly at your notes or slide.
3. Take one slow breath in and out.
4. Say: “Let me put that another way,” or “To recap where we are so far…” and restate your last clear point.
5. Move on; don’t apologize multiple times.

#### 3. Minimal, clear notes
You will not rely on memory only. Prepare:
- A one-page outline in large font with:
  - Opening sentence or two.
  - 3–5 main points (just keywords).
  - One closing sentence.
- Place it in front of you where you can glance down quickly.

Your goal isn’t to avoid ever blanking; it’s to be someone who can blank for a moment and then calmly continue.

### Human: I always feel like people are judging me. How do I stop thinking that way?
### Assistant: You’ll shift your focus from “me” to “service” and use a few targeted mental habits.

#### 1. Reframe the audience
Replace “They’re judging me” with something more accurate:
- “Most people are thinking about their own work and lives.”
- “If I’m clear and brief, they’ll be grateful, not critical.”
Choose one of these and repeat it before every practice and before your talk.

#### 2. Switch your goal
Instead of “I want them to like me,” use:
- “I want at least one person to walk away with something useful.”
That’s specific, realistic, and not about your worth.

#### 3. Use a “spotlight shift” technique
Before you start speaking:
- Pick one imaginary person in the audience who *wants* your information (someone who’s tired, behind on work, confused about the topic).
- Imagine you’re talking mainly to that person to help make their day easier.
This moves your brain from “How do I look?” to “How can I help?”

#### 4. Accept some judgment and make it irrelevant
Remind yourself once: “Some people might not care or might be critical; that’s normal and not my problem. My job is to be clear and respectful.” Then move on; do not argue with yourself about it.

Build these into your existing routine: one reframe line + one mental image of a specific person you’re helping.

### Human: Can you give me a short daily routine that combines everything I should do until the presentation?
### Assistant: You’ll follow this single, repeatable daily routine for the next two weeks. It covers mindset, body, and practice.

**Total time: ~20–30 minutes per day**

1. **Mindset (2 minutes)**
   - Say your intention out loud: “My job is to help them understand ___.”
   - Say your reframe: “Most people are busy with their own lives; if I’m clear and brief, they’ll be grateful.”

2. **Body and voice warmup (4–5 minutes)**
   - 1–2 minutes of movement (march, walk, or jumping jacks).
   - Shoulder rolls + shaking out hands (30–60 seconds).
   - 1–2 minutes of slow breathing (4 seconds in, 6 seconds out).
   - 30 seconds humming + a few slow, clear sentences.

3. **Focused practice run (10–15 minutes)**
   - Stand up, use your outline, and deliver your talk once.
   - Use pauses deliberately, anchor your feet, and gesture slowly.
   - If you blank, use your rescue line and continue.

4. **Quick review (3–5 minutes)**
   - Ask yourself:
     - “What was *one* thing that went better than yesterday?”
     - “What is *one* small thing I’ll adjust tomorrow?” (e.g., slower start, clearer example).
   - Make that adjustment note directly on your outline.

5. **Mini “public” speaking (throughout the day)**
   - Choose one everyday interaction to say 1–2 sentences more than you normally would (cashier, colleague, etc.).
   - Treat it as low-stakes practice.

If you do just this routine daily, you’ll walk into your presentation more prepared, more practiced, and less overwhelmed by fear.

### Human: Okay, I’ll try this. Any last advice for the actual day of the presentation?
### Assistant: On the day itself, you’ll simplify and protect your energy.

**Morning**
- One light, slow run-through of your talk.
- Do the full warmup routine once.
- Eat something light, drink water, avoid excess caffeine.

**30–60 minutes before you speak**
- Walk for 5–10 minutes if possible.
- Do a shortened warmup:
  - 1 minute of movement
  - 1 minute of breathing
  - 30 seconds humming and a few clear sentences
- Look at your one-page outline and mentally walk through your main points only.

**Right before you start**
- Stand with feet planted, shoulders back.
- Take one slow breath.
- Silently say: “My job is to help. It doesn’t have to be perfect.”

**During the talk**
- Speak a little slower than feels natural.
- Use pauses after each key point.
- If you feel a wave of anxiety, let it ride in the background and keep talking from your outline; don’t fight the feeling directly.

You’ll treat this presentation as a *first strong rep*, not a final exam. Whatever happens, you’ll have taken a concrete step toward being someone who can speak in public despite fear—and that’s the real win.
