**Episode 1 – Script (You Can Read This)**

**1. Cold Open (10–20 seconds)**  
You:

**2. Intro + Music (20–30 seconds)**  
[Intro music fades in underneath your voice]

You:

[Music fades out]

**3. Why This Matters & Common Mistakes (3–4 minutes)**  
You:  
Let’s start with why this matters. For most people, your salary is the biggest financial lever in your life. A 5–10% raise can be worth thousands of dollars this year, and tens of thousands over your career, because every future raise is built on that new base.

But here are the three most common mistakes I see when people ask for a raise:

1. They focus on **effort**, not **impact**.  
   They say, “I’ve been working really hard,” instead of, “Here are the results I’ve created.”

2. They make it **about their personal needs**, not business value.  
   Things like, “Rent went up” or “I have student loans.” Your manager might care about you, but raises come from budgets tied to performance and impact.

3. They **drop it on their manager with no warning**.  
   They bring it up at the end of a random one-on-one or in a rushed hallway conversation. The manager is surprised and has no data prepared.

We’re going to fix all of this with a simple three-step process you can follow.

**4. Step-by-Step Raise Strategy (6–8 minutes)**  
You:  
Here’s the process:

**Step 1: Gather your receipts.**  
Spend 30–60 minutes writing down:
- Projects you led or contributed to.
- Concrete results: revenue, savings, time saved, customer satisfaction, internal improvements.
- Positive feedback: emails, messages, performance review notes.

Where possible, turn these into numbers:
- “Reduced onboarding time by 25%.”
- “Handled 30% more support tickets while keeping satisfaction high.”
- “Closed 3 new clients worth $X total.”

**Step 2: Understand your market range.**  
Go to salary sites like Glassdoor, Levels.fyi (for tech), or Payscale and look up:
- Your role
- Your location
- Your years of experience

You’re not looking for a perfect number; you just want a reasonable range. For example, if you’re making $65k and you see typical ranges between $70–85k, that’s useful context.

**Step 3: Schedule a dedicated compensation conversation.**  
Don’t surprise your manager. You’re going to book a specific meeting with a clear subject line.

I’ll give you the exact email in a moment.

The mindset here is important: you’re not begging or apologizing. You’re presenting a business case based on your contribution and market data, and inviting your manager to partner with you.

**5. Example Email + Meeting Script (5–7 minutes)**

You:  
Let’s start with the email to schedule the conversation.

You can say:

> Subject: Request to discuss role, impact, and compensation  
>  
> Hi [Manager’s Name],  
>  
> I’d like to schedule 30 minutes sometime in the next week or two to talk about my role, the impact I’ve been having, and how my compensation aligns with that.  
>  
> I’ll come prepared with a short summary of my recent projects and contributions to make the discussion straightforward.  
>  
> Thanks,  
> [Your Name]

This sets the expectation clearly and professionally.

Now, let’s walk through the **actual conversation** once you’re in the meeting. I’ll give you a script you can adapt:

You:  
> Thanks for taking the time to chat with me today. I wanted to talk about my role, the results I’ve been driving, and how my compensation lines up with that.  
>  
> Over the past [time period], I’ve [briefly mention 2–3 key contributions]. For example:  
> – [Result 1: with a number if possible]  
> – [Result 2]  
> – [Result 3]  
>  
> I’ve also looked at market data for similar roles in our industry and location, and it suggests that a typical range is around [X–Y]. Based on the impact I’ve had, I’d like to discuss moving my compensation to [target amount or range].  
>  
> What would it take to make that possible?

Then you **stop talking** and let them respond.

**6. Handling Pushback (3–4 minutes)**  
You:  
Your manager might say things like:

- “We don’t have budget right now.”  
  You can respond with:

  > I understand budgets can be tight. Given the impact I’ve had, what would need to happen—either in terms of timing or results—for a compensation adjustment to be realistic?

- “You’re already paid fairly for your level.”  
  You can say:

  > I appreciate that perspective. Could you help me understand what specific expectations or results you’d want to see from me to be at the next compensation level?

The goal here is either:
- A “yes,” or
- A **clear plan**: what you need to do, by when, to get the raise.

If they give you conditions—like “You need to own X project” or “Hit Y target”—you repeat it back and ask to put it in writing in an email. That way you can follow up.

**7. Recap + Homework (2–3 minutes)**  
You:  
Let’s recap the key steps:

1. **Gather your receipts**: Write down your projects, impact, and any numbers that show results.
2. **Check your market range**: Get a reasonable sense of what similar roles pay.
3. **Book a dedicated meeting**: Use the short email I gave you.
4. **Present your case clearly**: Focus on impact, not effort or personal needs.
5. **Handle pushback professionally**: Ask what would need to happen to make a raise possible and get that plan in writing.

Your homework this week is simple:
- Block off 45 minutes on your calendar to write out your impact list.
- Draft the email to your manager, using the template from this episode.
- If it feels scary, remember: you’re not demanding anything. You’re starting a professional, reasonable conversation.

**8. Call to Action (30–45 seconds)**  
You:  
If you found this episode helpful, follow **Career Clarity Weekly** in your podcast app so you don’t miss next Tuesday’s episode, where we’ll fix your resume in 30 minutes.

If you know one friend or coworker who keeps saying they’re underpaid but hasn’t done anything about it yet, send them this episode. You might help them make thousands of dollars more this year.

I’m [Your Name], and this has been **Career Clarity Weekly**. Thanks for listening—and go schedule that conversation.
