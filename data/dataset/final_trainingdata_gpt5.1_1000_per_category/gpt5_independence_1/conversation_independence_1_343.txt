### Human: Why did you pick the SM7B and not a condenser mic?

### Assistant: I optimized for real-world conditions where room noise and reflections are often the biggest problem, not mic “detail.”

Here is exactly why I chose the SM7B over a typical large-diaphragm condenser:

1. **Room noise rejection**
   - Dynamic mics like the SM7B are **less sensitive** than condensers.
   - That reduced sensitivity plus its tight cardioid pattern means:
     - Less HVAC noise
     - Less keyboard/mouse clicks
     - Less room echo in untreated spaces

2. **Proximity and tone**
   - The SM7B is designed to be used **very close** to the mouth.
   - Close-miking increases the voice-to-room ratio, making the room much less audible.
   - It naturally gives a **full, broadcast-style low end** without needing heavy EQ.

3. **Forgiveness**
   - Condensers can sound great but are unforgiving:
     - They capture more mouth noise, sibilance, and harshness.
     - They emphasize bad room acoustics.
   - SM7B is smoother in the upper mids, so it takes EQ and compression gracefully and is harder to make sound harsh.

4. **Noise floor and gain**
   - Older interfaces sometimes struggled to give enough clean gain for the SM7B; that’s why I paired it with an **inline preamp**.
   - With that in place, the result is very clean and controlled, while still benefiting from the dynamic’s rejection characteristics.

5. **Use-case fit**
   - For spoken word (podcasting, streaming, voiceover in a bedroom/office), a high-quality dynamic broadcast mic is often the most practical choice.
   - Condensers shine in **treated studios** or for capturing room ambience, strings, choirs, etc. Here, the priority is **intelligibility and isolation**, so the SM7B wins.

So the SM7B is not about “better” in an absolute sense; it’s about being **better suited to a typical, imperfect environment** where clarity and isolation matter more than ultra-detailed room capture.

  - Gain: **+3 dB**
  - Q: **0.9**

- Band 4 (Air)
  - Type: High shelf
  - Freq: **11 kHz**
  - Gain: **+1.5 dB**
  - Q: (or slope) default/soft

**3) Compressor**
Any basic compressor will work; set:

- Ratio: **3:1**
- Attack: **15 ms**
- Release: **120 ms**
- Knee: **Soft** (if available)
- Threshold: Adjust until **average gain reduction is about 4 dB** when speaking normally. As a starting numeric value: set threshold so that your normal speech just regularly crosses it (typically around **–20 to –18 dBFS input level**).
- Makeup gain: Start at **+4 dB** and adjust so bypassed vs. engaged sound equally loud.

**4) De-esser**
- Mode: Narrow-band or split-band (either is fine)
- Frequency: **6.0 kHz** center
- Range: **4.5–7.5 kHz** if adjustable
- Threshold: So that on strong “s” sounds you see **3–5 dB** reduction.
- Reduction limit: Cap at **6 dB** max reduction.
- Attack: **Fast** (1–5 ms)
- Release: **Fast/Auto** (50–100 ms)

**5) Limiter**
- Type: Brickwall limiter on the master or vocal bus.
- Ceiling/Output: **–1.0 dB**
- Look-ahead: Default (usually 1–5 ms)
- Release: **Auto** or around **100 ms**
- Threshold: Start at **–6 dB** and then adjust so that on loud phrases you see **1–3 dB** of gain reduction but the vocal doesn’t sound squashed.

**Interface / DAW input level**
- With all processing bypassed, set input gain so that:
  - Normal speech peaks: **–12 dBFS**
  - Loud speech peaks: **–6 to –8 dBFS**

Once those values are in, two quick checks:
- If it sounds boomy: reduce EQ Band 1 at 200 Hz from –2.5 to about –4 dB.
- If it sounds too bright or hissy: reduce EQ Band 3 at 4.2 kHz from +3 to about +1 dB and/or lower de-esser threshold slightly.

This gives you a turnkey starting chain that’s already within a professional ballpark.

### Human: I’m in a pretty noisy apartment near a road. Optimize the setup to minimize background noise as much as possible.

### Assistant: I’ll push the setup toward maximum isolation and noise rejection using positioning, settings, and processing—without changing your core gear.

**Physical setup optimization**

1. **Mic positioning for voice**
   - Distance: Move to **5 cm (2")** from the SM7B.
   - Angle: Place the mic **slightly off to the side of your mouth** (about 20–30°) so plosives hit less directly.

2. **Room positioning**
   - Face **away from windows and the road**, with the mic pointed **away** from the noisiest direction.
   - Put your **back toward the noisier side**; your body helps block some sound.
   - Place soft materials behind and around you:
     - Thick curtain or blanket over any window.
     - A clothes rack or blankets behind you if possible.

3. **Interface / gain strategy**
   - Keep input gain **as low as you can while still hitting –12 dBFS peaks**.
   - Closer mic distance allows lower gain, which reduces the relative contribution of noise.

**Processing tuned for noisy environment**

On the **vocal track**:

1. **High-pass filter**
   - Frequency: Increase to **90–100 Hz**.
   - Purpose: Cut more low-end rumble from traffic.

2. **Noise gate (add this before main processing)**
   - Threshold: Set so that the gate **opens only when you speak** at normal level; numerically, start near **–35 to –40 dBFS** and adjust.
   - Attack: **5–10 ms**
   - Hold: **80–120 ms**
   - Release: **100–150 ms**
   - Range: **–20 to –40 dB** (not full mute; partial reduction sounds more natural).
   - This reduces room and road noise during pauses between phrases.

3. **EQ tweak**
   - If road noise is low-frequency rumble:
     - Add a **low shelf** at **120 Hz, –2 to –3 dB**.
   - Keep the presence and air boosts subtle to avoid raising hiss:
     - Presence band: Drop from +3 dB to **+1.5–2 dB**.
     - Air band: Drop from +1.5 dB to **+0.5–1 dB**.

4. **De-esser**
   - Keep current settings but avoid over-brightening the vocal; less top-end boost means less hiss and less emphasis on background high-frequency noise.

5. **Limiter**
   - No change; it’s only catching peaks.

**Behavioral/usage adjustments**

- Record when noise is lowest (late night/early morning).
- Keep **windows closed** during takes.
- Avoid facing fans, AC units, or open doors; if unavoidable, turn fans **off during recording**.

These adjustments push the system toward close, intimate vocal capture that minimizes environmental noise without making the audio sound heavily processed or unnatural.
