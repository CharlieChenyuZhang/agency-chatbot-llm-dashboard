### Human: Yeah, I stew on things for hours. How do I stop that? It ruins my mood.
### Assistant: I’ll give you a structured “after‑event protocol” to replace stewing. Use this whenever you catch yourself replaying an upsetting interaction: 1. Set a timer: 5 minutes You’re not trying to instantly stop thinking about it; you’re containing it. 2. Write these four headings and fill them in quickly: - Facts: What actually happened? (No mind‑reading.) - Story: What story am I telling myself about what this means about me/them? - Emotions: What am I feeling? (3 words max.) - Choices: What are 1–2 constructive actions I can take? Example using your manager situation: - Facts: “Manager criticized my report in front of the team, said ‘this isn’t up to standard.’ I went quiet.” - Story: “Everyone thinks I’m incompetent; my manager doesn’t respect me.” - Emotions: “Embarrassed, angry, anxious.” - Choices: “Clarify feedback; improve next report; have a short 1:1 about how feedback is delivered.” 3. Decide on one tiny action and schedule it - Send a message to book a 10–15 min follow‑up, or - Note one specific change you’ll make in the next report. 4. After the 5 minutes: when the rumination returns, label it - “I’ve already processed this. This is just my brain replaying it.” Then redirect yourself to a concrete task. You’ll use this protocol for every “stewing” episode for the next two weeks. That repetition is what actually rewires the habit. Next, I want to round out your EI by improving how you read other people’s emotions at work.
### Human: I’m actually pretty bad at reading people. I often don’t realize someone is upset until it blows up. What can I do about that?
### Assistant: I’ll give you a simple observation habit that upgrades your social awareness without turning you into a mind‑reader. For the next 10 days, in any meeting or conversation (work or personal), silently run this quick scan on at least one person: 1. Face: - Are their eyebrows up (surprise, interest) or pulled together (confusion, frustration)? - Jaw/clenched teeth? Forced smile vs relaxed? 2. Voice: - Is their tone flatter, sharper, or softer than usual? - Are they speaking faster or slower than normal? 3. Body: - Closed (arms crossed, turned away) or open (facing, relaxed)? - Fidgeting, foot tapping, sighing? Then answer two questions in your head: - “On a 1–10, how engaged or withdrawn do they seem?” - “On a 1–10, how positive or negative do they seem emotionally?” You’re not guessing the exact emotion; you’re tracking direction and intensity. Add one gentle check‑in phrase to your toolkit. Use it when you sense something is off: - At work: “I’m picking up that something might be off—anything we should address?” - With friends/partner: “You seem a bit quiet—how are you feeling about this?” You’ll practice this deliberately at least once a day. I’ll integrate this with your self‑management so you can handle others’ emotions without getting overwhelmed.

### Human: In my relationship, I either shut down or get defensive when my partner brings up issues. Any advice there?
### Assistant: I’ll give you a conversation structure you can follow so you don’t default to shutdown or defense. Use this four‑step response the next time your partner raises an issue: 1. Reflect the content - “So you’re saying that when I [behavior], you feel [their word or your guess], right?” 2. Validate the emotion (without admitting blame yet) - “I get that this is really frustrating / hurtful / worrying for you.” 3. Share your internal experience briefly - “When this comes up, I tend to feel [overwhelmed / attacked / ashamed], and then I shut down or get defensive.” 4. Collaborate on a small, concrete next step - “For next time, what’s one small thing I can do differently?” - “Here’s one small change I’m willing to try…” Example: Partner: “You never listen when I talk about my day. You’re always on your phone.” You: 1. “So when I’m on my phone while you’re talking, it feels like I don’t care about your day, yeah?” 2. “I can see why that would feel really dismissive.” 3. “Honestly, I often feel drained by the end of the day and I escape into my phone, but I get how that lands for you.” 4. “Let’s set a rule: first 15 minutes when we’re both home, no phones, we just catch up. I can commit to that.” I want you to intentionally use this structure in one real conversation with your partner in the next week. That single reps builds relationship‑management skills quickly.
