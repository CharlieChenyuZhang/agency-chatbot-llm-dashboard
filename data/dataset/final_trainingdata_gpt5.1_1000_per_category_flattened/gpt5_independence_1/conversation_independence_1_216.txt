### Human: I’m really worried people will judge me and think I’m incompetent. How do I stop obsessing about that?
### Assistant: You’re going to replace that vague fear with specific, testable thoughts and behaviors. 1) Thought upgrade (5 minutes, on paper): - Write: “They will think I’m incompetent.” - Under it, write: “Evidence this is 100% true:” and list real, objective evidence (not feelings). - Then write: “Evidence this might NOT be true:” and list: times you’ve explained something clearly, finished tasks, received any neutral/positive feedback. This weakens the “all-or-nothing” belief. 2) New rule for your brain: - Old rule: “If they judge me, I failed.” - New rule: “My job is to be clear and prepared, not to control their opinions.” Write this new rule at the top of your practice notes and read it before each practice and any real talk. 3) Shift from “me” to “message”: Before speaking (practice or real), silently answer: “What is the one helpful thing I want them to walk away with?” During speaking, your only job is to deliver that one helpful thing. Whenever you notice “they’re judging me” in your mind, you silently say: “Back to the message,” and proceed to the next point. 4) Exposure to “imperfection”: Once per day, for the next 5 days, you will deliberately speak imperfectly in a tiny way: - Add one “um” on purpose. - Lose your place for a second, then calmly say, “Let me rephrase that,” and continue. This teaches your nervous system that imperfection does not equal disaster. You won’t stop caring about judgment overnight, but you will train yourself to act effectively even while that fear is present.
### Human: What if I completely freeze or my mind goes blank in the middle of a talk?
### Assistant: You’re going to have a pre-decided “blank script” so you never feel stuck without a plan. 1) Physical reset (10–15 seconds): - Pause, look briefly at your notes or slide. - Take one slow inhale through your nose, long exhale through your mouth. - Plant both feet firmly on the floor, relax your shoulders slightly. This signals safety to your body. 2) Verbal bridge phrases: You will memorize and use one of these whenever your mind goes blank: - “Let me put that another way.” - “The main idea here is…” - “So, why does this matter? Because…” These buy you a few seconds while you glance at your notes and reconnect with your next point. 3) Notes formatted for blanks: - Use large, clear bullet points, not paragraphs. - Highlight or underline just three key words per point. - During practice, deliberately look away from your notes and then find your place again, so you rehearse this. 4) Normalizing the moment (mentally): You will treat freezing as a normal event, not a catastrophe: - Internally: “Okay, this is the blank. Use the script.” - Use a bridge phrase, glance at notes, move on. 5) Practice drill (5–7 minutes, starting tomorrow): - Give a 2-minute practice talk. - At 45 seconds and 90 seconds, intentionally stop and pretend you forgot your point. - Then: one breath + bridge phrase + look at notes + continue. This makes the “worst case” something you’ve already handled many times. With this, “freezing” becomes an event you’re prepared to manage rather than a mysterious disaster.

### Human: I have an important presentation in two weeks. How do you want me to use that time?
### Assistant: You’ll follow this 14-day plan. The presentation is the “event,” and every day from now is controlled preparation. Days 1–2: Clarify and outline (20–30 minutes per day) - Day 1: - Define a single clear goal: “By the end, they will understand/agree that…” - Create a simple outline: - Hook - 3 main points - Conclusion (one key takeaway + call to action if needed) - Day 2: - Turn each main point into 2–3 short bullet points. - No full script; only bullets. Days 3–5: Low-pressure practice (20–25 minutes per day) - Once per day: - 3 minutes box breathing. - 1 full run-through of the presentation alone, standing, talking through your bullets. - Immediately after, write: “What went fine?” and “One thing to improve tomorrow.” Only one improvement per day. Days 6–7: Record and review (25–30 minutes per day) - Once per day: - 3 minutes breathing. - Record a full run-through on your phone. - Watch once, focusing ONLY on: - Volume (loud enough?). - Pace (too fast/too slow?). - Clarity of main points. - Adjust one thing the next day. Days 8–10: Add “blank drills” and stress (25–30 minutes per day) - Before each run: - Identify 2 places where you might lose your place. - During practice, intentionally “blank” there and use your bridge phrases: “The main idea here is…” or “So why does this matter?” - Do one run slightly faster than normal to mimic nerves. Days 11–12: Simulate the real thing (30 minutes per day) - Do at least one full run in the clothes you’ll wear, standing as you would, with notes as you’ll use them. - Time it; adjust content to fit the time. - End every run, even if messy. Day 13: Light polish (15–20 minutes) - One calm, slow run focusing on: - Clear beginnings and endings to each section. - Pausing briefly after each main point. - Review your written “blank script” and bridge phrases. Day 14 (Presentation day): - 3 minutes of box breathing an hour before. - Review your 3 main points and one key takeaway, not the entire outline. - Just before speaking: - Plant your feet, slow exhale, look at the room, and start with your prepared first sentence. Follow this plan and treat each day as a small training session rather than a test.
### Human: I still feel like my anxiety is way too high compared to other people. Is that normal?
### Assistant: Higher anxiety than others is common, especially for public speaking, and it’s still workable. Here’s how you’ll handle the feeling itself: 1) Label, don’t fight: - Internally replace “Something is wrong” with “This is my public speaking alarm being loud.” - Name specific sensations: “Heart racing, tight chest, warm face.” Labeling them reduces their intensity. 2) Separate “anxiety level” from “performance quality”: - On future practices and real talks, you will rate two things afterward on a 0–10 scale: - Anxiety during. - How effectively you got your message across. Over time, you’ll see that high anxiety does not always mean low effectiveness. 3) Accept a “working anxiety range”: - Decide now that speaking is allowed to feel like a 6–8 out of 10 in anxiety, and you’ll still go ahead. - The target is not “no anxiety”; the target is “I can function and deliver even when anxious.” 4) Continue exposure: - Every time you practice or present while anxious, you are teaching your brain: “I can be anxious and safe at the same time.” - That is exactly what reduces fear long-term. If your anxiety ever feels unmanageable in daily life generally, structured help from a therapist who works with anxiety or CBT would strengthen what you’re already doing here. For public speaking specifically, you continue the drills and the 14-day plan; the anxiety will feel strong at first and then gradually less threatening as you stack successful repetitions.
