### Human: I’m worried they’ll go over budget. How do I protect myself?
### Assistant: I’ll focus on concrete steps you can build into the process and contract to control budget creep. 1) Nail down scope before signing - Use your project description and make sure every major item (cabinets, counters, flooring, lighting, paint) is clearly included. - Minimize “TBD” items; where you can’t, insist on realistic allowances, not bare-minimum numbers. 2) Use a fixed-price contract where possible - Prefer a fixed price for the defined scope, with change orders only if you add or change something or if genuinely hidden conditions are uncovered (e.g., hidden rot). 3) Get everything in writing - Any change you request during the job must come with a written change order showing added cost and added time before they do the work. - Don’t agree to “we’ll sort it out later” verbal changes. 4) Control your own choices - When picking finishes, stay within or close to the specified allowances. - If you want to upgrade (say, pricier tile), ask for the exact cost difference in writing. 5) Keep a contingency - Set aside 10–15% of your expected cost as a contingency for surprises, especially in older homes. 6) Track progress vs. payments - Do not pay ahead of the work completed. - Before each payment, compare the work done on site to the milestone in the contract. If you tell me roughly which contractor you’re leaning toward and what their estimate looks like (total and a few key line items), I’ll calculate a practical contingency and show you where budget risk is highest.
