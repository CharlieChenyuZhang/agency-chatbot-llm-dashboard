### Human: Recommend a book to read
### Assistant: Start with "Project Hail Mary" by Andy Weir. It’s a fast, accessible science fiction novel about a lone astronaut who wakes up on a spaceship with no memory, then slowly discovers he’s on a last-ditch mission to save humanity. The pacing is brisk, the science is explained clearly, and the tone is surprisingly funny and hopeful, so it works well even if you don’t usually read sci‑fi.
### Human: I usually prefer non-fiction, though.
### Assistant: Shift to "Sapiens: A Brief History of Humankind" by Yuval Noah Harari. It’s narrative-driven non-fiction that reads almost like a story, covering how Homo sapiens went from insignificant apes to the dominant species on the planet. It mixes history, anthropology, psychology, and big-picture ideas about culture, money, religion, and technology, so it stays engaging rather than feeling like a textbook.

### Human: Could you give me a quick summary of Sapiens so I know what I’m getting into?
### Assistant: "Sapiens" traces human history from early hunter-gatherers to the modern era and beyond, framed around a few key revolutions: - **Cognitive Revolution**: Humans developed complex language and the ability to believe in shared “fictions” (like nations, money, religions), which let large groups cooperate. - **Agricultural Revolution**: Farming allowed population growth and cities but arguably made individual lives harder, with more disease, hard labor, and social hierarchy. - **Unification of Humankind**: Trade, empires, and universal religions gradually connected different human groups into a single global civilization. - **Scientific Revolution**: Scientific thinking, combined with capitalism and technology, radically sped up change, giving humans immense power but raising ethical and existential questions. The book is less about dates and more about big ideas: what makes us human, what we sacrifice for progress, and where we might be heading with biotechnology and AI.
