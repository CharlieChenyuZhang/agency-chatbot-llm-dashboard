### Assistant: You’ll build problem-solving skills fastest by doing three things in parallel: learning a simple method, practicing on varied problems, and reflecting on what you did. ### Human: That sounds good. What’s a simple example I can follow?
### Assistant: Use a classic everyday issue: “I never finish important tasks; I get distracted.” Applying the method: 1. **Define the problem** - Problem: “I start important tasks but get pulled into distractions and end the day with key work unfinished.” - Success: “I consistently finish 1–2 important tasks before getting lost in smaller things.” 2. **Break it down** - Subproblems: - No clear “most important task” each day. - Phone and notifications nearby. - Work sessions are long and tiring. - No clear stopping point, so it’s easy to drift. 3. **Generate options** - Option A: Decide 1 MIT (Most Important Task) each evening for the next day. - Option B: Work in 25-minute focused blocks with 5-minute breaks. - Option C: Put phone in another room during focus blocks. - Option D: Start the day with the MIT before checking email or messages. 4. **Choose using criteria** - Criteria: Simple, low effort, high impact. - Chosen starter combo: A (MIT), B (25-minute blocks), C (phone away). 5. **Test small** - For the next 3 workdays: - Each evening: write down one MIT for tomorrow. - Next morning: do one 25-minute block on that MIT with phone in another room. 6. **Review** - After 3 days, look at: - How many days you completed the MIT. - What blocked you (unexpected meetings, energy, tools, etc.). - Adjust: maybe shorten sessions, change time of day, or refine what counts as “MIT.” This is how you’ll approach problems: concrete definition → smaller pieces → options → small test → adjust. ### Human: Ok, give me that daily routine then.
### Assistant: Here’s a 15–20 minute daily routine you can start using immediately: **Daily (Mon–Fri):** 1. **2-minute setup (morning or evening)** - Write down: - One real problem you’re facing today. - One small desired outcome (what “better” looks like by tonight). 2. **5-minute structured thinking** - For that real problem, do: - 1 sentence: clear problem definition. - 3 bullet points: break it into subproblems. - 3 bullet points: possible actions. - 1 bullet point: which action you’ll try first and when. 3. **5–10-minute “puzzle rep”** - Alternate each day: - Day A: Logic/brain teaser. - Day B: Small scenario-based problem (e.g., “Two teammates disagree on approach; what do you do?”). - For each: - Write your reasoning in 3–5 bullet points, not just the answer. 4. **3-minute review (evening)** - Answer briefly: - What did I try? - What actually happened? - What would I do differently next time? This routine builds the habit of: - Defining problems clearly. - Generating options instead of freezing. - Learning from each attempt. ### Human: I usually freeze when a problem feels too big. How do I stop that?
### Assistant: Treat “freezing” itself as a problem and apply the method to it. **1. Define it** - Problem: “When a task feels big or unclear, I stare at it, feel overwhelmed, and do nothing.” - Success: “When a task feels big, I automatically reduce it to a tiny first step and start within 5 minutes.” **2. Break it down** Freezing usually has components like: - Task is vague (“study for exam”, “improve career”). - Outcome is unclear. - Fear of doing it badly. - No defined first action. - Task has no time limit, so it expands mentally. **3. Pre-decide your anti-freeze protocol** You’ll use a fixed mini-procedure whenever you notice freezing: - Step A: Rewrite the task as a **15-minute action**. - “Study for exam” → “Do 15 minutes of practice questions from chapter 1.” - Step B: Set a simple rule: “I only commit to 15 minutes, nothing more.” - Step C: Remove one friction point (e.g., open the document, get the book, clear desk). - Step D: Start a timer and begin before you can think too much. **4. Practice on purpose** - Take one “big scary task” you already have. - Apply the anti-freeze protocol right now in writing. - Run a single 15-minute session. - Afterward, write 2–3 sentences: - What changed in how you felt? - What made it easier/harder? The key is to turn “big problem → freeze” into “big problem → automatically shrink it to a 15-minute step.” You’re training that reflex by using the same small pattern repeatedly.
### Human: I’m also bad at choosing between options. I overthink and get stuck. Any method for that?
### Assistant: Use a simple “good-enough decision” framework so you don’t get trapped in analysis. **1. Separate reversible vs. hard-to-reverse decisions** - Reversible (most daily choices): you can change your mind with low cost. - Hard-to-reverse: big financial, career, or relationship choices. For **reversible decisions** (what to study first, which tool to try, which routine to use): **2. Use the 3-criteria rule** Pick 3 criteria max, for example: - Speed - Effort - Learning value Then: - List options - Give each option a quick 1–5 score for each criterion - Add them up and pick the highest total **3. Timebox your decision** - “I have 5 minutes to decide.” - When the timer ends, you choose the best-scoring option and move. **4. Focus on outcome, not perfection** Ask in your notes: - “What will I learn if this is wrong?” This reframes the decision as an experiment instead of a permanent judgment. For **hard-to-reverse decisions**, slow down: - Use more criteria (e.g., risk, long-term impact, alignment with values). - Seek data and advice. - Still set a deadline, but longer. Use this framework explicitly for a week on small choices (study order, task order, tools). The goal isn’t perfect decisions; it’s breaking the habit of indecision.

### Human: I don’t always stay consistent though. I start routines and stop after a few days.
### Assistant: You’ll design your routine to survive inconsistency instead of depending on willpower. **1. Shrink the minimum** - Set a **ridiculously small daily minimum**: - 5 minutes of problem-solving practice - or 10 minutes of programming work - Anything beyond that is a bonus, not the standard. **2. Use a “never zero” rule** - Even on bad days: - One tiny problem defined and one option written, - or one very small coding exercise. - This keeps the habit “alive” and makes restarting easier. **3. Track only one thing** - Each day, mark: - “Did I do at least the minimum?” Yes/No. - No judgment on duration or quality, just consistency. **4. Plan for failure explicitly** - Assume you’ll miss days. - When you miss: - No catch-up. - Next day, do only the minimum. You’re training the skill of **resuming quickly**, which is more important than never missing. With this approach, the bar is low enough that you can maintain it even on bad days, and your identity shifts to “I’m someone who solves at least one small problem every day.”
