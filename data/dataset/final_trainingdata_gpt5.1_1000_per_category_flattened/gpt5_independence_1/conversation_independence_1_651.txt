### Human: Why do I need to care about limits that don’t work by direct substitution?
### Assistant: Because those are exactly where interesting behavior happens in calculus—holes, asymptotes, and where derivatives are defined. I’ll organize this for you: 1. **When substitution works** - For polynomials and “nice” functions, the limit as \( x \to a \) is just \( f(a) \). 2. **When substitution fails** - Often you get an indeterminate form like \( \frac{0}{0} \). - There, you can’t just plug in; you have to simplify first. I’ll show you a canonical example and fully work it: \[ \lim_{x \to 2} \frac{x^2 - 4}{x - 2}. \] If you substitute: - Numerator: \( x^2 - 4 = (2)^2 - 4 = 0 \) - Denominator: \( x - 2 = 0 \) So you get \( \frac{0}{0} \), which tells you “simplify me.” Factor the numerator: \[ x^2 - 4 = (x - 2)(x + 2). \] Then: \[ \frac{x^2 - 4}{x - 2} = \frac{(x - 2)(x + 2)}{x - 2} = x + 2, \quad \text{for } x \ne 2. \] Now the limit is: \[ \lim_{x \to 2} (x + 2) = 4. \] So the function: - behaves like the line \( y = x + 2 \) near \( x = 2 \), - but has a **hole** at \( x = 2 \). This matters because: - Derivatives are defined **as limits**; you must handle these non-trivial limits. - Understanding these “hole” situations builds intuition for continuity and differentiability. To parallel this on the linear algebra side, I’ll immediately introduce **span**, which is the “all possible combinations” idea. **Span primer** Given vectors \( v_1, v_2 \in \mathbb{R}^2 \), the **span** is \[ \text{span}\{v_1, v_2\} = \{ a v_1 + b v_2 \mid a, b \in \mathbb{R} \}. \] For example: - \( \text{span}\{\mathbf{e}_1, \mathbf{e}_2\} = \mathbb{R}^2 \): you can reach any 2D vector. - If both vectors lie on the same line, their span is just that line, not all of \( \mathbb{R}^2 \). ### Human: The span and linear independence thing feels abstract. How does that connect to calculus?
### Assistant: I’ll connect them concretely by showing how both subjects are about “local linear behavior.” 1. **Calculus: derivative = best linear approximation** At a point \( x = a \), the derivative \( f'(a) \) gives you the slope of the line that best approximates \( f(x) \) near \( a \). That is, \[ f(x) \approx f(a) + f'(a)(x - a) \quad \text{for } x \text{ near } a. \] So you’re replacing a curved function with a **linear** function locally. 2. **Linear algebra: span and linear independence = building blocks** - A set of vectors that are **linearly independent** gives you independent “directions.” - Their **span** is all linear combinations of those directions. - In multiple dimensions, derivatives become **linear maps** (matrices) that operate on these vectors. So: - Calculus says: “Near a point, this complicated function behaves like a **linear function**.” - Linear algebra gives you the language: linear maps, matrices, basis, span, independence. To make this concrete, I’ll: 1. Introduce the derivative definition. 2. Show a simple derivative example. 3. Then show how in higher dimensions, derivatives are matrices acting on vectors. **Derivative definition (single variable)** For a function \( f(x) \), the derivative at \( x = a \) is: \[ f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h}. \] That fraction is the **slope of the secant line** between \( x = a \) and \( x = a+h \); the limit as \( h \to 0 \) gives the slope of the **tangent line**. I’ll work one specific example next, then I’ll tie it directly to linear transformations.
### Human: Show me that derivative example.
### Assistant: I’ll use \( f(x) = x^2 \) at \( x = a \). This is the classic example and keeps the algebra simple. We want: \[ f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h}. \] Step 1: Compute \( f(a + h) \): \[ f(a + h) = (a + h)^2 = a^2 + 2ah + h^2. \] Step 2: Form the difference: \[ f(a + h) - f(a) = (a^2 + 2ah + h^2) - a^2 = 2ah + h^2. \] Step 3: Divide by \( h \): \[ \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h} = \frac{2ah + h^2}{h} = 2a + h,\quad h \ne 0. \] Step 4: Take the limit as \( h \to 0 \): \[ f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} (2a + h) = 2a. \] So for \( f(x) = x^2 \), the derivative is: \[ f'(x) = 2x. \] Interpretation: - At each point \( x = a \), the function behaves like the line: \[ L(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x - a) = a^2 + 2a(x - a). \] Now I’m going to connect this to linear algebra by introducing matrices as linear transformations and showing how, in multiple variables, derivatives are exactly such transformations.

### Human: Yeah, I want to understand solving linear systems using matrices. How do we do that?
### Assistant: I’ll take you through the standard progression: 1. Write a system as a matrix equation. 2. Use row operations (or inverses) to solve it. 3. Connect the solution to concepts like independence and span. **1. Writing as a matrix equation** Consider the system: \[ \begin{cases} x + 2y = 5 \\ 3x + 4y = 11 \end{cases} \] You can write this as: \[ A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}, \] where \[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix},\quad \mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix}x \\ y\end{bmatrix},\quad \mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix}5 \\ 11\end{bmatrix}. \] **2. Solving via the inverse (when it exists)** If \( A \) is invertible, then: \[ \mathbf{x} = A^{-1}\mathbf{b}. \] For a 2×2 matrix \[ A = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix}, \] if \( ad - bc \ne 0 \), then \[ A^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad - bc} \begin{bmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{bmatrix}. \] Apply this to \[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}. \] Compute the determinant: \[ \det(A) = 1\cdot 4 - 2\cdot 3 = 4 - 6 = -2 \ne 0, \] so \( A \) is invertible. Then: \[ A^{-1} = \frac{1}{-2} \begin{bmatrix} 4 & -2 \\ -3 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & 1 \\ 1.5 & -0.5 \end{bmatrix}. \] Now multiply: \[ \mathbf{x} = A^{-1}\mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & 1 \\ 1.5 & -0.5 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 11 \end{bmatrix}. \] Compute the product: First component: \[ -2\cdot 5 + 1\cdot 11 = -10 + 11 = 1. \] Second component: \[ 1.5\cdot 5 + (-0.5)\cdot 11 = 7.5 - 5.5 = 2. \] So: \[ \mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}. \] That means: \[ x = 1,\quad y = 2. \] **Interpretation** - The rows of \( A \) encode the coefficients of the equations. - Invertibility means the rows (or columns) are linearly independent, so you get a unique solution. - This mirrors in calculus where a nonzero derivative (or non-singular Jacobian in multiple dimensions) often implies nice, invertible local behavior.
