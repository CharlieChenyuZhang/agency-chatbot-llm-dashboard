### Human: Can you give me an example of how to reframe a typical marketing task strategically?
### Assistant: Use this example and copy the pattern. Typical task: “Create a new email campaign to boost engagement.” Strategic reframe: 1. Clarify the real problem - Possible real issue: “We’re not moving trial users to active, paying customers fast enough.” - Strategic problem statement: “Activation from trial to first value is too low/slow.” 2. Clarify the success metric - Instead of “email open rate,” use: - “% of new signups who reach [key action] within 7 days.” - “Conversion rate from trial to paid in 14 or 30 days.” 3. Generate alternative strategies Instead of jumping straight to “new emails,” list at least three different approaches: - Improve onboarding inside the product (tooltips, in-app messages). - Create a single, highly targeted education sequence driven by user behavior. - Add a time-bound, value-focused offer at a specific activation milestone. 4. Evaluate with a simple 2x2 On one axis: Impact (low to high). On the other: Effort (low to high). - Place each idea in the grid. - Prioritize “high impact / low–medium effort.” 5. Turn back into a concrete plan Example: - Strategy: “Increase activation by focusing on one key ‘aha’ action and driving users there with behavior-based messaging.” - Tactics: specific emails or in-app prompts, but now built around that strategic choice. You’ll apply this pattern to your own tasks: restate the problem, pick the right metric, generate alternatives, choose using a simple model, then convert back into execution.
### Human: What are the most common mistakes people make when trying to be more strategic?
### Assistant: You’ll avoid these five traps deliberately: 1. Confusing detail with strategy - Writing long docs, complex decks, or big plans that are just tactical checklists. - Fix: always be able to state your strategy in one or two clear sentences. 2. Skipping trade-offs - Trying to do everything: all channels, all segments, all ideas. - Fix: explicitly state what you’re not doing or who you’re not targeting. 3. Starting from tools instead of problems - “We should do TikTok,” “We need a funnel,” “We should A/B test” without a specific problem to solve. - Fix: start every initiative by writing a problem statement and success metric. 4. Ignoring second-order effects - Optimizing one metric while harming another (e.g., aggressive discounts boost sales but destroy brand perception and margins). - Fix: for every move, list 1–2 possible unintended consequences. 5. Not closing the loop - Making decisions but not revisiting them, so there’s no learning. - Fix: maintain a simple “decision journal” for important calls: what you decided, why, and what happened. You’ll deliberately counter each of these in your weekly review by asking: - “Did I make trade-offs?” - “Did I start from the real problem?” - “Did I consider second-order effects?” - “Did I learn from at least one decision this week?”
