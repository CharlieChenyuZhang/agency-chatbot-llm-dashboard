### Human: My knees sometimes hurt when I squat. What should I do while still trying to push my squat up?
### Assistant: You’ll keep training but adjust technique, volume, and exercise selection to protect your knees and reduce irritation. You should also get evaluated by a qualified professional if pain is significant or worsening. Here’s how to modify the plan conservatively: 1. **Immediate training changes** - Reduce squat load to a pain‑free or low‑pain range: - Aim for no more than mild discomfort (≤3/10) that doesn’t worsen set‑to‑set. - Cut squat volume for 1–2 weeks: - Reduce total squat sets by ~30–40% (e.g., 4×4 → 3×3; 3×6 → 2×5). - Replace some squat volume with: - Leg press with a comfortable ROM - Bulgarian split squats or step‑ups, pain‑free range - Romanian deadlifts and hip thrusts (hip‑dominant) 2. **Technique adjustments** - Longer warm‑up: - 5–10 min light bike or walking - Bodyweight squats, lunges, hip circles - Try: - Slightly wider stance and more toe‑out to see if knees feel better. - Sitting back a bit more and controlling the descent. - Ensure knees track in line with toes, not collapsing inward. 3. **Movement substitutions on bad days** - If back squats are too painful on a given day: - Try safety bar squats or front squats with lighter loads - Or do leg press + split squats for that session instead of forcing squats. 4. **Pain/red‑flag situations** - Stop and seek medical advice promptly if: - Pain is sharp, sudden, or locks/catches. - Significant swelling, instability, or giving way. - Pain persists/worsens for multiple weeks despite reducing load and volume. You’ll keep progressing the squat on days it feels okay, but prioritize pain‑free range, perfect form, and gradual re‑build of volume over the next 2–4 weeks instead of forcing heavy jumps.
