### Human: Now explain the definition of limit of a sequence rigorously, but keep it under 150 words, include one simple example, and do not prove anything.
### Assistant: A sequence (a_n) of real numbers has limit L (written a_n → L) if, for every ε > 0, there exists an N ∈ ℕ such that for all n ≥ N, |a_n − L| < ε. Intuitively, beyond some index N, all terms of the sequence lie within any prescribed distance ε from L. Example: Consider a_n = 1/n. Claim: a_n → 0. For any ε > 0, we can choose N > 1/ε. Then for n ≥ N, we have 1/n ≤ 1/N < ε, so |a_n − 0| = |1/n| < ε. Thus, the sequence (1/n) converges to 0.

### Human: State the Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem only for bounded sequences in ℝ, and prove it, but don’t mention compactness or use the word “compact” anywhere.
### Assistant: Theorem: Every bounded sequence of real numbers has a convergent subsequence. Proof: Let (a_n) be bounded, so all terms lie in some interval [m, M]. Divide [m, M] into two halves; at least one half contains infinitely many terms. Choose that half and call it I_1. Inductively bisect I_k to get I_{k+1} containing infinitely many terms. Pick n_k strictly increasing with a_{n_k} ∈ I_k. The intervals shrink and their lengths go to zero, so (a_{n_k}) converges to the unique point common to all intervals.

### Human: Introduce Lebesgue measure in at most 80 words, still one short paragraph, no bullets.
### Assistant: Lebesgue measure extends the usual notion of length on ℝ to a much larger class of sets while preserving translation invariance and countable additivity. Intervals have measure equal to their length, disjoint unions have measure equal to the sum of measures, and “small” scattered sets like countable sets have measure zero. Some pathological sets cannot be assigned a Lebesgue measure, but they require the axiom of choice to construct.
### Human: Define Lebesgue integrability of a nonnegative measurable function on ℝ in basic terms only, under 80 words, no bullets.
### Assistant: For a nonnegative measurable function f on ℝ, the Lebesgue integral is defined as the supremum of the integrals of all simple functions g that are measurable, nonnegative, satisfy 0 ≤ g ≤ f, and take only finitely many values. The function f is Lebesgue integrable (in this nonnegative case) if this supremum is finite.
### Human: Now switch topics: give me a brief, under-80-word paragraph explaining what a metric space is, but don’t give any examples.
### Assistant: A metric space is a set equipped with a distance function, called a metric, that assigns to each pair of points a nonnegative real number, interpreted as their distance, and satisfies four conditions: distance is zero exactly on identical points, symmetric in its arguments, and obeys the triangle inequality. This framework generalizes many notions of convergence, continuity, and open or closed sets beyond the real line.

### Human: Fair enough. Then briefly explain why measure theory is useful for probability, under 80 words.
### Assistant: Measure theory provides a rigorous foundation for probability by modeling outcomes as a measurable space, events as measurable sets, and probabilities as a measure with total mass one. Random variables become measurable functions, and expectations are Lebesgue integrals. This framework cleanly handles infinite sample spaces, continuous distributions, and convergence theorems like the law of large numbers and central limit theorem.
