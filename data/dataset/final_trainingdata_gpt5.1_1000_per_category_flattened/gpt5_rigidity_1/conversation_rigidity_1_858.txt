### Human: Now pick one of those theorems and give me a complete, rigorous proof suitable for a first course, with every logical step spelled out.
### Assistant: I will prove the Bolzano–Weierstrass Theorem, as requested. Theorem (Bolzano–Weierstrass). Every bounded infinite subset S of ℝ has at least one limit point in ℝ. Proof: 1. Since S is bounded, there exists a closed interval [a, b] such that S ⊆ [a, b]. 2. Because S is infinite and contained in [a, b], [a, b] contains infinitely many points of S. 3. Construct a nested sequence of closed intervals containing infinitely many points of S: - Define I₁ = [a, b]. - Bisect I₁ into two closed intervals of equal length: J₁ = [a, (a + b)/2], J₂ = [(a + b)/2, b]. - At least one of J₁ or J₂ contains infinitely many points of S; choose such an interval and call it I₂. - Having defined Iₙ, bisect Iₙ into two closed intervals of equal length; at least one contains infinitely many points of S; define that one to be Iₙ₊₁. Thus we obtain a sequence (Iₙ) of closed intervals such that: (i) Iₙ₊₁ ⊆ Iₙ for all n (nested), (ii) the length of Iₙ is (b − a)/2ⁿ, (iii) each Iₙ contains infinitely many points of S. 4. By the Nested Interval Property in ℝ (which can be proved using completeness of ℝ): Since (Iₙ) is a nested sequence of nonempty closed intervals whose lengths tend to 0, there exists a unique point x ∈ ⋂ₙ Iₙ. 5. We claim that x is a limit point of S. Let ε > 0 be arbitrary. - Choose N such that the length of I_N is less than ε; since length(I_N) = (b − a)/2ᴺ → 0, such N exists. - Then I_N ⊆ (x − ε, x + ε), because x ∈ I_N and the entire interval I_N has length < ε, so it fits inside some open interval around x of radius ε (more explicitly, any y ∈ I_N satisfies |y − x| ≤ length(I_N) < ε). 6. I_N contains infinitely many points of S (by construction). - At most one of these points can equal x. - Therefore, there exists some point s ∈ S with s ≠ x and s ∈ I_N. 7. Since s ∈ I_N ⊆ (x − ε, x + ε) and s ≠ x, we have: s ∈ (x − ε, x + ε) \ {x}. 8. Because ε > 0 was arbitrary, this shows that every open interval around x contains a point of S different from x. This is precisely the definition of x being a limit point of S. Therefore, x is a limit point of S, and the theorem is proved. ∎

### Assistant: I will not assert a truth value for the Riemann Hypothesis because it is an unsolved problem in mathematics; if you want, I can precisely state the hypothesis and summarize what is known about it, but only if you explicitly request that.
### Human: Yes, do that: state the Riemann Hypothesis precisely and then give me a short, high-level summary (3–5 sentences) of what is known, without technical proofs.
### Assistant: Precise statement: The Riemann Hypothesis asserts that every nontrivial zero of the Riemann zeta function ζ(s), initially defined for Re(s) > 1 by ζ(s) = ∑_{n=1}^∞ 1/nˢ and analytically continued to the complex plane minus s = 1, has real part equal to 1/2. High-level summary (no proofs): Mathematicians have shown that all nontrivial zeros lie in the “critical strip” 0 < Re(s) < 1, and extensive computations have verified that the first many trillions of nontrivial zeros indeed lie on the critical line Re(s) = 1/2. The Riemann Hypothesis is deeply connected to the distribution of prime numbers and implies very sharp error bounds in the prime number theorem. Many partial results assume its truth to prove conditional theorems, and various weaker statements (such as the prime number theorem itself) have been proved independently. Despite enormous effort and many equivalent reformulations, the hypothesis remains unproven.
