### Assistant: - Write a short “future memo” from 12 months ahead describing what went right and wrong in your career, then work backward to identify today’s key strategic bets. - For a current project, draw a simple decision tree with at least two alternative paths and the major risks, costs, and benefits attached to each branch. - Do a “pre-mortem” on your main quarterly goal: assume it failed badly and list 5–10 plausible reasons why, then decide what you’ll do now to prevent each one.

### Assistant: - Define 1–3 primary objectives for the quarter (e.g., new trials, qualified leads, expansion revenue) and rank them. - List all current and potential channels, then estimate their existing performance (if any) and the main hypothesis for each (“We believe X will drive Y because Z”). - Choose a small portfolio of channels (e.g., 3–5) that collectively address your top objectives and diversify risk. - For each selected channel, design a minimum viable experiment (clear offer, audience, time frame, and success metric). - Allocate limited test budget and time windows to each experiment, keeping them comparable where possible. - Run the experiments, then review results in a single meeting focused only on: cost, impact on objectives, and learning. - Double down on the 1–2 channels with the best evidence and explicitly kill or pause underperformers, documenting why.
### Human: Now switch back to short paragraph answers (no bullets). Explain how I can recognize when I’m being too tactical and not strategic enough.
### Assistant: You’re likely being too tactical when you find yourself reacting to tasks and requests without regularly connecting them to long-term outcomes, when you measure success mainly by activity (emails sent, meetings held) rather than by impact on key goals, and when you rarely pause to consider alternative paths or second-order effects. Notice patterns like constantly dealing with “urgent” items, solving the same kinds of problems repeatedly, or changing direction every time a new idea appears—these often signal a lack of stable strategic priorities. To counter this, regularly ask yourself, “What bigger goal does this serve?”, “What happens if I keep doing this for a year?”, and “What am I choosing not to do by saying yes to this?” and adjust your actions based on the answers.
