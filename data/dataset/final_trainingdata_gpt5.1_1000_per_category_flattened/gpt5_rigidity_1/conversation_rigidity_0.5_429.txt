Here’s a Wikipedia-style summary of CRISPR gene editing: CRISPR (Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats) refers to segments of prokaryotic DNA containing short, repetitive base sequences. These sequences, together with CRISPR-associated (Cas) proteins, form an adaptive immune system in bacteria and archaea that protects against invading genetic elements like viruses (bacteriophages). In gene editing, the most commonly used system is CRISPR-Cas9. It works in three main parts: 1. A guide RNA (gRNA) designed to match a specific DNA sequence. 2. The Cas9 nuclease, an enzyme that cuts DNA. 3. The target DNA sequence in the genome. Mechanism (in editing applications): 1. The guide RNA binds to Cas9 and directs it to a complementary DNA sequence that lies next to a short motif called the PAM (Protospacer Adjacent Motif), typically “NGG” for the commonly used Cas9 from Streptococcus pyogenes. 2. Cas9 creates a double-strand break at the target site. 3. The cell repairs this break by: - Non-homologous end joining (NHEJ), which is error-prone and often introduces insertions or deletions (indels), potentially knocking out a gene. - Homology-directed repair (HDR), which can use a supplied DNA template to introduce a precise change (e.g., correcting a mutation). Origins and discovery: CRISPR sequences were first noticed in bacteria in the late 1980s and early 1990s, but their function wasn’t clear. In the 2000s, researchers recognized them as part of an adaptive immune system using RNA to target viral DNA. Around 2012, work by researchers including Emmanuelle Charpentier and Jennifer Doudna showed that CRISPR-Cas9 could be programmed for targeted genome editing in vitro, leading to widespread adoption in biology. Charpentier and Doudna received the 2020 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for this work.
