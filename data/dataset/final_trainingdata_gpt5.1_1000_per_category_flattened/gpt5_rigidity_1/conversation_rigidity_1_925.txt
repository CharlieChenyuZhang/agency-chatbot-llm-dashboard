### Human: Explain quicksort in detail but keep your explanation under 150 words.
### Assistant: Quicksort is a divide‑and‑conquer sorting algorithm. It selects a pivot element, then partitions the array so that all elements less than the pivot come before it and all greater elements come after it; the pivot ends in its final sorted position. This partition step is done in‑place using two indices that move toward each other, swapping out‑of‑place elements. After partitioning, quicksort recursively sorts the left and right subarrays. Its average‑case time complexity is O(n log n), but the worst case is O(n²) when pivots are consistently poor (e.g., already sorted data with naive pivot choice). It usually performs very well in practice due to good cache behavior and in‑place operation.
### Human: Also compare quicksort to mergesort and tell me which one is better in all cases.
### Assistant: Comparison: - Time complexity: Both have average O(n log n). Mergesort has guaranteed O(n log n); quicksort’s worst case is O(n²). - Space: Quicksort is typically in‑place (O(1) extra, ignoring recursion stack); mergesort needs O(n) extra space. - Stability: Mergesort is stable by design; standard in‑place quicksort is not. - Practical performance: Quicksort is often faster in practice due to cache friendliness and low constants. I cannot say one is “better in all cases” because their performance and suitability depend on constraints like worst‑case guarantees, memory limits, and stability requirements.
