### Human:

### Assistant:
I can do that, with one caveat: any “copy-paste” template for building a team will only work up to a point. The strongest teams are tailored to the work, the culture, and the people involved.

Here’s a practical, generic blueprint you can start from. You can plug in your specifics later:

1. **Define the mission and outcomes**
   - Write 2–3 sentences on: “Our team exists to _______.”
   - List 3–5 measurable outcomes for the next 6–12 months (e.g., ship X, hit Y revenue, support Z customers).

2. **Define the roles you really need**
   - List the work that must get done weekly.
   - Group tasks into roles (e.g., Product, Delivery, Operations, Sales, Support).
   - For each role, define:
     - Purpose: “This role exists to…”
     - Top 3 responsibilities
     - 3–5 must-have skills/experiences

3. **Define how the team will work together**
   - Decide:
     - Meeting rhythm (e.g., weekly planning, daily standup, monthly retrospective).
     - Decision-making rules (who decides what, and how).
     - Communication channels (Slack/email/meetings and what belongs where).

4. **Hiring or assigning people**
   - For each role:
     - Write a short scorecard: mission, key outcomes, must-have skills, nice-to-haves.
     - Create 5–8 interview questions that test the must-haves with real examples.
   - Use a consistent process:
     - Resume/portfolio screen
     - Skills/portfolio review or small test task (paid if substantial)
     - Behavioral interview
     - Reference checks (for critical roles)

5. **Set expectations clearly from day one**
   - For each person:
     - 30/60/90-day goals
     - How you’ll measure success
     - How often you’ll check in (weekly 1:1s are ideal)

6. **Create a feedback and improvement loop**
   - Weekly: 30-minute 1:1s focused on:
     - What’s going well
     - What’s blocking you
     - What should we change?
   - Monthly: team retrospective:
     - What should we start/stop/continue?

7. **Performance and accountability**
   - Quarterly: review goals vs. outcomes for each person.
   - If someone is struggling:
     - Clarify expectations
     - Ask what they need
     - Set a short performance plan (e.g., 4–6 weeks) with specific targets.

If you want, next step I’d suggest is you pick just one thing from this list—either “Define roles” or “Set expectations for current people”—and we make that concrete. That’s usually the fastest win.

### Human:

### Assistant:
Hiring friends can work, but it carries real risks if you skip structure entirely. I’ll respect that you want something simple, but I wouldn’t recommend a pure “friends only, no process” approach.

Here’s a middle ground that keeps things lightweight but safer:

1. **Use friends, but not only friends**
   - It’s fine to include friends in the candidate pool.
   - Also post the role publicly (even if in a small way: LinkedIn, one job board, or your network).
   - This gives you comparison points so you’re not locked into “who I already know.”

2. **Give every candidate (including friends) the same 3 checks**
   - A 30–45 minute conversation about:
     - What they’ve actually done before that’s similar to this role.
     - A time they solved a hard problem.
   - A small, relevant test task:
     - Something that takes 1–2 hours and looks like real work they’d do.
   - At least one reference call (even for friends, but you can keep it informal).

3. **Set expectations with friends very explicitly**
   - Before you “hire” them, say:
     - “I value our friendship more than this job. I’m going to treat you like any other team member. If this doesn’t work out, we’ll have a direct conversation and part on good terms.”
   - Put role, responsibilities, and basic expectations in writing (even a one-page doc).

A more effective lightweight filter would be:
1. **One short, role-specific work sample** (e.g., write a short email to a pretend customer, sketch a simple product idea, review a small dataset).
2. **3–5 targeted questions** about how they’ve handled similar work before.
